MATH DETECTIVE: CASE FILE #23

Subject: Angle Bisectors of Two Intersecting Lines

Mission: Find why equal perpendicular distances mark the two angle bisectors, and turn that geometric fact into their equations.

Detective Briefing: Two crossing lines make four angles. Instead of measuring those angles first, we will follow a point and compare its shortest distances from the two lines.

Act 1: Check the Lines Before Using Them

The setting
\[ L_1:A_1x+B_1y+C_1=0, \qquad L_2:A_2x+B_2y+C_2=0. \]

The lines are distinct and nonparallel. They meet at one point, which we call O.

What must be true before either equation can describe a line?

Detective hint: If both coefficients of x and y were zero, what set would the equation describe?
\[ A_i^2+B_i^2>0 \iff A_i\ne0\text{ or }B_i\ne0, \qquad i=1,2. \]

If \(A_i=B_i=0\), the equation becomes \(C_i=0\). It then describes either no points or the whole plane—not a line. The condition also makes \(\sqrt{A_i^2+B_i^2}\) positive, so division by it is allowed.

Why do we also need the lines to be nonparallel?

Detective hint: Picture two lines with different directions. How many times can they cross?

In coordinates, nonparallel means

\[ A_1B_2-A_2B_1\ne0. \]

This says the two normal vectors \((A_1,B_1)\) and \((A_2,B_2)\) are not multiples of each other. We will return to the parallel case at the end.

Act 2: From an Angle Bisector to Equal Distances

Choose a point \(P\ne O\) on one angle bisector. Drop perpendiculars from P to the two lines:

\[ PH\perp L_1, \qquad PK\perp L_2. \]

Why do PH and PK give the distances from P to the lines?

The distance from a point to a line is the length of its perpendicular segment. Therefore \(PH=d(P,L_1)\) and \(PK=d(P,L_2)\).

Why must the two perpendicular lengths be equal?

Detective hint: Find the two right angles, the shared side, and the equal angles made by the bisector.

The triangles have two equal angles and the same hypotenuse OP, so they are congruent. Their matching perpendicular sides are equal:

\[ PH=PK. \]
\[ P\text{ on an angle bisector} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad d(P,L_1)=d(P,L_2). \]

Act 3: Does Equal Distance Force a Bisector?

Extra clue 1 · We proved that every bisector point has equal distances. What must we check before saying the equal-distance set is exactly the bisectors?

Hint: The first direction puts bisector points inside the equal-distance set. Could that set contain extra points?

Open the reasoning

We still need the converse: equal perpendicular distances must force a bisector. This rules out extra points and turns a one-way statement into an “if and only if” statement.

Now begin with the distance fact. Let \(P\ne O\) and suppose

\[ PH=PK. \]

Again, PH and PK are perpendicular to the two lines.

Can we now prove that OP cuts one of the angles into two equal parts?

Detective hint: Use the right-triangle hypotenuse-and-leg test.

The right triangles are congruent by the hypotenuse-and-leg test. Their matching angles at O are equal. Therefore the ray OP bisects the sector that contains P.

If \(P=O\), both distances are zero, and O lies on both complete bisector lines.

\[ \boxed{ P\text{ lies on an angle bisector} \iff d(P,L_1)=d(P,L_2).} \]

This geometric fact is the heart of the coordinate formula.

Act 4: Turn Equal Distance into Equations

Let \(P=(x,y)\). The point-to-line distance formula gives

\[ d(P,L_1)=\frac{|A_1x+B_1y+C_1|}{\sqrt{A_1^2+B_1^2}}, \qquad d(P,L_2)=\frac{|A_2x+B_2y+C_2|}{\sqrt{A_2^2+B_2^2}}. \]

Why can we not simply compare the two numerators?

Detective hint: Multiply one line equation by 10. The line stays the same, but what happens to its raw numerator?

For shorter writing, set

\[ E_1=\frac{A_1x+B_1y+C_1}{\sqrt{A_1^2+B_1^2}}, \qquad E_2=\frac{A_2x+B_2y+C_2}{\sqrt{A_2^2+B_2^2}}. \]

The values \(E_1,E_2\) are signed distances. Equal ordinary distances mean \(|E_1|=|E_2|\).

What two sign choices are hidden inside \(|E_1|=|E_2|\)?

Detective hint: Which real numbers have the same distance from zero?

We can also see the two choices by squaring and factoring:

\[ \begin{aligned} |E_1|=|E_2| &\iff E_1^2=E_2^2\\ &\iff (E_1-E_2)(E_1+E_2)=0\\ &\iff E_1=E_2\text{ or }E_1=-E_2. \end{aligned} \]
\[ \boxed{ \frac{A_1x+B_1y+C_1}{\sqrt{A_1^2+B_1^2}} =\pm \frac{A_2x+B_2y+C_2}{\sqrt{A_2^2+B_2^2}}.} \]

The plus and minus choices give the two complete bisector lines.

Act 5: Why Two Bisector Lines—and Why Perpendicular?

The original lines form four sectors. Why does the answer contain two lines, not four?

Detective hint: Two rays pointing in opposite directions from the same point make one straight line.
Extra clue 2 · Before using vectors, what immediate observation can prove that the two bisectors are perpendicular?

Hint: Choose two sectors next to each other. Together they make a straight angle.

Open the reasoning

If the adjacent angles are \(\alpha\) and \(180^\circ-\alpha\), the angle between their bisector rays is \(\alpha/2+(180^\circ-\alpha)/2=90^\circ\). The complete bisector lines therefore meet at a right angle. The vector calculation below confirms the same fact.

Now define the unit normal vectors

\[ \mathbf n_1=\frac{(A_1,B_1)}{\sqrt{A_1^2+B_1^2}}, \qquad \mathbf n_2=\frac{(A_2,B_2)}{\sqrt{A_2^2+B_2^2}}. \]

Why are these vectors both normal and unit?

They are normal because each is perpendicular to its line. They are unit vectors because each has length 1. The two bisector equations have normal vectors \(\mathbf n_1-\mathbf n_2\) and \(\mathbf n_1+\mathbf n_2\).

What makes the two bisectors perpendicular?

Detective hint: Expand the dot product. The two middle terms cancel, and both unit normals have length 1.
\[ (\mathbf n_1-\mathbf n_2)\cdot(\mathbf n_1+\mathbf n_2) =|\mathbf n_1|^2-|\mathbf n_2|^2=0. \]

Recall that \(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v=|\mathbf u|\,|\mathbf v|\cos\theta\). The two normals here are nonzero because the original lines are nonparallel. Their dot product is zero, so the angle between them is \(90^\circ\). Rotating both normals by \(90^\circ\) gives the directions of their lines, and keeps that right angle.

\[ \boxed{\text{The two angle-bisector lines are perpendicular.}} \]

Act 6: Does the Plus Sign Always Mean “Internal”?

Extra clue 3 · Someone says the plus equation always gives the internal bisector. What quick test can we use?

Hint: Changing an equation's sign keeps its line but reverses its signed-distance expression.

Open the reasoning

Replacing \(E_2\) by \(-E_2\) keeps the two original lines and both geometric bisectors. It exchanges the equations called “plus” and “minus.” So the sign alone cannot name the internal bisector of a chosen angle.

No. First, two complete lines make several sectors. We must name the angle before “internal” has a clear meaning.

There is also an equation reason. Replace the equation of \(L_2\) by its negative. This does not change any point on the line, but it changes \(E_2\) to \(-E_2\):

\[ E_1=E_2 \quad\longleftrightarrow\quad E_1=-(-E_2). \]

So the same geometric bisector moves from the plus case to the minus case. Use the two equation-form buttons in the visual lab to see the green and orange labels exchange while the two geometric bisectors stay where they are.

To choose the bisector of one named angle: pick a point or a direction inside that angle, then check which of the two equations it satisfies. Do not decide from the sign alone.

Act 7: What Changes for Parallel Lines?

Take the distinct parallel lines

\[ x=0, \qquad x=4. \]

A point \((x,y)\) is equally far from them when

\[ |x|=|x-4|. \]

Squaring both sides gives \(x^2=(x-4)^2\), so \(x=2\). The equal-distance set is one middle parallel line.

Edge case · equal distance between parallelsThe green line sits halfway between the two given lines.

If the two original lines are the same line, every point has equal distance from them. This is why the words distinct and nonparallel are needed for the usual two-bisector result.

Final Report: The Full Chain of Ideas

\[ \boxed{ \frac{A_1x+B_1y+C_1}{\sqrt{A_1^2+B_1^2}} =\pm \frac{A_2x+B_2y+C_2}{\sqrt{A_2^2+B_2^2}}.} \]
