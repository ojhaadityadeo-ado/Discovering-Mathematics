MATH DETECTIVE: CASE FILE #10

Subject: Three Basic Pythagorean Identities in Trigonometry

Mission: First understand the main identity from the unit circle, then use it to get two more identities and see where each one is valid.

Detective Briefing: This is our first case file in trigonometry, so we start from the basics. The main idea is the unit circle. Once we understand why \[ \sin^2x+\cos^2x=1, \] the next two identities will follow by simple algebra. We only have to be careful about when division is allowed.
Foundation Investigation

Act 1: What Do Sine and Cosine Mean on the Unit Circle?

Quick Recall: Before we move to the unit circle, let us confirm the basic right-triangle ratios once. \[ \sin x=\frac{\text{perpendicular}}{\text{hypotenuse}},\qquad \cos x=\frac{\text{base}}{\text{hypotenuse}},\qquad \tan x=\frac{\text{perpendicular}}{\text{base}}. \] On the unit circle, the same ideas appear in coordinate form: horizontal coordinate $=\cos x$ and vertical coordinate $=\sin x$.

A unit circle is a circle with center at the origin and radius:

🕵️ Detective's Note: “Unit” means one unit.
Evidence Diagram: Unit Circle
P(cos x, sin x): the point on the unit circle.
OQ = cos x: horizontal distance from the origin.
QP = sin x: vertical distance from the axis to P.
OP = 1: the radius of the unit circle.
Take an angle $x$ in standard position. Its terminal side meets the unit circle at a point $P(u,v)$. On the unit circle, \[ u=\cos x,\qquad v=\sin x. \] So the point is \[ P=(\cos x,\sin x). \]

Which coordinate records the horizontal position of $P$?

🕵️ Detective's Note: The first coordinate is the horizontal coordinate.

Which coordinate records the vertical position of $P$?

🕵️ Detective's Note: The second coordinate is the vertical coordinate.
Important: On the unit circle, $\sin x$ and $\cos x$ can be positive or negative, depending on the quadrant. But the sides of the triangle are lengths, so we use $|\cos x|$ and $|\sin x|$. After squaring, the signs do not matter: \[ |\cos x|^2=\cos^2x,\qquad |\sin x|^2=\sin^2x. \]

Why is $OP=1$?

🕵️ Detective's Note: $OP$ is a radius of the unit circle.
Case 14

Act 2: Solve the Master Identity

Now drop a perpendicular from $P$ to the horizontal axis at $Q$. Then $\triangle OQP$ is a right triangle with \[ OQ=|\cos x|,\qquad QP=|\sin x|,\qquad OP=1. \]

Which theorem relates the three sides of this right triangle?

🕵️ Detective's Note: In a right triangle, leg² + leg² = hypotenuse².
Applying the theorem, \[ OQ^2+QP^2=OP^2. \] Therefore \[ |\cos x|^2+|\sin x|^2=1^2. \]

After squaring, what happens to the absolute-value signs?

🕵️ Detective's Note: A number and its absolute value have the same square.
Hence \[ \boxed{\sin^2x+\cos^2x=1}. \] This is the fundamental trigonometric identity. We will use it as the main clue for the next two results.

Why does this identity hold for every real $x$?

🕵️ Detective's Note: The unit-circle definition of sine and cosine works in every quadrant and after any number of rotations.
Domain clue: No division was used in this proof. Since $\sin x$ and $\cos x$ are defined for every real $x$, the identity is valid for all \[ x\in\mathbb R. \]
Case 15

Act 3: Recover the Secant–Tangent Identity

Now recall the definitions \[ \tan x=\frac{\sin x}{\cos x},\qquad \sec x=\frac{1}{\cos x}. \] Both require $\cos x\ne0$.

Starting from $\sin^2x+\cos^2x=1$, what should we divide by if we want the ratios $\frac{\sin x}{\cos x}$ and $\frac{1}{\cos x}$ to appear?

🕵️ Detective's Note: Tangent and secant both have cosine in the denominator.
Assuming $\cos x\ne0$, \[ \frac{\sin^2x}{\cos^2x}+\frac{\cos^2x}{\cos^2x}=\frac{1}{\cos^2x}. \]

What does the middle fraction simplify to?

🕵️ Detective's Note: A nonzero quantity divided by itself is $1$.
Rewrite the remaining fractions as squares: \[ \left(\frac{\sin x}{\cos x}\right)^2+1 =\left(\frac{1}{\cos x}\right)^2. \] Therefore \[ \boxed{1+\tan^2x=\sec^2x}. \]

Why can we not use this identity when $\cos x=0$?

🕵️ Detective's Note: Look at the denominators in the definitions of tangent and secant.
Since $\cos x=0$ at \[ x=\frac{\pi}{2}+k\pi,\qquad k\in\mathbb Z, \] the identity $1+\tan^2x=\sec^2x$ is valid only where these expressions are defined.
Case 16

Act 4: Recover the Cosecant–Cotangent Identity

Now use \[ \cot x=\frac{\cos x}{\sin x},\qquad \csc x=\frac{1}{\sin x}. \] Both require $\sin x\ne0$.

What should we divide the master identity by this time?

🕵️ Detective's Note: Cotangent and cosecant both have sine in the denominator.
Assuming $\sin x\ne0$, \[ \frac{\sin^2x}{\sin^2x}+\frac{\cos^2x}{\sin^2x}=\frac{1}{\sin^2x}. \] Hence \[ 1+\left(\frac{\cos x}{\sin x}\right)^2 =\left(\frac{1}{\sin x}\right)^2, \] so \[ \boxed{1+\cot^2x=\csc^2x}. \]

What must be true before dividing by $\sin^2x$?

🕵️ Detective's Note: We may divide only by a nonzero quantity.
Since $\sin x=0$ at \[ x=k\pi,\qquad k\in\mathbb Z, \] the identity $1+\cot^2x=\csc^2x$ is valid only away from those points.

Act 5: Recovering Both Identities from the Master Clue

Everything comes from \[ \sin^2x+\cos^2x=1. \] There are two useful ways to divide this identity: \[ \frac{\sin^2x+\cos^2x}{\cos^2x}=\frac1{\cos^2x} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \tan^2x+1=\sec^2x, \] and \[ \frac{\sin^2x+\cos^2x}{\sin^2x}=\frac1{\sin^2x} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 1+\cot^2x=\csc^2x. \]

Which statement best describes the relationship among the three identities?

🕵️ Detective's Note: We derived both secondary identities by dividing the master identity.
Key idea: the first identity comes from geometry. The other two come from it by dividing by $\cos^2x$ or $\sin^2x$, whenever that division is allowed.

Act 7: The Shape Mystery — Circle or Hyperbola?

We can also look at the identities as equations in two coordinates. Let \[ X=\cos x,\qquad Y=\sin x. \] Then \[ X^2+Y^2=1, \] which is the unit circle. Both coordinates stay between $-1$ and $1$, so the graph is closed and bounded.

Rearrange the secant–tangent identity:

\[ 1+\tan^2x=\sec^2x \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \sec^2x-\tan^2x=1. \]

If we now write $X=\sec x$ and $Y=\tan x$, what kind of locus is $X^2-Y^2=1$?

🕵️ Detective's Note: A difference of two squares equal to $1$ is the standard hyperbola pattern.
In the same way, \[ \csc^2x-\cot^2x=1 \] has the same hyperbolic form if we use coordinates $X=\csc x$ and $Y=\cot x$.

Why can secant, tangent, cosecant and cotangent become very large near certain angles?

🕵️ Detective's Note: Fractions such as $1/\cos x$ or $1/\sin x$ can grow very large when the denominator is close to zero.
Be precise about “asymptotes”: the coordinate locus \[ X^2-Y^2=1 \] is a hyperbola whose asymptotes are the diagonal lines $Y=\pm X$. Separately, when $\tan x$, $\sec x$, $\cot x$, or $\csc x$ are graphed as functions of the angle $x$, they have vertical asymptotes at their excluded domain values. These are related ideas, but they are not the same graph.
Case Summary \[ \boxed{\sin^2x+\cos^2x=1}\qquad (x\in\mathbb R) \] \[ \boxed{1+\tan^2x=\sec^2x}\qquad (\cos x\ne0) \] \[ \boxed{1+\cot^2x=\csc^2x}\qquad (\sin x\ne0) \] The first identity comes directly from the geometry of the unit circle. The other two are obtained from it by legal division.
