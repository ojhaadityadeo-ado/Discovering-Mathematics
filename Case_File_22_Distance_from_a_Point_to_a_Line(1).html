MATH DETECTIVE: CASE FILE #22

Subject: Distance from a Point to a Line

Mission: Find the unique closest point on a line, read the perpendicular direction from the equation, and explain every part of the distance formula.

Detective Briefing: A line contains infinitely many points. The distance from P to the line means the smallest distance from P to any of those points. First identify the right point; then calculate its distance.

Act 1: Which Point on the Line Is Closest?

The setting
\[ L:Ax+By+C=0,\qquad A^2+B^2>0,\qquad P=(x_0,y_0). \]

We want the smallest possible distance \(PQ\), where \(Q\) may be any point on L.

What does \(A^2+B^2>0\) mean, and why must we check it before using the formula?

Detective hint: Squares are never negative. When can their sum be zero?

Since squares are nonnegative,

\[ A^2+B^2>0\iff A\ne0\text{ or }B\ne0. \]

If \(A=B=0\), the equation becomes \(C=0\). It then describes no points when \(C\ne0\), or the whole plane when \(C=0\). Neither set is a line. Also, \((A,B)=(0,0)\) gives no direction, and the distance formula would divide by zero.

Case when A = B = 0Set describedWhat fails
\(C\ne0\): the equation \(C=0\) is impossibleNo pointsThere is no line to reach.
\(C=0\): the equation is \(0=0\)The whole planeThere is no single line or nonzero perpendicular direction.

Draw the perpendicular from P to L and call its meeting point H. Choose any other point \(Q\in L\).

Why does the right triangle prove that H is the unique closest point?

Detective hint: Triangle PHQ is right-angled at H. Use Pythagoras, and ask when the extra term HQ² is zero.
\[ PQ^2=PH^2+HQ^2\ge PH^2. \]

Lengths are nonnegative, so \(PQ\ge PH\). Equality is possible only when \(HQ=0\), which means \(Q=H\).

The perpendicular foot H is the unique closest point. We now know what to calculate: \(PH\).

Act 2: Read the Perpendicular Direction

Let \(U=(x_1,y_1)\) and \(V=(x_2,y_2)\) be two different coordinate pairs. Start by writing

\[ Ax_1+By_1+C=0,\qquad Ax_2+By_2+C=0. \]

Why are we allowed to write these two equations?

Detective hint: The equation of a line is also a test for whether a point belongs to that line.

We choose U and V on L. The line L is the set of all points whose coordinates make \(Ax+By+C=0\) true. That is why both equations above equal zero.

Extra clue 1 · Both point equations contain the same constant C. We want information about the direction from U to V. What can we do?

Hint: The vector from U to V uses x₂ − x₁ and y₂ − y₁.

Open the reasoning

Subtracting gives \(A(x_2-x_1)+B(y_2-y_1)=0\). The coordinate differences are the components of \(\overrightarrow{UV}\), so this equation tells us how \((A,B)\) relates to a direction along the line.

Subtract the first equation from the second:

\[ A(x_2-x_1)+B(y_2-y_1)=0. \]

The vector \(\overrightarrow{UV}=(x_2-x_1,y_2-y_1)\) points along the line.

Before using a dot product, what does a dot product mean?

Detective hint: One form uses coordinates. The other uses the two vector lengths and the angle θ between them.

For vectors \(\mathbf u=(u_1,u_2)\) and \(\mathbf v=(v_1,v_2)\),

\[ \boxed{\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v=u_1v_1+u_2v_2 =|\mathbf u|\,|\mathbf v|\cos\theta,} \]

where \(\theta\) is the angle between the two vectors. The first form uses coordinates. The second form tells us about the angle.

Now the subtracted equation says

\[ (A,B)\cdot\overrightarrow{UV}=0. \]

The vector \((A,B)\) is nonzero by the condition checked in Act 1. Also, \(\overrightarrow{UV}\ne(0,0)\) because U and V are different. Therefore

\[ |(A,B)|\,|\overrightarrow{UV}|\cos\theta=0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \cos\theta=0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \theta=90^\circ. \]
\[ \boxed{(A,B)\text{ is perpendicular to the line}.} \]

This is the geometric meaning of the coefficients of x and y.

Normal direction\((A,B)\) points perpendicular to L.
Direction along L\((-B,A)\) points along L because \(A(-B)+B(A)=0\).

This works for sloping, horizontal, and vertical lines. We do not need to divide by B to find a slope.

Act 3: Locate H and Measure PH

Move from P in the normal direction. Write

\[ H=P-t(A,B)=(x_0-tA,\ y_0-tB). \]
Extra clue 2 · We have written H = P − t(A, B). What condition can determine the unknown t?

Hint: Moving in the normal direction describes a whole line of possible positions. Which position is the foot H?

Open the reasoning

The direction is already correct. To select the foot, require \(H\in L\). Substituting \(H=(x_0-tA,y_0-tB)\) into the line equation gives one equation for t.

The sign of t chooses which way along the normal direction we move. Because H lies on L,

\[ \begin{aligned} A(x_0-tA)+B(y_0-tB)+C&=0,\\ Ax_0+By_0+C-t(A^2+B^2)&=0. \end{aligned} \]

Therefore

\[ t=\frac{Ax_0+By_0+C}{A^2+B^2}. \]

Is the number |t| already the distance PH?

Detective hint: The move from P to H is the vector −t(A, B). The length of a multiple is the absolute value of the number times the vector’s length.
\[ \overrightarrow{PH}=-t(A,B),\qquad PH=|t|\sqrt{A^2+B^2}. \]

Substitute the value of t:

\[ \boxed{d(P,L)=PH=\frac{|Ax_0+By_0+C|}{\sqrt{A^2+B^2}}.} \]

The numerator gives the size of the equation value at P. The denominator turns that scaled value into a true length.

Act 4: Why Divide by the Normal Length?

Extra clue 3 · We have a proposed distance formula. What quick check tells us whether it depends on how we write the same line?

Hint: A geometric distance belongs to the line and point, not to the scale of the equation's coefficients.

Open the reasoning

Multiplying A, B and C by the same \(k\ne0\) leaves the line unchanged. A valid distance formula must therefore be unchanged too. Both the absolute numerator and the normal length multiply by \(|k|\), so their ratio stays fixed.

The same geometric line has many equations. If \(k\ne0\), then

\[ Ax+By+C=0 \quad\text{and}\quad kAx+kBy+kC=0 \]

have exactly the same solutions.

Could |Ax₀ + By₀ + C| alone be the distance?

Detective hint: A geometric distance must not depend on which equation we use for the same line. Substitute kA, kB, and kC into the numerator.

\[ \frac{|k(Ax_0+By_0+C)|}{\sqrt{(kA)^2+(kB)^2}} = \frac{|k|\,|Ax_0+By_0+C|}{|k|\sqrt{A^2+B^2}} = \frac{|Ax_0+By_0+C|}{\sqrt{A^2+B^2}}. \]

Both parts scale by \(|k|\), so the ratio stays the same. This is why the denominator is necessary.

Act 5: What Does the Sign Tell Us?

Before taking absolute value, we have

\[ d_s(P,L)=\frac{Ax_0+By_0+C}{\sqrt{A^2+B^2}}. \]

This is a signed distance for the chosen normal direction \((A,B)\).

Detective hint: The square root in the denominator is positive. Replace the whole line equation by its negative and watch what happens to the sign.
Positive signed distanceP lies on the side toward which \((A,B)\) points.
Negative signed distanceP lies on the opposite side.

Replacing the equation by its negative changes the chosen normal from \((A,B)\) to \((-A,-B)\). The signed distance changes sign, but its absolute value does not.

Zero distance means P is on the line

Because the denominator is positive,

\[ d(P,L)=0 \iff |Ax_0+By_0+C|=0 \iff Ax_0+By_0+C=0 \iff P\in L. \]

The “Put P on L” button in Act 3 lets you see both P and H become the same point.

The condition \(A^2+B^2>0\), investigated before the proof in Act 1, guarantees that the denominator here is positive.

Act 6: A Fixed-Distance Locus

Fix a positive number \(r\). We want every point \((x,y)\) whose distance from L is r:

\[ \frac{|Ax+By+C|}{\sqrt{A^2+B^2}}=r. \]

Multiply by the positive denominator:

\[ |Ax+By+C|=r\sqrt{A^2+B^2}. \]

What set is described when r > 0?

Detective hint: The equations Ax + By + C = r√(A² + B²) and Ax + By + C = −r√(A² + B²) have the same A and B as L.
\[ Ax+By+C=\pm r\sqrt{A^2+B^2}. \]

Each equation has the same normal vector \((A,B)\), so both lines are parallel to L. One lies on each side, and each is r units away.

Final Report: What Every Part of the Formula Does

\[ \boxed{d(P,L)=\frac{|Ax_0+By_0+C|}{\sqrt{A^2+B^2}}} \]
PartMeaning
\((A,B)\)A direction perpendicular to the line.
\(Ax_0+By_0+C\)A signed measure of how far P is from satisfying the equation.
\(\sqrt{A^2+B^2}\)Changes the normal vector to unit length and removes equation-scaling effects.
Absolute valueRemoves the side sign and gives a nonnegative distance.
Perpendicular foot HThe unique closest point, by \(PQ^2=PH^2+HQ^2\).

The foot itself is

\[ H=\left( x_0-\frac{A(Ax_0+By_0+C)}{A^2+B^2},\; y_0-\frac{B(Ax_0+By_0+C)}{A^2+B^2} \right). \]

The equation \(d_s=r\) gives a line parallel to L. The set \(d=r>0\) uses the two signed values \(d_s=\pm r\).
