MATH DETECTIVE: CASE FILE

Subject: Finite and Infinite Geometric Progressions

Mission: Discover why multiplying a geometric sum by its common ratio creates the exact alignment needed for cancellation, derive the finite-sum formula without guessing it, then understand precisely when and why that formula has a meaningful infinite limit.

The case: A geometric progression has a repeated multiplicative structure. Can we turn that repetition into cancellation? And once the finite sum is known, can we justify replacing “infinitely many terms” by a genuine limit rather than by informal endless addition?

Follow 8 clues and the small-step questions. Try each question before opening its reasoning. Several prompts ask what a step contributes to the global goal; these are meant to make the proof strategy visible, not just the algebra.

Act 1: Detect the structure before calculating

Consider the first n terms of a geometric progression:

\[S_n=a+ar+ar^2+\cdots+ar^{n-1}.\]

Direct addition gives no obvious simplification. So instead of asking “how do I add these?”, ask a structural question.

Clue 1 · What is the defining relationship between consecutive terms?

Follow this clue: Compare ar with a, and ar² with ar.
Open the reasoning

The progression is geometric because every term is the previous term multiplied by the same ratio r:

\[a\xrightarrow{\times r}ar\xrightarrow{\times r}ar^2\xrightarrow{\times r}\cdots.\]

This is the feature the proof must exploit. If a manipulation does not use this multiplicative repetition, it is unlikely to simplify the sum efficiently.

Small step 1. Why is identifying the “multiply by r” pattern important for the global goal?

Open the answer

Because we want many terms to cancel at once. Multiplying the entire sum by r moves every term to the form of the next term. That creates a second expression with almost the same list of terms as the original, which is exactly the situation in which subtraction can remove the middle terms.

Small step 2. Why does simply factoring out a from the original sum not finish the problem?

Open the answer

It gives \(S_n=a(1+r+r^2+\cdots+r^{n-1})\), which is true but still leaves a geometric sum to evaluate. It exposes a common factor, not the cancellation mechanism.

Act 2: Manufacture an aligned copy

What should we do with the whole equation so that each power of r moves one step forward?

Clue 2 · Which operation creates the useful shifted copy?

Follow this clue: We want a to become ar, ar to become ar², and so on.

Multiplying by r gives

\[rS_n=ar+ar^2+ar^3+\cdots+ar^n.\]

The point of this step is not merely to create another equation. It creates matching middle terms. Those matches are the evidence that subtraction will simplify the sum dramatically.

Small step 3. What new term appears at the far right after the shift?

Open the answer

The last original term is \(ar^{n-1}\). Multiplying it by r gives \(ar^n\). This new boundary term will survive the subtraction.

Small step 4. Which original term has no matching partner in the shifted row?

Open the answer

The first term a. The shifted row begins with ar, so a has nothing beneath it. Thus the two unmatched boundary terms are a on the left and \(ar^n\) on the right.

Act 3: Use the alignment — subtract

Now we have two expressions containing the same middle terms.

Clue 3 · What is the immediate next step, and why?

Follow this clue: We deliberately created equal terms in the two rows. Which operation makes equal terms disappear?

Subtract:

\[ \begin{aligned} S_n-rS_n &=(a+ar+ar^2+\cdots+ar^{n-1})\\ &\quad-(ar+ar^2+\cdots+ar^{n-1}+ar^n). \end{aligned} \]

Every middle term cancels:

\[ar-ar=0,\quad ar^2-ar^2=0,\quad\ldots\]

Only the two boundary terms survive:

\[S_n-rS_n=a-ar^n.\]

Small step 5. Why is this cancellation the decisive moment of the finite proof?

Open the answer

Before subtraction, the unknown \(S_n\) was represented by n separate terms. After subtraction, all of that internal complexity has collapsed to two boundary terms. We have converted a long sum into a simple algebraic equation involving \(S_n\).

Factor each side:

\[S_n(1-r)=a(1-r^n).\]

Small step 6. What should the immediate next step be if our global goal is a formula for \(S_n\) alone?

Open the answer

Isolate \(S_n\) by dividing by \(1-r\). But before dividing, we must ask whether that denominator could be zero.

If \(r\neq1\), then \(1-r\neq0\), so division is valid:

\[\boxed{S_n=\frac{a(1-r^n)}{1-r}},\qquad r\neq1.\]
Finite G.P. solved: The proof worked because multiplication by r produced a one-step shift, and subtraction converted that shift into cancellation. The formula was not guessed; it was forced by the structure of the progression.

Act 4: Investigate the excluded case r = 1

The formula above explicitly assumes \(r\neq1\). That condition is not a technical decoration; it protects a division step.

Clue 4 · What exactly fails when r = 1?

Follow this clue: Substitute r=1 into the denominator.

When \(r=1\), every term equals a:

\[S_n=a+a+\cdots+a.\]

There are n copies, so

\[\boxed{S_n=na,\qquad r=1.}\]

Small step 7. Does the failure of the fraction at r=1 mean the sum itself is undefined?

Open the answer

No. Only that particular algebraic expression has a zero denominator. The original finite sum is perfectly meaningful and is simply na.

Numerical stress-test

Take \(a=3, r=2, n=4\). Directly,

\[S_4=3+6+12+24=45.\]

The formula gives

\[ S_4=\frac{3(1-2^4)}{1-2}=\frac{3(1-16)}{-1}=45. \]

Small step 8. Why is a numerical check useful even though it is not a proof?

Open the answer

It can catch sign mistakes, exponent mistakes, or a missing factor. It does not establish the formula for all values, but it tests whether the derived expression behaves correctly in a concrete case.

Act 5: What does an infinite G.P. actually mean?

Now consider

\[a+ar+ar^2+ar^3+\cdots.\]

Can we simply say “keep adding forever”?

Clue 5 · What is the mathematically precise meaning of the infinite sum?

Follow this clue: We already know how to sum the first n terms. What quantity can we study as n grows?

Define the nth partial sum

\[S_n=a+ar+\cdots+ar^{n-1}.\]

Then the infinite sum, if it exists, is

\[\boxed{S_\infty=\lim_{n\to\infty}S_n}.\]

Our finite result therefore becomes the bridge to the infinite problem:

\[S_n=\frac{a(1-r^n)}{1-r}.\]

Small step 9. Why was proving the finite formula first important for the infinite formula?

Open the answer

Because the infinite sum is defined through the sequence of finite partial sums. Once we have a closed expression for \(S_n\), the infinite problem reduces to studying one remaining n-dependent piece: \(r^n\).

Global reduction: At this point the entire convergence question has been reduced to a single question: What happens to \(r^n\) as n grows without bound?

Act 6: Investigate the crucial term rⁿ

Clue 6 · Which condition makes rⁿ tend to zero?

Follow this clue: Test a negative number such as r=−2. It satisfies r<1, but what happens to its powers?

If \(0<|r|<1\), then multiplying repeatedly by \(|r|\) shrinks the magnitude:

\[|r|,\quad |r|^2,\quad |r|^3,\quad\ldots\longrightarrow0.\]

For instance, when \(r=\tfrac12\),

\[\frac12,\frac14,\frac18,\frac1{16},\ldots\to0.\]

If \(r=-\tfrac12\), the signs alternate but the magnitudes still shrink:

\[-\frac12,\frac14,-\frac18,\frac1{16},\ldots\to0.\]

Hence

\[\boxed{\lim_{n\to\infty}r^n=0\qquad(|r|<1)}.\]

Small step 10. Why does the absolute value appear in the condition?

Open the answer

Convergence to zero depends on the size of the powers, not on their sign. A negative ratio with magnitude below 1 may alternate in sign but still approaches zero. A negative ratio such as −2 has r<1 but magnitude greater than 1, so its powers do not approach zero.

|r| < 1The powers \(r^n\) approach 0. This is the convergence case needed for a nontrivial infinite G.P.
r = 1The partial sums are \(na\). For \(a\neq0\), they do not approach a finite limit.
|r| > 1The magnitude \(|r|^n\) grows rather than shrinks, so the terms do not even approach 0 when \(a\neq0\).

Small step 11. What happens at r=−1?

Open the answer

The partial sums alternate between values such as a and 0 (for \(a\neq0\)), so they do not approach one fixed limit. Thus the usual infinite G.P. does not converge at r=−1.

Small step 12. Why is “the terms get small” relevant but not by itself a universal convergence test for all infinite series?

Open the answer

For any convergent series, individual terms must approach zero, but that condition alone is not sufficient for an arbitrary series. Here we have more: an explicit partial-sum formula. Once \(r^n\to0\), that formula directly gives the limit of the partial sums.

Act 7: Take the limit — but only after the condition is secured

Under the condition \(|r|<1\), we know \(r\neq1\), so the finite formula is valid:

\[S_n=\frac{a(1-r^n)}{1-r}.\]
Clue 7 · What is the immediate next step toward S∞?

Follow this clue: The infinite sum was defined as a limit of Sₙ.
Step 1 · Start from the definition.
\[S_\infty=\lim_{n\to\infty}S_n.\]
Why is this important? It prevents us from treating infinity as an ordinary index. We are studying the limiting behavior of a sequence of ordinary finite sums.
Step 2 · Substitute the finite formula.
\[S_\infty=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{a(1-r^n)}{1-r}.\]
Why is this useful? All n-dependence is now concentrated in \(r^n\).

Small step 13. What value replaces rⁿ in the limit when |r|<1?

Open the answer

Zero, because \(\lim_{n\to\infty}r^n=0\).

Step 3 · Evaluate the limit.
\[ \begin{aligned} S_\infty &=\frac{a(1-0)}{1-r}\\[2pt] &=\boxed{\frac{a}{1-r}}. \end{aligned} \]
Global meaning: The finite “tail correction” \(ar^n/(1-r)\) disappears as n grows because \(r^n\to0\). What remains is the limiting total.
Infinite G.P. solved: For \(|r|<1\), the partial sums approach
\[\boxed{S_\infty=\frac{a}{1-r}}.\]
The condition \(|r|<1\) is part of the theorem, not an optional afterthought.

Act 8: Stress-test the result and reconstruct the logic

Clue 8 · Which statement best describes the relationship between the finite and infinite formulas?

Follow this clue: Recall the definition \(S_\infty=\lim S_n\).

Stress-test A · A positive ratio below 1

Let \(a=8\) and \(r=\tfrac12\). Then

\[8+4+2+1+\cdots\]

has predicted sum

\[\frac{8}{1-1/2}=16.\]

The finite partial sums \(8,12,14,15,15.5,\ldots\) approach 16.

Stress-test B · A negative ratio with |r|<1

Let \(a=6\) and \(r=-\tfrac12\). Then

\[6-3+\frac32-\frac34+\cdots\]

has sum

\[\frac{6}{1+1/2}=4.\]

The partial sums oscillate around 4 while getting closer to it.

Stress-test C · r=0

The progression is

\[a+0+0+\cdots.\]

The formula gives

\[\frac{a}{1-0}=a,\]

which is correct.

Small step 14. What special exception should we remember if a=0?

Open the answer

If a=0, every term is zero regardless of r, so every partial sum is zero. The usual condition \(|r|<1\) describes convergence of a nontrivial geometric series; the all-zero series is a harmless degenerate exception.

Final case report

Finite G.P. For

\[S_n=a+ar+\cdots+ar^{n-1},\]

multiplying by r creates a one-step shift. Subtracting cancels the aligned middle terms:

\[S_n-rS_n=a-ar^n.\]

Hence, for \(r\neq1\),

\[\boxed{S_n=\frac{a(1-r^n)}{1-r}}.\]

For \(r=1\), the separate finite result is \(\boxed{S_n=na}\).

Infinite G.P. The infinite sum means the limit of these finite partial sums. When \(|r|<1\), \(r^n\to0\), so

\[\boxed{S_\infty=\frac{a}{1-r}},\qquad |r|<1.\]

The proof strategy: use the repeated ratio to manufacture alignment; use subtraction to create cancellation; use the finite formula to reduce the infinite problem to the behavior of \(r^n\).
