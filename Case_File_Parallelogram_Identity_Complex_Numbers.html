MATH DETECTIVE: CASE FILE

Subject: The Parallelogram Identity for Complex Numbers

Mission: Build a parallelogram from two complex displacements, identify the two diagonals, isolate the only angle-dependent term, and explain why adding the squared diagonal lengths makes that dependence disappear. Then use the same identity to detect rectangles and solve a midpoint optimization problem.

The case: Two adjacent sides of a parallelogram are represented by complex numbers \(z_1\) and \(z_2\). The side lengths can stay fixed while the angle changes, so each diagonal can change. Is there a combination of the two diagonal lengths that remains invariant?

Follow the clues before opening the explanations. The parallelogram explorer updates the geometry and the identity together, so use the presets to test rectangles, flat cases, and ordinary slanted parallelograms.

Act 1: Identify what the two diagonals really are

Place one vertex of the parallelogram at the origin. If the adjacent sides are \(z_1\) and \(z_2\), the four vertices are

\[0,\qquad z_1,\qquad z_2,\qquad z_1+z_2.\]
Clue 1 · Which complex number represents the diagonal from the origin to the opposite vertex?

Follow this clue: Starting at 0, travel first by one side vector and then by the other.
Open the reasoning

Vector addition gives the opposite vertex \(z_1+z_2\). Therefore that diagonal has length \(|z_1+z_2|\).

Small step 1. What complex number gives the other diagonal, joining the endpoints \(z_1\) and \(z_2\)?

Open the answer

The displacement from \(z_2\) to \(z_1\) is \(z_1-z_2\), so the other diagonal has length \(|z_1-z_2|\). Using \(z_2-z_1\) would give the same length.

Act 2: Find the term that remembers the angle

For the first diagonal, use \(|w|^2=w\overline w\):

Step 1 · Expand \(|z_1+z_2|^2\).
\[ \begin{aligned} |z_1+z_2|^2 &=(z_1+z_2)(\overline{z_1}+\overline{z_2})\\ &=|z_1|^2+|z_2|^2+z_1\overline{z_2}+\overline{z_1}z_2\\ &=|z_1|^2+|z_2|^2+2\operatorname{Re}(z_1\overline{z_2}). \end{aligned} \]
Clue 2 · Which part can change if the side lengths stay fixed but the angle changes?

Follow this clue: The two magnitudes are being held fixed.

For nonzero sides, if the angle between them is \(\theta\), then

\[\operatorname{Re}(z_1\overline{z_2})=|z_1||z_2|\cos\theta.\]

Small step 2. What does the diagonal formula become in cosine-rule form?

Open the answer

It becomes \(|z_1+z_2|^2=|z_1|^2+|z_2|^2+2|z_1||z_2|\cos\theta\).

Act 3: Expand the second diagonal

The other diagonal has length \(|z_1-z_2|\). Squaring and expanding gives

Step 2 · Expand \(|z_1-z_2|^2\).
\[ \begin{aligned} |z_1-z_2|^2 &=(z_1-z_2)(\overline{z_1}-\overline{z_2})\\ &=|z_1|^2+|z_2|^2-z_1\overline{z_2}-\overline{z_1}z_2\\ &=|z_1|^2+|z_2|^2-2\operatorname{Re}(z_1\overline{z_2}). \end{aligned} \]
Clue 3 · Compare the two diagonal-square formulas. What changed?

Follow this clue: Put the two formulas directly underneath one another.

Small step 3. If one diagonal-square contains \(+2|z_1||z_2|\cos\theta\) and the other contains the negative of the same quantity, what operation is now especially tempting?

Open the answer

Add the two equations. The angle-dependent terms are exact opposites and will cancel.

Act 4: Make the angle disappear

\[ \begin{aligned} |z_1+z_2|^2 &=|z_1|^2+|z_2|^2+2\operatorname{Re}(z_1\overline{z_2}),\\ |z_1-z_2|^2 &=|z_1|^2+|z_2|^2-2\operatorname{Re}(z_1\overline{z_2}). \end{aligned} \]
Clue 4 · What survives after addition?

Follow this clue: Add term by term and watch the opposite cross terms cancel.

Therefore

\[ \boxed{|z_1+z_2|^2+|z_1-z_2|^2=2(|z_1|^2+|z_2|^2).} \]

The invariant: changing the angle redistributes squared length between the two diagonals, but their total squared length remains fixed.

Small step 4. Why is the invariant about the squares of the diagonal lengths rather than the lengths themselves?

Open the answer

The expansions of squared moduli are linear in the angle term \(\pm2\operatorname{Re}(z_1\overline{z_2})\). Those terms cancel exactly. The unsquared moduli contain square roots, so there is no corresponding linear cancellation.

Act 5: Translate the identity back into geometry

A parallelogram has two sides of length \(|z_1|\) and two of length \(|z_2|\). Therefore the sum of the squares of all four side lengths is

\[2|z_1|^2+2|z_2|^2=2(|z_1|^2+|z_2|^2).\]

So the identity says:

Parallelogram law: The sum of the squares of the two diagonal lengths equals the sum of the squares of the lengths of all four sides.

Clue 5 · If the angle increases and one diagonal-square decreases, what must happen to the other?

Follow this clue: Their sum is fixed once the side lengths are fixed.

Small step 5. Does the argument require a nondegenerate parallelogram?

Open the answer

No. The algebra remains valid if the vectors are parallel, opposite, or one is zero. The geometric figure may collapse onto a line, but the identity still holds.

Act 6: When are the diagonals equal?

Instead of adding the two diagonal-square formulas, subtract them:

\[ \boxed{|z_1+z_2|^2-|z_1-z_2|^2=4\operatorname{Re}(z_1\overline{z_2}).} \]
Clue 6 · For nonzero side vectors, what condition makes the two diagonal lengths equal?

Follow this clue: Equal diagonal lengths mean their squared difference is zero.

For nonzero \(z_1,z_2\),

\[ \operatorname{Re}(z_1\overline{z_2})=|z_1||z_2|\cos\theta. \]

So equality of the diagonals forces

\[\cos\theta=0,\qquad\theta=90^\circ.\]

Thus a nondegenerate parallelogram has equal diagonals exactly when its adjacent sides are perpendicular—that is, exactly when it is a rectangle.

Small step 6. Why is equal side length not the right condition?

Open the answer

A rhombus has equal side lengths but usually unequal diagonals. The diagonal equality is controlled by the dot-product term, hence by perpendicularity, not by equality of the side lengths.

Act 7: Use the identity to find the best midpoint

Let points \(A,B\) have complex coordinates \(a,b\), and let

\[m=\frac{a+b}{2}\]

be their midpoint. For an arbitrary point \(z\), define

\[u=z-m,\qquad v=\frac{b-a}{2}.\]
Clue 7 · What do \(u+v\) and \(u-v\) become?

Follow this clue: Substitute \(m=(a+b)/2\) and simplify carefully.

Small step 7. Verify the first one explicitly.

Open the answer

\(u+v=z-\frac{a+b}{2}+\frac{b-a}{2}=z-a\). Similarly, \(u-v=z-b\).

Apply the parallelogram identity to \(u,v\):

\[ |u+v|^2+|u-v|^2=2|u|^2+2|v|^2. \]

Substitute back:

\[ \boxed{|z-a|^2+|z-b|^2=2|z-m|^2+\frac{|a-b|^2}{2}.} \]
Clue 8 · Which part changes as the point \(z\) moves?

Follow this clue: The endpoints \(A,B\) are fixed.

Since

\[2|z-m|^2\ge0,\]

the sum \(|z-a|^2+|z-b|^2\) is smallest precisely when

\[|z-m|^2=0,\qquad z=m.\]

Optimization consequence: The midpoint uniquely minimizes the sum of the squared distances to the two fixed endpoints.

Small step 8. Why is the minimizer unique?

Open the answer

Because \(|z-m|^2=0\) occurs only at \(z=m\). At every other point it is strictly positive.

Open the answer

Final case report

Main identity:

\[\boxed{|z_1+z_2|^2+|z_1-z_2|^2=2(|z_1|^2+|z_2|^2).}\]

Why it works: each diagonal square contains the same side-length contribution and opposite angle contributions \(\pm2\operatorname{Re}(z_1\overline{z_2})\). Adding cancels the angle.

Geometric meaning: the sum of the squares of the diagonals of a parallelogram equals the sum of the squares of its four sides.

Rectangle test: for nonzero sides, equal diagonals are equivalent to \(\operatorname{Re}(z_1\overline{z_2})=0\), hence perpendicular sides.

Midpoint consequence: \(|z-a|^2+|z-b|^2=2|z-m|^2+|a-b|^2/2\), so the midpoint uniquely minimizes the sum of squared distances.
