MATH DETECTIVE: CASE FILE #36

Subject: Why the Middle Binomial Coefficients Are Largest

Mission: Compare neighboring committee counts, locate the peak exactly, and separate the size of a coefficient from the value of a whole binomial term.

Case: A school has n students. It lists every possible committee and groups the committees by size. Which group contains the most committees?

Choose answers to 8 main clues. Try the short “Small step” questions before opening their answers. The two visual labs and answer checking work offline; equation formatting and display fonts need internet access.

Act 1: Look for a pattern, then ask what it proves

For a nonnegative integer n, the number of r-person committees is \(\binom nr\), where \(0\le r\le n\). The empty committee counts once when r = 0.

Number of studentsCommittee counts, from size 0 onward
n = 41, 4, 6, 4, 1
n = 51, 5, 10, 10, 5, 1

The counts rise toward the middle and then fall. They are also symmetric because choosing r students is the same as choosing the \(n-r\) students left out:

\[\binom nr=\binom n{n-r}.\]
Clue 1 · Does symmetry by itself prove that the middle entries are largest?

Hint: Think of the symmetric sequence 5, 2, 1, 2, 5.

Small step 1 · For n = 4, why is the count for committee size 0 equal to 1 rather than 0?

Check this step

There is exactly one way to choose nobody: choose the empty set. Thus \(\binom40=1\).

Small step 2 · What immediate observation do you make about n = 4 and n = 5?

Check this step

For even n = 4 there is one middle size, r = 2, and one largest coefficient. For odd n = 5 there are two middle sizes, r = 2 and r = 3, and their counts are equal. These examples suggest the theorem, but they do not prove it for every n.

To prove a peak, we will compare each count directly with the next one.

Act 2: Count the same marked records in two ways

We want to collect committees of size r with committees of size \(r+1\). We therefore make one collection of objects and describe every object in that collection in two different ways.

The object we will count is a marked committee record:

\[ (S,a),\qquad |S|=r,\qquad a\notin S. \]

It consists of an r-person committee S together with one named student a outside S.

For example, take \(S=\{\text{Asha},\text{Ben}\}\) and \(a=\text{Chen}\). We can describe this one record in either of these ways:

Build-up descriptionTear-down description
The two-person committee \(\{\text{Asha},\text{Ben}\}\), with Chen named as an outsiderThe three-person committee \(\{\text{Asha},\text{Ben},\text{Chen}^{\star}\}\), with Chen starred

These are not two different objects. They are two descriptions of the same choice. In the first approach, we choose the small committee and then add a named outsider. In the second approach, we choose the enlarged committee and mark the member who was added. Because the descriptions match one for one, their totals must be equal. This idea is called double counting.

Fix an integer r with \(0\le r\le n-1\). We will count pairs \((S,a)\), where S is an r-person committee and a is one student outside S.

Clue 2 · If we choose S first, how many pairs (S, a) can we build?

Hint: After S is fixed, count only students who are not already members.

\[\#\{(S,a)\}=\binom nr(n-r).\]

Small step 3 · Why did we restrict r to at most n − 1?

Check this step

We need an \((r+1)\)-person committee and at least one student outside S. When r = n, there is no next committee size and \(n-r=0\).

Small step 4 · For n = 5 and r = 2, calculate this count.

Check this step
\[\binom52(5-2)=10\cdot3=30.\]

There are 10 two-person committees, and each has 3 possible added students.

Now describe the same object after the new student has been added. Put

\[T=S\cup\{a\}.\]

Then T is an \((r+1)\)-person committee with one distinguished, or starred, member a.

Clue 3 · If we begin with T, how many ways can we choose the distinguished member?

Hint: T has r + 1 members, and the star may be placed on any one of them.

\[\#\{(T,\text{starred member})\} =\binom n{r+1}(r+1).\]

Small step 5 · How do T and its starred member recover the original pair (S, a)?

Check this step

Let a be the starred member and remove it:

\[S=T\setminus\{a\}.\]

This gives one r-person committee S with \(a\notin S\). The two descriptions match pair for pair, so neither count misses or repeats an object.

Counting the same set of pairs in two ways gives

\[\boxed{(n-r)\binom nr=(r+1)\binom n{r+1}.}\]

This is the bridge between neighboring committee sizes.

Act 3: Turn the double count into a comparison

All binomial coefficients here are positive. Divide the double-counting identity by \((r+1)\binom nr\):

\[\boxed{\frac{\binom n{r+1}}{\binom nr} =\frac{n-r}{r+1}.}\]

Small step 6 · Why is this division allowed?

Check this step

For \(0\le r\le n-1\), we have \(r+1>0\), and \(\binom nr>0\). The divisor is therefore nonzero.

Clue 4 · What does this ratio say when it is greater than 1?

Hint: The numerator of the ratio is the next coefficient and the denominator is the current one.

Small step 7 · Solve the inequality carefully: when is the ratio greater than 1?

Check this step
\[\frac{n-r}{r+1}>1 \iff n-r>r+1 \iff n-1>2r \iff r<\frac{n-1}{2}.\]

Both denominators used in the comparison are positive, so the inequality direction does not change.

Position of rNeighbor comparisonWhat happens?
\(r<(n-1)/2\)ratio > 1\(\binom n{r+1}>\binom nr\)
\(r=(n-1)/2\)ratio = 1neighboring coefficients are equal
\(r>(n-1)/2\)ratio < 1\(\binom n{r+1}<\binom nr\)

Small step 8 · When can two neighboring coefficients be equal?

Check this step
\[ \frac{n-r}{r+1}=1 \iff n-r=r+1 \iff r=\frac{n-1}{2}. \]

Since r must be an integer, equality can occur only when n is odd.

Act 4: Locate the peak exactly

The boundary \((n-1)/2\) behaves differently according to whether n is even or odd.

Clue 5 · Let \(n=2m\). Which coefficient is uniquely largest?

Hint: The boundary m − 1/2 lies between the integer indices m − 1 and m.

Follow the last rise and the first fall

For \(n=2m\), the boundary is

\[\frac{n-1}{2}=m-\frac12,\]

which is not an integer. The sequence rises whenever \(r\le m-1\), because then \(r

\[\frac{\binom{2m}{m}}{\binom{2m}{m-1}} =\frac{m+1}{m}>1,\qquad \frac{\binom{2m}{m+1}}{\binom{2m}{m}} =\frac{m}{m+1}<1.\]

So the unique maximum occurs at r = m:

\[\boxed{\binom{2m}{m}}\]

Small step 9 · The coefficient index is r = m. What is its term position in a binomial expansion?

Check this step
\[T_{r+1}=T_{m+1}.\]

The first term has r = 0, so a coefficient with index r appears in position r + 1.

Clue 6 · Let \(n=2m+1\). Where are the largest coefficients?

Hint: Now the boundary (n − 1)/2 is the integer m.

See why the top is flat for one step

For \(n=2m+1\),

\[\frac{n-1}{2}=m.\]

The sequence rises before r = m. At r = m the ratio equals 1:

\[\frac{\binom{2m+1}{m+1}}{\binom{2m+1}{m}} =\frac{m+1}{m+1}=1.\]

After that it falls. Hence

\[\boxed{\binom{2m+1}{m}=\binom{2m+1}{m+1}}\]

are the two maxima, in term positions \(T_{m+1}\) and \(T_{m+2}\).

Small step 10 · For n = 7, identify m, the two largest indices, and their term positions.

Check this step
\[7=2(3)+1,\quad m=3.\]
\[r=3,4;\qquad T_4,\ T_5.\]

Indeed, the row is \(1,7,21,35,35,21,7,1\).

The neighbor ratios prove more than symmetry: the sequence rises, has one peak or one equal pair, and then falls. This shape is called unimodal.

Act 5: Verify the ratio with factorials

The committee argument explains the ratio. The factorial calculation checks the same fact algebraically. For \(0\le r\le n-1\),

\[\binom nr=\frac{n!}{r!(n-r)!},\qquad \binom n{r+1}=\frac{n!}{(r+1)!(n-r-1)!}.\]

Small step 11 · When we divide by \(\binom nr\), what operation should we perform?

Check this step
\[\frac{\binom n{r+1}}{\binom nr} = \frac{n!}{(r+1)!(n-r-1)!} \cdot \frac{r!(n-r)!}{n!}.\]

Dividing by a fraction means multiplying by its reciprocal.

Cancel \(n!\):

\[=\frac{r!(n-r)!}{(r+1)!(n-r-1)!}.\]

Small step 12 · Which two larger factorials should we expand by one factor?

Check this step
\[(r+1)!=(r+1)r!,\qquad (n-r)!=(n-r)(n-r-1)!.\]

Substitution now makes every cancellation visible:

\[\begin{aligned} \frac{\binom n{r+1}}{\binom nr} &=\frac{r!\,(n-r)(n-r-1)!} {(r+1)r!\,(n-r-1)!}\\ &=\frac{n-r}{r+1}. \end{aligned}\]

Small step 13 · Why would this written factorial calculation need care at r = n?

Check this step

It would contain \((n-r-1)!=(-1)!\), which is not defined. More basically, \(\binom n{r+1}\) would ask for an \((n+1)\)-person committee from n students. Our neighbor comparison is meant for \(0\le r\le n-1\).

The combinatorial proof and the algebraic proof reach the same exact ratio for two different reasons.

Act 7: Why do binomial coefficients appear in an expansion?

Begin with a small example:

\[ (x+y)^3=(x+y)(x+y)(x+y). \]

To form one product term, choose one symbol from each factor. Choosing x from the first factor and y from the next two gives

\[ x\cdot y\cdot y=xy^2. \]

But this is not the only way to obtain \(xy^2\). We may choose y from any two of the three factor positions:

Factor 1Factor 2Factor 3Product
xyy\(xy^2\)
yxy\(xy^2\)
yyx\(xy^2\)

These three identical products are added, giving \(3xy^2\). The coefficient 3 counts the three choices that produced the same product.

Now use the same idea for \((x+y)^n\). To obtain \(x^{n-r}y^r\), choose y from exactly r of the n factor positions. Every remaining position then supplies x.

\[ \text{number of ways to choose the r y-positions}=\binom nr. \]

Each choice gives one copy of \(x^{n-r}y^r\). Adding those copies produces the coefficient \(\binom nr\).

Clue 7 · Why is the coefficient of \(x^{n-r}y^r\) equal to \(\binom nr\)?

Hint: Once the r positions that supply y are chosen, all remaining positions must supply x.

Small step 14 · For \((x+y)^4\), how many choices produce \(x^2y^2\)?

Check this step

Choose 2 of the 4 factor positions to supply y. The possible pairs of y-positions are

\[ \{1,2\},\ \{1,3\},\ \{1,4\},\ \{2,3\},\ \{2,4\},\ \{3,4\}. \]

There are 6 choices, so the coefficient is \(\binom42=6\). Each choice contributes one copy of \(x^2y^2\).

The middle coefficient is largest because the middle-sized group contains the greatest number of different position choices. Every individual choice contributes exactly once.

When n is even, the most balanced product uses n/2 copies of x and n/2 copies of y. When n is odd, the two most balanced power patterns differ by one choice and have equal coefficients.

Act 8: Coefficients and whole terms are different

The \((r+1)\)-st term in the expansion is

\[T_{r+1}=\binom nr x^{n-r}y^r.\]
Clue 8 · Does the largest binomial coefficient always give the term with the largest magnitude?

Hint: Compare neighboring whole terms, including their powers, rather than comparing coefficients alone.

For nonzero \(x\) and \(y\), compare neighboring term magnitudes:

\[\frac{|T_{r+2}|}{|T_{r+1}|} = \frac{\binom n{r+1}|x|^{n-r-1}|y|^{r+1}} {\binom nr|x|^{n-r}|y|^r} = \frac{n-r}{r+1}\frac{|y|}{|x|}.\]

Small step 15 · Why do the coefficient peaks and term-magnitude peaks agree when \(|x|=|y|>0\)?

Check this step
\[|T_{r+1}|=\binom nr|x|^{n-r}|y|^r =\binom nr|x|^n.\]

The factor \(|x|^n\) is the same for every r, so only the coefficient changes. The signs of x and y may change term signs, but not these magnitudes.

Small step 16 · If \(|y|>|x|\), which way does the extra factor \(|y|/|x|\) push the largest term?

Check this step

It makes the neighboring-term ratio larger, so the terms keep increasing for more steps. The peak moves toward larger r, where y has the larger exponent. If \(|x|>|y|\), the peak moves toward smaller r.
