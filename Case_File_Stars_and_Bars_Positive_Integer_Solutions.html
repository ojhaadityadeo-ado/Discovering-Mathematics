MATH DETECTIVE: CASE FILE

Subject: Stars and Bars — Positive Integer Solutions

Mission: Explain why requiring every variable to be positive changes the stars-and-bars count from \(\binom{n+r-1}{r-1}\) to \(\binom{n-1}{r-1}\), and prove the result in two different but equivalent ways.

The case: A teacher has n identical chocolates and r named students, but this time every student must receive at least one. Before counting anything, we must first decide whether such a distribution can exist.

Follow the clues in order. The two proofs use different pictures, but both remove the same obstacle: the compulsory minimum of one chocolate per student.

Act 1: Check whether a solution can exist

Before using any formula, what must be true if every one of the r students is to receive at least one chocolate?

Clue 1 · What is the necessary feasibility condition?

Follow this clue: Imagine first handing exactly one chocolate to each student.

To give at least one chocolate to each of r students, we must spend at least r chocolates. Thus

\[\boxed{n\ge r}.\]

Small step 2. What happens at the boundary \(n=r\)?

Open the answer

Exactly one distribution is possible: every student receives one chocolate, so the unique solution is \((1,1,\ldots,1)\).

Act 2: Remove the compulsory minimum

Assume now that \(n\ge r\).

Can we change the problem into the nonnegative stars-and-bars problem we already know?

Clue 2 · What should we do first?

Follow this clue: Satisfy the unavoidable requirement before counting the free choices.

Give one chocolate to each of the r students. That compulsory step uses r chocolates, leaving

\[n-r\]

chocolates still to distribute.

Small step 3. After this compulsory allocation, may a student receive zero of the remaining chocolates?

Open the answer

Yes. The student already has the required one chocolate, so receiving zero additional chocolates is now allowed. The remaining problem is therefore nonnegative rather than positive.

Act 3: Encode the shift algebraically

Let the original positive solution be

\[(x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_r),\qquad x_i\ge1.\]

Define

\[y_i=x_i-1.\]
Clue 3 · What properties do the new variables have?

Follow this clue: Substitute \(x_i=y_i+1\) into the original equation.

Since \(x_i\ge1\), subtracting one gives

\[y_i\ge0.\]

Also,

\[ (y_1+1)+(y_2+1)+\cdots+(y_r+1)=n. \]

There are r copies of 1, so

\[y_1+y_2+\cdots+y_r+r=n,\]

hence

\[\boxed{y_1+\cdots+y_r=n-r,\qquad y_i\ge0}.\]

Small step 4. What has become simpler?

Open the answer

The lower bound has changed from 1 to 0. That puts the transformed equation exactly into the standard nonnegative stars-and-bars form.

Act 4: Check that the transformation loses nothing

If we count the y-solutions, are we guaranteed to count the original x-solutions exactly once?

Clue 4 · Why is the substitution reversible?

Follow this clue: Ask whether knowing y determines x without any choice.
Positive solution
\((x_1,\ldots,x_r)\), each \(x_i\ge1\), sum n
↔
Nonnegative solution
\((y_1,\ldots,y_r)\), each \(y_i\ge0\), sum \(n-r\)

The two directions are

\[y_i=x_i-1\qquad\longleftrightarrow\qquad x_i=y_i+1.\]

Small step 5. Why is reversibility essential?

Open the answer

Without it, two different positive solutions might collapse to the same y-solution, or some y-solutions might fail to correspond to any original solution. Reversibility proves an exact one-to-one correspondence.

Key idea: “Give one to everyone first” is not just a story. Algebraically, it is the bijection \(x_i\leftrightarrow y_i=x_i-1\).

Act 5: Apply the nonnegative formula

The transformed equation is

\[y_1+\cdots+y_r=n-r,\qquad y_i\ge0.\]

For a nonnegative equation

\[z_1+\cdots+z_r=N,\]

the number of solutions is

\[\binom{N+r-1}{r-1}.\]
Clue 5 · What should replace N here?

Follow this clue: The y-variables sum to the number of chocolates left after the compulsory allocation.

Therefore

\[ \binom{(n-r)+r-1}{r-1}. \]

Small step 6. What cancels inside the upper entry?

Open the answer

The terms \(-r\) and \(+r\) cancel, leaving \(n-1\).

Hence

\[ \boxed{ \#\{(x_1,\ldots,x_r)\in\mathbb Z_{\ge1}^r:x_1+\cdots+x_r=n\} =\binom{n-1}{r-1} }. \]

Act 6: A second proof from gaps between stars

Now forget the substitution and look directly at n stars in a row:

Clue 6 · Where may separators be placed if no group may be empty?

Follow this clue: Any empty compartment would represent a zero variable, which is forbidden.

To obtain r nonempty groups, choose r−1 different internal gaps for separators.

There are n−1 possible gaps, so the count is

\[\boxed{\binom{n-1}{r-1}}.\]

Small step 7. Why are beginning and ending separators forbidden now?

Open the answer

A separator before the first star or after the last star would create an empty first or last group, corresponding to a variable equal to zero.

Small step 8. Why are repeated separators in the same gap forbidden?

Open the answer

Two separators with no star between them would create an empty middle group, again producing a zero variable.

Act 7: Why the positive and nonnegative formulas differ

Clue 7 · What is the structural reason for the change?

Follow this clue: Compare what adjacent bars mean in the nonnegative problem.

Small step 9. How do the two proofs express the same restriction in different languages?

Open the answer

The substitution proof spends one compulsory unit on every variable before counting the remainder. The gap proof forces one star into every compartment by allowing separators only between existing stars.

Act 8: Stress-test the formula

Case 1 · n=r

The only solution is

\[(1,1,\ldots,1).\]

The formula gives

\[\binom{r-1}{r-1}=1.\]

Small step 10. What does the gap picture look like here?

Open the answer

There are r stars and r−1 internal gaps, and every gap must receive a separator. This creates r groups of exactly one star each.

Case 2 · n<r

There are too few chocolates to give one to each student, so there are no solutions.

Clue 8 · Should we force the formula to count this case?

Follow this clue: Can r people each receive at least one object if fewer than r objects exist?

Act 9: Generalize the minimum requirement

Suppose variable \(x_i\) must satisfy

\[x_i\ge a_i,\]

where each \(a_i\) is a fixed nonnegative integer.

Set

\[y_i=x_i-a_i.\]

Then \(y_i\ge0\), and

\[ \sum_{i=1}^r y_i = \sum_{i=1}^r x_i- \sum_{i=1}^r a_i = n-\sum_{i=1}^r a_i. \]

Small step 11. What feasibility condition appears immediately?

Open the answer

We need \(n\ge\sum a_i\); otherwise the compulsory minimum allocations already exceed the available total.

Applying the nonnegative formula gives

\[ \boxed{ \binom{n-\sum a_i+r-1}{r-1} } \qquad\text{provided }n\ge\sum a_i. \]

Small step 12. How does the positive-solution formula appear as a special case?

Open the answer

Take \(a_1=a_2=\cdots=a_r=1\). Then \(\sum a_i=r\), so the general formula becomes \(\binom{n-r+r-1}{r-1}=\binom{n-1}{r-1}\).

Reusable principle: Satisfy every compulsory minimum first. Then count how the remaining total can be distributed freely.

Final case report

For integers \(r\ge1\) and \(n\ge r\), the equation

\[x_1+x_2+\cdots+x_r=n,\qquad x_i\ge1\]

has

\[\boxed{\binom{n-1}{r-1}}\]

positive integer solutions.

First proof: Give one unit to every variable, or set \(y_i=x_i-1\). This produces a reversible correspondence with nonnegative solutions of \(y_1+\cdots+y_r=n-r\).

Second proof: Place n stars in a row and choose r−1 of the n−1 internal gaps as separators. Distinct internal separators guarantee that every group is nonempty.
