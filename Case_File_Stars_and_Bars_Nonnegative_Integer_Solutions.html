MATH DETECTIVE: CASE FILE

Subject: Stars and Bars — Nonnegative Integer Solutions

Mission: Turn the equation \(x_1+x_2+\cdots+x_r=n\) into a counting problem, discover a reversible stars-and-bars encoding, and prove why the number of nonnegative integer solutions is \(\binom{n+r-1}{r-1}\).

The case: A teacher has n identical chocolates and r named students. Every chocolate must be distributed, and a student may receive none. Count all possible distributions—and explain why the familiar stars-and-bars picture is an exact proof rather than a pattern-recognition trick.

Follow the clues in order. Try to predict each move before opening the explanation. The central issue is not drawing stars and bars; it is proving that the drawing gives a one-to-one reversible encoding of the original solutions.

Act 1: Translate the story into an equation

What information is enough to specify one distribution?

Clue 1 · Which record completely determines a distribution?

Follow this clue: The students are named, so we must remember how many chocolates each particular student receives.

If student \(i\) receives \(x_i\) chocolates, then a distribution is recorded by

\[(x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_r).\]

Because every chocolate is distributed,

\[x_1+x_2+\cdots+x_r=n.\]

And because a student may receive none but cannot receive a negative or fractional number,

\[x_i\in\{0,1,2,\ldots\}.\]

Small step 1. Why does the distribution problem become exactly the integer-solution problem?

Open the answer

Each distribution gives one tuple of nonnegative integers whose sum is n. Conversely, any such tuple tells us to give student i exactly \(x_i\) chocolates. No information is lost in either direction.

Small step 2. What would change if some chocolates were left undistributed?

Open the answer

Then we would have \(x_1+\cdots+x_r\le n\), not equality. The present formula is built around distributing all n identical objects.

Act 2: Identify what is identical and what is distinguishable

Clue 2 · Why does it matter that the chocolates are identical?

Follow this clue: Ask whether swapping two physically identical chocolates changes anyone's total.

If the chocolates were labelled, giving chocolate A to student 1 and chocolate B to student 2 could differ from exchanging them. Here, only the amounts matter.

The students, however, are named. So

\[(3,1)\neq(1,3).\]

Small step 3. Which side of the problem should the bars eventually represent: chocolates or students?

Open the answer

The stars will represent the identical chocolates. The bars will separate the shares belonging to different named students.

Act 3: Discover an encoding

Take a small case:

\[n=5,\qquad r=3.\]

Suppose the distribution is

\[(2,0,3).\]
Clue 3 · Why do r students require only r−1 bars?

Follow this clue: Two dividers split a line into three regions; three dividers split it into four.

Small step 4. If there are four students, how many compartments and how many bars are needed?

Open the answer

Four compartments and three bars. In general, \(r-1\) bars create r compartments.

Act 4: Let zero appear naturally

Clue 4 · What does a pair of adjacent bars mean?

Follow this clue: Count the stars between those two bars.

In

\[\star\star\mid\mid\star\star\star,\]

the middle compartment has no stars. That is exactly the value \(x_2=0\).

A bar at the beginning similarly allows \(x_1=0\), and a bar at the end allows \(x_r=0\).

Small step 5. Why would forbidding adjacent bars change the problem?

Open the answer

It would force every interior compartment to contain at least one star. That would correspond to positive solutions, not the nonnegative solutions we are counting here.

Observation: The permission to place bars next to one another is not a technical nuisance. It is the visual mechanism that represents zero values.

Act 5: Prove the correspondence in both directions

A useful encoding is not enough. How do we know it neither loses solutions nor creates fake ones?

Clue 5 · What must be checked before we can count strings instead of solutions?

Follow this clue: We need a reversible rule, not just a picture.

Direction 1 · From a solution to a string

Given

\[(x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_r),\]

write \(x_1\) stars, then a bar; then \(x_2\) stars, then a bar; continue until the final \(x_r\) stars.

Once the tuple is known, there is no choice in how to build the string.

Small step 6. Why does this string contain exactly n stars?

Open the answer

Because the total number written is \(x_1+\cdots+x_r=n\). The stars are grouped differently, but their total remains n.

Direction 2 · From a string to a solution

Start with any string containing exactly n stars and \(r-1\) bars. Count the stars in the r compartments:

\[(x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_r).\]

Each count is a nonnegative integer, and together they use all n stars, so

\[x_1+\cdots+x_r=n.\]

Small step 7. Could two different strings give the same ordered tuple?

Open the answer

No. Once the compartment counts are fixed, the positions of the bars are forced: after \(x_1\) stars place the first bar, after the next \(x_2\) stars place the second, and so on.

Nonnegative solutions
\((x_1,\ldots,x_r)\) with sum n
↔
Stars-and-bars strings
n identical stars and \(r-1\) identical bars
The key proof step: The construction is reversible. That one-to-one correspondence is why counting the strings gives exactly the number of solutions.

Act 6: Count the strings

A stars-and-bars string contains

\[n+(r-1)=n+r-1\]

symbols altogether.

Clue 6 · What is the simplest way to specify one string?

Follow this clue: Once the bar positions are chosen, every other position must be a star.

There are \(n+r-1\) positions. Choose the \(r-1\) bar positions:

\[\binom{n+r-1}{r-1}.\]

The remaining n positions automatically contain stars.

Small step 8. Could we instead choose the star positions?

Open the answer

Yes. That gives \(\binom{n+r-1}{n}\), which is equal to \(\binom{n+r-1}{r-1}\) by binomial symmetry.

Clue 7 · Why do we not multiply by \((r-1)!\) after choosing the bar positions?

Follow this clue: Ask whether swapping the identities of two separator bars changes any compartment size.

Therefore

\[ \boxed{ \#\{(x_1,\ldots,x_r)\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^r:x_1+\cdots+x_r=n\} =\binom{n+r-1}{r-1} }. \]

Act 7: Read the same count in factorial form

The string contains n identical stars and \(r-1\) identical bars.

If all \(n+r-1\) symbols were temporarily distinct, there would be

\[(n+r-1)!\]

orders.

Small step 9. Why does that overcount each genuine string?

Open the answer

Permuting the n temporary star labels does not change the visible string, giving a factor of \(n!\). Permuting the \(r-1\) temporary bar labels also changes nothing, giving a factor of \((r-1)!\).

So the number of distinct strings is

\[ \frac{(n+r-1)!}{n!(r-1)!} =\binom{n+r-1}{r-1}. \]

Small step 10. Is this a second proof of the correspondence?

Open the answer

No. This only counts the strings. The reason those strings solve the original problem was established earlier by the reversible encoding.

Act 8: Stress-test the formula

Case 1 · No chocolates: n=0

The only possible distribution is

\[(0,0,\ldots,0).\]

The formula gives

\[\binom{r-1}{r-1}=1.\]

Small step 11. What does the stars-and-bars string look like when n=0?

Open the answer

It contains no stars and only \(r-1\) bars. Those adjacent bars create r empty compartments, corresponding to all variables being zero.

Case 2 · One student: r=1

The equation becomes

\[x_1=n,\]

so there is exactly one solution. The formula gives

\[\binom n0=1.\]

Small step 12. How many bars are used when r=1?

Open the answer

Zero. One compartment needs no divider. The n stars simply form the one student's share.

Case 3 · What if the recipients were indistinguishable?

Clue 8 · Would the same formula still count correctly?

Follow this clue: Compare what (3,1) and (1,3) mean when the two recipients have no labels.

That becomes a different counting problem involving integer partitions. Stars and bars in this form counts ordered shares assigned to distinguishable recipients.

Final case report

For integers \(n\ge0\) and \(r\ge1\),

\[ \boxed{x_1+x_2+\cdots+x_r=n,\qquad x_i\ge0} \]

has

\[\boxed{\binom{n+r-1}{r-1}}\]

nonnegative integer solutions.

Why: Encode each solution by n identical stars separated into r labelled compartments by \(r-1\) identical bars. Zero values appear as empty compartments. The encoding is reversible, so it is a bijection. Counting the resulting strings means choosing the \(r-1\) bar positions among \(n+r-1\) total positions.
