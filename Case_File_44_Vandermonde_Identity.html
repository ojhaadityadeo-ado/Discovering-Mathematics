MATH DETECTIVE: CASE FILE #44

Subject: Vandermonde's Identity

Mission: Count one r-person committee from two departments in two ways. Track how many members come from the first department, determine the truly feasible values of k, then verify the identity by comparing coefficients in a product of binomial expansions.

The case: Department A has m employees and Department B has n employees. The departments are disjoint, and one joint committee of exactly r people is required. How many committees can be formed?

Follow 7 clues and the small-step questions. Try each clue before opening its reasoning. The Vandermonde lab works offline; equation formatting and display fonts use an internet connection.

Act 1: Count the committee globally

Department A contributes m people and Department B contributes n. Because the departments are disjoint, the combined pool contains

\[m+n\]

people. We want one committee containing exactly r people.

Clue 1 · What is the immediate global count?

Follow this clue: Ignore department labels for one moment. How many people are in the union, and how many must be chosen?
Open the reasoning

Once the two disjoint departments are merged into one pool, the problem is simply: choose r people from m+n. Therefore the direct count is \(\binom{m+n}{r}\).

Small step 1. Why must the departments be treated as disjoint groups?

Open the answer

If the same person belonged to both groups and were counted twice, the combined pool would not have m+n distinct people. Vandermonde's committee interpretation assumes two disjoint source groups.

Act 2: Record one extra piece of information

Instead of ignoring department labels, what can we record about each final committee?

Let k be the number of committee members who come from Department A.

Clue 2 · If k members come from Department A, how many must come from Department B?

Follow this clue: The final committee size is fixed at r.
Open the reasoning

There are r places altogether. If k of them are already filled by Department A, exactly r−k places remain for Department B. The second number is forced; it is not an independent choice.

For one fixed value of k:

\[\binom mk\]

chooses the Department A members, while

\[\binom n{r-k}\]

chooses the Department B members.

Clue 3 · Why do these two counts multiply?

Follow this clue: First choose the A-part of the committee; then choose the B-part.

Thus the number of committees with exactly k members from Department A is

\[\binom mk\binom n{r-k}.\]

Small step 2. Why does the union of the two selected subgroups identify one unique final committee?

Open the answer

The source groups are disjoint. So from the final committee we can recover exactly which selected members came from A and which came from B. No information is lost when the two subgroup selections are united.

Act 3: Which k-values are actually possible?

The convenient sum will run from k=0 to k=r, but not every one of those values must be feasible when m or n is small.

Department A can supply k members only if

\[0\le k\le m.\]

Department B can supply r−k members only if

\[0\le r-k\le n.\]
Clue 4 · What range satisfies both departments at once?

Follow this clue: Rewrite \(r-k\le n\) as a lower bound on k, and combine all lower and upper bounds.
Open the reasoning

From \(r-k\le n\) we get \(k\ge r-n\). Together with k≥0, the lower bound is \(\max(0,r-n)\). The upper conditions are k≤m and k≤r, so the upper bound is \(\min(m,r)\).

\[\boxed{\max(0,r-n)\le k\le\min(m,r)}.\]

Small step 3. If m=3, n=5 and r=6, what k-values are feasible?

Open the answer

The lower bound is \(\max(0,6-5)=1\), and the upper bound is \(\min(3,6)=3\). So k=1,2,3 are feasible.

With the standard zero convention, impossible choices contribute zero:

\[\binom ab=0\qquad\text{when }b<0\text{ or }b>a.\]

That is why the compact version can safely be written from k=0 to k=r.

Act 4: Add the composition classes

Every r-person committee has exactly one value of k: the number of its members from Department A. Therefore different k-values describe non-overlapping classes, and together the classes contain every possible committee.

Clue 5 · Why are we allowed to sum the products over k?

Follow this clue: Take one fixed committee and count how many of its members belong to Department A.

Counting by composition gives

\[\sum_{k=0}^{r}\binom mk\binom n{r-k}.\]

Act 1 counted the same collection directly as \(\binom{m+n}{r}\). Hence

\[\boxed{\sum_{k=0}^{r}\binom mk\binom n{r-k}=\binom{m+n}{r}}.\]

The feasible-range version is

\[\sum_{k=\max(0,r-n)}^{\min(m,r)}\binom mk\binom n{r-k}=\binom{m+n}{r}.\]
The main counting idea: one joint committee can be counted globally, or split according to how many members came from Department A. Vandermonde's identity says those two descriptions count the same collection.

Small step 4. What do the zero rows in the lab mean?

Open the answer

They represent proposed splits that ask one department for an impossible number of people. The zero convention lets those terms remain in the compact sum without changing its value.

Act 5: Recover the sum-of-squares identity

Vandermonde's identity contains the previous sum-of-squares result as a special case.

Set both department sizes equal to N and choose a committee of size N:

\[m=n=N,\qquad r=N.\]

Then Vandermonde becomes

\[\sum_{k=0}^{N}\binom Nk\binom N{N-k}=\binom{2N}{N}.\]
Clue 6 · Why does this turn into a sum of squares?

Follow this clue: Recall \(\binom Na=\binom N{N-a}\).

Using symmetry term by term,

\[\binom N{N-k}=\binom Nk,\]

so

\[\boxed{\sum_{k=0}^{N}\binom Nk^2=\binom{2N}{N}}.\]

Small step 5. Does Vandermonde require the two source groups to have equal sizes?

Open the answer

No. Equal group sizes are only the special choice that produces the square-sum identity. Vandermonde itself allows any nonnegative m and n.

Act 6: Stress-test an impossible committee

Suppose r>m+n. Then the requested committee is larger than the entire combined pool.

Clue 7 · What happens to both sides when r>m+n?

Follow this clue: Can any split k and r−k fit inside groups of total size m+n?
Open the reasoning

The right side is \(\binom{m+n}{r}=0\). On the left, every proposed split requires too many people from at least one department, so every product contains a zero binomial coefficient. The whole sum is also zero.

Small step 6. What happens at r=0?

Open the answer

There is exactly one committee: the empty committee. The only nonzero split is k=0, giving \(\binom m0\binom n0=1\), while the right side is \(\binom{m+n}{0}=1\).

Act 7: Coefficient proof, one step at a time

The counting proof already proves the identity. Now verify it algebraically using

\[(1+t)^m(1+t)^n=(1+t)^{m+n}.\]
Step 1 · Expand each factor separately.
\[(1+t)^m=\sum_{k=0}^{m}\binom mk t^k,\]

and use a different index for the second factor:

\[(1+t)^n=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\binom nj t^j.\]
Why use k and j? The two factors make independent exponent choices. Separate index letters prevent us from accidentally treating them as the same choice.

Small step 7. What power of t appears when \(\binom mk t^k\) is multiplied by \(\binom nj t^j\)?

Open the answer

The powers multiply as \(t^kt^j=t^{k+j}\), so the exponent is k+j and the coefficient contribution is \(\binom mk\binom nj\).

Step 2 · Multiply the two expansions.
\[ (1+t)^m(1+t)^n =\left(\sum_{k=0}^{m}\binom mk t^k\right) \left(\sum_{j=0}^{n}\binom nj t^j\right). \]

A typical product term is

\[\binom mk\binom nj t^{k+j}.\]

Small step 8. Which pairs of exponents contribute to the coefficient of \(t^r\)?

Open the answer

Exactly the pairs satisfying \(k+j=r\). Once k is chosen, j is forced to be r−k.

Step 3 · Replace j by r−k.

For the terms that produce \(t^r\),

\[j=r-k.\]

So the coefficient contribution associated with k is

\[\binom mk\binom n{r-k}.\]
Why is the second index not independent? We are no longer looking at every product term; we are selecting only those whose exponents add to the fixed target r.

Small step 9. Which inequalities make one such term genuinely feasible?

Open the answer

We need \(0\le k\le m\) and \(0\le r-k\le n\). Equivalently, \(\max(0,r-n)\le k\le\min(m,r)\).

Step 4 · Add every contribution to t^r.

Coefficients of the same power add, so

\[ [t^r]\big((1+t)^m(1+t)^n\big) =\sum_{k=0}^{r}\binom mk\binom n{r-k}. \]
Why is the compact range 0≤k≤r allowed? If k>m or r−k>n, the corresponding binomial coefficient is zero. Impossible exponent choices therefore contribute nothing.

Small step 10. If we instead wrote only the nonzero terms, what would the limits be?

Open the answer

They would be \(k=\max(0,r-n)\) through \(k=\min(m,r)\). This is the same feasible range found combinatorially.

Step 5 · Simplify the product before reading the right coefficient.

Powers with the same base add exponents:

\[(1+t)^m(1+t)^n=(1+t)^{m+n}.\]

By the binomial theorem,

\[(1+t)^{m+n}=\sum_{q=0}^{m+n}\binom{m+n}{q}t^q.\]

Therefore

\[[t^r](1+t)^{m+n}=\binom{m+n}{r}.\]

Small step 11. Why may we equate the two coefficient expressions?

Open the answer

The two polynomial expressions are equal for every t. Equal polynomials have equal coefficients of each power, including the coefficient of \(t^r\).

Step 6 · Equate the coefficients of t^r.
\[ \sum_{k=0}^{r}\binom mk\binom n{r-k} =\binom{m+n}{r}. \]

Thus

\[\boxed{\sum_{k=0}^{r}\binom mk\binom n{r-k}=\binom{m+n}{r}}.\]

Full coefficient calculation in one chain

\[ \begin{aligned} (1+t)^m(1+t)^n &=\left(\sum_{k=0}^{m}\binom mk t^k\right) \left(\sum_{j=0}^{n}\binom nj t^j\right),\\[4pt] [t^r]\big((1+t)^m(1+t)^n\big) &=\sum_{k=0}^{r}\binom mk\binom n{r-k},\\[4pt] (1+t)^m(1+t)^n &=(1+t)^{m+n},\\[4pt] [t^r](1+t)^{m+n} &=\binom{m+n}{r}. \end{aligned} \]

Final case report

For nonnegative integers m and n, using the usual zero convention for impossible binomial choices,

\[\boxed{\sum_{k=0}^{r}\binom mk\binom n{r-k}=\binom{m+n}{r}}.\]

Why it makes sense: choose r people from two disjoint departments. Globally there are \(\binom{m+n}{r}\) committees.

Why each summand appears: if exactly k members come from Department A, then r−k must come from Department B, giving \(\binom mk\binom n{r-k}\).

Why the terms add: every committee has one unique value of k.

True feasible range: \(\max(0,r-n)\le k\le\min(m,r)\). The zero convention lets us write the cleaner range 0≤k≤r.

Special case: m=n=N and r=N gives the sum-of-squares identity.

Why the algebra agrees: the coefficient of \(t^r\) in the product comes from exponent pairs k and r−k.
