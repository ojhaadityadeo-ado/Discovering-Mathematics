MATH DETECTIVE: CASE FILE #28

Subject: Darboux’s Theorem — Can a Derivative Jump Over a Slope?

Mission: Prove that a derivative takes every slope between two of its values—even when the derivative is not continuous.

Detective Briefing: Ordinary IVT belongs to continuous functions. A derivative may fail to be continuous, so why can it still not make a jump? We need a proof that uses f itself, not assumed continuity of \(f'\).

Choose an answer before opening its reasoning. The questions and visual labs work offline; equation formatting and display fonts need internet access.

Act 1: Why is ordinary IVT not enough?

Darboux’s theorem

Suppose \(a < b\) and f is differentiable on an open interval containing \([a,b]\). If

\[ f'(a) < \lambda < f'(b), \]

then there is some \(c\in(a,b)\) such that

\[\boxed{f'(c)=\lambda.}\]

The result also holds when the endpoint inequalities are reversed.

The conclusion is called the intermediate value property: between two derivative values, no slope can be skipped.

Clue 1 · We want a point where \(f'(c)=\lambda\). Why can we not simply apply IVT to \(f'\)?

Hint: IVT needs continuity of the function to which it is applied. Which function would that be here?

Open the warning example

Define

\[ f(x)= \begin{cases} x^2\sin(1/x),&x\ne0,\\ 0,&x=0. \end{cases} \]

At 0,

\[ f'(0)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{h^2\sin(1/h)}{h} =\lim_{h\to0}h\sin(1/h)=0, \]

because \(|h\sin(1/h)|\le |h|\to0\). For \(x\ne0\), the product and chain rules give

\[ f'(x)=2x\sin(1/x)-\cos(1/x). \]

Now take two sequences approaching 0:

\[ x_n=\frac1{2\pi n} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad f'(x_n)=-1, \] \[ y_n=\frac1{(2n+1)\pi} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad f'(y_n)=1. \]

Both sequences tend to 0, but the derivative values tend to different numbers. Thus \(f'\) is not continuous at 0, even though f is differentiable there.

Important: We are not trying to prove that every derivative is continuous. We are proving the weaker—and surprising—fact that every derivative has the intermediate value property.

Act 2: Turn the wanted slope into zero

We want to solve \(f'(c)=\lambda\). Subtract the line whose slope is \(\lambda\):

\[ H(x)=f(x)-\lambda x. \]
Clue 2 · What happens to the target equation after this change?

Hint: Differentiating the line \(\lambda x\) gives the constant slope \(\lambda\).

Open the slope shift
\[ H'(x)=f'(x)-\lambda. \]

Subtracting \(\lambda x\) lowers every slope of f by exactly \(\lambda\). Therefore the slope \(\lambda\) on f becomes the slope 0 on H.

The endpoint inequalities become

\[ H'(a) = f'(a) - \lambda < 0, \qquad H'(b) = f'(b) - \lambda > 0. \]

So H is moving downward just to the right of a and upward just to the left of b. That suggests a low point somewhere between them.

Act 3: Why can neither endpoint be the minimum?

Look inward from a

Since \(H'(a) < 0\), its difference quotients are negative for all sufficiently small nonzero h. Choose a small \(h > 0\), so \(a+h\) lies inside \([a,b]\).

Clue 3 · From \(\dfrac{H(a+h)-H(a)}{h} < 0\) and \(h > 0\), what can we conclude?

Hint: Multiplying a negative quotient by a positive denominator keeps it negative.

Write the sign step
\[ H(a+h)-H(a) < 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad H(a+h) < H(a). \]

There is a point of the interval with a smaller value than \(H(a)\). Therefore a is not an absolute minimum of H on \([a,b]\).

Look inward from b

Since \(H'(b) > 0\), choose a sufficiently small \(h < 0\). Then \(b+h\) lies just to the left of b, inside the interval.

Clue 4 · Now \(\dfrac{H(b+h)-H(b)}{h} > 0\) but \(h < 0\). What immediate sign does the numerator have?

Hint: A positive quotient has numerator and denominator of the same sign.

Do not lose the sign when multiplying

Multiplying an inequality by the negative number h reverses its direction:

\[ \frac{H(b+h)-H(b)}{h} > 0,\quad h < 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad H(b+h)-H(b) < 0. \]

Hence \(H(b+h) < H(b)\). A nearby point has a smaller value than \(H(b)\), so b is not an absolute minimum either.

The signs of \(H'\) at the endpoints do not tell us that H is decreasing or increasing on the entire interval. They give only the small inward steps needed to rule out both endpoints.

Act 4: Where must the minimum occur?

Clue 5 · We need a minimum and then a zero derivative. Which two results finish the proof?

Hint: First guarantee that a minimum exists. Then use the theorem about a differentiable function at an interior extremum.

Complete the proof

H is differentiable on an open interval containing \([a,b]\), so H is continuous on \([a,b]\). By the Extreme Value Theorem, H reaches an absolute minimum somewhere on this closed interval.

Acts 2 and 3 show that neither a nor b can be a minimum. Therefore an absolute minimum occurs at some

\[c\in(a,b).\]

H is differentiable at this interior point. Fermat’s theorem gives

\[ H'(c)=0. \]

But \(H'(c)=f'(c)-\lambda\). Hence

\[ f'(c)-\lambda=0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{f'(c)=\lambda}. \]

This proves Darboux’s theorem for \(f'(a) < \lambda < f'(b)\).

What if the endpoint slopes are reversed?

Clue 6 · Suppose \(f'(a) > \lambda > f'(b)\). What should we change in the proof?

Hint: The new signs are \(H'(a)>0\) and \(H'(b)<0\). Look at which endpoint behavior rules out a maximum.

Open the reversed case

Now \(H'(a)>0\), so H has a larger value just to the right of a; a cannot be an absolute maximum. Also \(H'(b)<0\), and a small negative step from b produces a larger value; b cannot be an absolute maximum.

EVT gives an absolute maximum, it must occur at an interior point c, and Fermat again gives \(H'(c)=0\). Therefore \(f'(c)=\lambda\).

Act 6: Discontinuous does not mean “jump”

Clue 8 · Suppose a derivative is negative at one point and positive at another point of the same interval. Why must it be zero somewhere between them?

Hint: Zero lies between a negative number and a positive number. Which theorem gives intermediate values specifically for derivatives?

State the corrected argument

If \(f'(u) < 0 < f'(v)\) with \(u < v\), apply Darboux’s theorem to f on the interval between u and v, using \(\lambda=0\). It gives some \(c\in(u,v)\) with \(f'(c)=0\).

The conclusion is correct, but saying “IVT applies to \(f'\)” is not justified unless \(f'\) is known to be continuous. Darboux’s theorem supplies exactly the missing reason.

Final report: close the case

\[ \boxed{ f'(a) < \lambda < f'(b) \ \Longrightarrow\ H'(a) < 0 < H'(b) \ \Longrightarrow\ H\text{ has an interior minimum} \ \Longrightarrow\ H'(c)=0 \ \Longrightarrow\ f'(c)=\lambda. } \]
