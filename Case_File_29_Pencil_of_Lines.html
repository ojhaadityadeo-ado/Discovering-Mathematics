MATH DETECTIVE: CASE FILE #29

Subject: Why \(L_1+\lambda L_2=0\) Gives a Pencil of Lines

Mission: Show which common point every member keeps, check that each member is really a line, find the missing line, and prove that the symmetric family is complete.

Detective Briefing: Adding two line equations certainly makes another linear equation—but that alone does not prove everything we want. We must check the common point, rule out a failed equation, prove the converse, and account for one missing direction.

Choose an answer before opening its reasoning. The questions and visual lab work offline; equation formatting and display fonts need internet access.

Act 1: What stays fixed when equations are combined?

Write

\[ f_1(x,y)=A_1x+B_1y+C_1,\qquad f_2(x,y)=A_2x+B_2y+C_2. \]

Assume \(f_1=0\) and \(f_2=0\) are nonparallel lines meeting at \(O\). In particular, each normal \((A_i,B_i)\) is nonzero, and the two normals are not proportional.

Consider the family

\[ \boxed{f_1(x,y)+\lambda f_2(x,y)=0,\qquad \lambda\in\mathbb R.} \]
Clue 1 · What immediate observation shows that every member contains O?

Hint: Substitute O before doing any expansion.

Open the substitution

Because O lies on both original lines,

\[ f_1(O)=0,\qquad f_2(O)=0. \]

Therefore, for every real \(\lambda\),

\[ f_1(O)+\lambda f_2(O)=0+\lambda\cdot0=0. \]

So every member contains O. This is a general useful idea: a linear combination of equations keeps every solution common to the original equations.

Act 2: Is every member really a line?

Expand a member:

\[ (A_1+\lambda A_2)x+ (B_1+\lambda B_2)y+ (C_1+\lambda C_2)=0. \]

This is linear, but an equation describes a line only when its x- and y-coefficients are not both zero.

Clue 2 · Suppose (A_1+lambda A_2=0) and (B_1+lambda B_2=0). What would that tell us?

Hint: Rewrite the two equalities as ((A_1,B_1)=-lambda(A_2,B_2)).

Open the contradiction
\[ (A_1,B_1)=-\lambda(A_2,B_2). \]

The normals would be proportional. Lines with proportional normals have the same direction, so the original lines would be parallel or coincident. They could not be the two nonparallel lines in our setting.

Therefore \((A_1+\lambda A_2,B_1+\lambda B_2)\ne(0,0)\) for every finite real \(\lambda\). Every member is a genuine line, and Act 1 shows that it passes through O.

\[ \boxed{\text{finite family member}\ \Longrightarrow\ \text{line through }O.} \]

Act 3: Have we found every line through O?

Clue 3 · We proved that each family member is a line through O. Why is the proof not finished?

Hint: “Every member has the property” does not automatically mean “everything with the property is a member.”

Separate the two directions
\[ \text{member}\Longrightarrow\text{line through }O \]

is only one implication. To claim that the family is all lines through O, we would also need

\[ \text{line through }O\Longrightarrow\text{member}. \]

That converse needs its own proof—and the finite-\(\lambda\) form actually misses one line.

Clue 4 · Which original line is missing from (f_1+lambda f_2=0) when (lambda) must be finite?

Hint: Set (lambda=0) first. Then ask whether the fixed coefficient 1 can ever become 0.

Check both original lines

At \(\lambda=0\), the equation is \(f_1=0\), so the first original line is present.

To obtain only \(f_2=0\), we would need the coefficient of \(f_1\) to vanish. But that coefficient is always 1. No finite real \(\lambda\) removes it.

The complete symmetric form is

\[ \boxed{\alpha f_1+\beta f_2=0,\qquad(\alpha,\beta)\ne(0,0).} \]

Now \(f_1=0\) appears when \((\alpha,\beta)=(1,0)\), while \(f_2=0\) appears when \((\alpha,\beta)=(0,1)\).

Clue 5 · Why must the pair ((alpha,eta)=(0,0)) be excluded?

Hint: Substitute both parameter values into the symmetric equation.

Open the check

With both parameters zero, every coefficient vanishes and the equation becomes \(0=0\). Every point satisfies it, so it is not a line.

For every nonzero parameter pair, the x- and y-coefficients cannot both vanish: that would give a nontrivial linear relation between the two independent normal vectors. Thus every allowed symmetric member is a genuine line through O.

Also, multiplying \((\alpha,\beta)\) by any nonzero number gives the same geometric line. The parameter pair is not unique as a pair; only its ratio matters.

Act 4: Why is the symmetric family complete?

Let a target line through O be

\[ g(x,y)=Ax+By+C=0,\qquad(A,B)\ne(0,0). \]
Clue 6 · We need to build this target from (f_1) and (f_2). What can we do first?

Hint: Two nonparallel normal vectors form two independent directions in the two-dimensional vector plane.

Follow the coefficient argument

Because \((A_1,B_1)\) and \((A_2,B_2)\) are linearly independent, they form a basis of \(\mathbb R^2\). Therefore there are unique numbers \(\alpha,\beta\) such that

\[ (A,B)=\alpha(A_1,B_1)+\beta(A_2,B_2). \]

The expressions \(g\) and \(\alpha f_1+\beta f_2\) now have the same x- and y-coefficients. Their difference has no x or y term, so it is some constant K:

\[ g(x,y)-\bigl(\alpha f_1(x,y)+\beta f_2(x,y)\bigr)=K. \]

Evaluate at O. The target line and both original lines pass through O, so every expression on the left is zero. Hence \(K=0\), and

\[ \boxed{g=\alpha f_1+\beta f_2.} \]

Thus every line through O occurs in the symmetric family.

If \(\alpha\ne0\), divide the whole equation by \(\alpha\):

\[ f_1+\frac{\beta}{\alpha}f_2=0. \]

This is the finite family with \(\lambda=\beta/\alpha\). If \(\alpha=0\), then \(\beta\ne0\) and the member is \(f_2=0\). Therefore

\[ \boxed{f_1+\lambda f_2=0\text{ gives every line through }O\text{ except }f_2=0.} \]

Act 6: What do books mean by \(\lambda=\infty\)?

Clue 7 · Is (lambda=infty) an ordinary member of the real-parameter family?

Hint: For nonzero λ, divide the entire equation by λ and inspect the coefficients.

Open the limiting calculation

For \(\lambda\ne0\), divide the equation by \(\lambda\):

\[ \frac1\lambda f_1+f_2=0. \]

As \(|\lambda|\to\infty\), the number \(1/\lambda\) tends to 0, so the normalized coefficients approach those of

\[ f_2=0. \]

This explains why the red line in the lab approaches the dashed orange line for large positive or negative \(\lambda\). It never equals \(f_2=0\) at a finite real value. “\(\lambda=\infty\)” is a convenient limiting phrase, not a real parameter substitution.

Why was “nonparallel” essential?

Take the parallel lines

\[ f_1=x,\qquad f_2=x-1. \]
Clue 8 · What happens at (lambda=-1) in (f_1+lambda f_2=0)?

Hint: Compute (x-(x-1)) before setting it equal to zero.

Open the failure
\[ f_1-f_2=x-(x-1)=1. \]

The supposed member is \(1=0\), which has no solution. Here the original normals \((1,0)\) and \((1,0)\) are proportional, so a nontrivial combination can cancel both x- and y-coefficients.

The original parallel lines also have no common point for the family to preserve. Without the nonparallel assumption, even the statement “every member is a line through one fixed point” can fail.
