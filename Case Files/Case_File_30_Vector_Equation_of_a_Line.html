MATH DETECTIVE: CASE FILE #30

Subject: Why \(\mathbf r=\mathbf a+t\mathbf b\) Describes a Straight Line

Mission: Start at a fixed point, move along one fixed direction, and show why this gives exactly a line.

Detective Briefing: Picture a straight rod in a room. One point tells us where it passes, but not which way it points. A direction tells us which way it points, but not where it sits. What happens when we know both?

Choose an answer before opening the reasoning. Questions and the visual lab work offline; equation formatting and display fonts need internet access.

Act 1: What information fixes a line?

Let A be a fixed point and \(\mathbf b\) a fixed direction vector. Let O be the coordinate origin, and P a point we want to locate:

\[\mathbf a=\overrightarrow{OA},\qquad \mathbf r=\overrightarrow{OP}.\]

The letter \(\mathbf b\) denotes a vector, not a point. It must be nonzero.

Clue 1 · What does \(\mathbf b\ne\mathbf0\) mean, and why do we need it?

Hint: A vector can point along an axis and still be nonzero. Think about (0,0,3).

Open the reasoning

A nonzero vector has positive length and a direction. Some of its components may be zero. For example, \((0,0,3)\) points along the z-axis.

If \(\mathbf b=\mathbf0\), then \(\mathbf r=\mathbf a+t\mathbf b=\mathbf a\) for every t. The only point is A. So the nonzero condition is needed to obtain a line rather than one point.

A point fixes position; a nonzero direction fixes orientation. Together they fix a unique line.

Act 2: How do we describe movement from A?

Clue 2 · We want to reach a point P on the line. Which displacement should be a multiple of \(\mathbf b\)?

Hint: The vector r starts at O, but our movement starts at A.

Build the equation

If P is on the line and \(P\ne A\), the displacement \(\overrightarrow{AP}\) is parallel to \(\mathbf b\). Thus it is a scalar multiple \(t\mathbf b\), for some real t. If \(P=A\), choose \(t=0\).

\[ \overrightarrow{AP}=\overrightarrow{OP}-\overrightarrow{OA} =\mathbf r-\mathbf a=t\mathbf b. \]

Add \(\mathbf a\) to both sides:

\[\boxed{\mathbf r=\mathbf a+t\mathbf b.}\]

The equation says: start at A, then add a movement along the fixed direction.

Clue 3 · Every point on the line satisfies the equation. What must we check next?

Hint: To describe exactly a line, an equation must include all its points and no points outside it.

Check the other direction

Suppose \(\mathbf r=\mathbf a+t\mathbf b\). Then \(\overrightarrow{AP}=t\mathbf b\). For \(t\ne0\), this displacement is parallel to the line’s fixed direction, so P lies on the line through A. For \(t=0\), \(P=A\), which is already on the line.

\[ \boxed{P\text{ lies on the line}\iff \mathbf r=\mathbf a+t\mathbf b\text{ for some }t\in\mathbb R.} \]

Because \(\mathbf b\ne\mathbf0\), each point has just one t in this fixed description: if \(\mathbf a+t_1\mathbf b=\mathbf a+t_2\mathbf b\), then \((t_1-t_2)\mathbf b=\mathbf0\), so \(t_1=t_2\).

Act 3: What does the parameter tell us?

Clue 4 · What would we lose if we allowed only t ≥ 0?

Hint: A negative multiple reverses the direction of a vector.

Read the three signs
ParameterMovement from A
\(t>0\)In the direction of \(\mathbf b\).
\(t=0\)No movement: P is A.
\(t<0\)In the opposite direction.

The whole line needs all real values of t. There is no farthest point in either direction.

Clue 5 · For \(\mathbf b=(2,0,3)\) and \(t=-2\), what is the distance AP?

Hint: The vector b has length √(2² + 0² + 3²), not length 1.

Calculate the distance
\[ AP=|\overrightarrow{AP}|=|t\mathbf b|=|t|\,|\mathbf b|, \qquad |(2,0,3)|=\sqrt{13}. \] \[ t=-2\quad\Longrightarrow\quad AP=2\sqrt{13}. \]

The minus sign tells us which way to move. It does not make the distance negative. Only when \(|\mathbf b|=1\) does \(|t|\) equal AP.

This is the distance from A, not the total distance of a journey that goes back and forth.

Can a different direction vector give the same line?

Yes. Compare

\[ \mathbf r=\mathbf a+t\mathbf b, \qquad \mathbf r=\mathbf a+s(5\mathbf b). \]

They give the same point when \(t=5s\). As either parameter ranges over all real numbers, the same entire line is reached. A longer direction vector needs a smaller parameter to reach the same point.

Any nonzero multiple \(k\mathbf b\) works, with \(s=t/k\). If k is negative, the chosen direction reverses. The complete line stays the same.

Changing k keeps P fixed by using \(s=t/k\), so \(s(k\mathbf b)=t\mathbf b\). The range selector always refers to the original t. If \(k<0\), the corresponding s-inequalities reverse.

One useful observation: y stays equal to 2 throughout. A zero y-component of the direction means no change in y, not that the line disappears.

Act 5: Where do the coordinate forms come from?

Write \(\mathbf a=(x_0,y_0,z_0)\) and \(\mathbf b=(l,m,n)\). Equality of vectors means equality of each component:

\[ \boxed{x=x_0+tl,\qquad y=y_0+tm,\qquad z=z_0+tn.} \]

These three equations use the same t. The coordinates cannot be chosen independently.

Clue 6 · We want to remove t. What can we do when l, m and n are all nonzero?

Hint: Each quotient must give the parameter of the same point.

Find the symmetric form
\[ t=\frac{x-x_0}{l}=\frac{y-y_0}{m}=\frac{z-z_0}{n}. \]

Thus

\[ \boxed{\frac{x-x_0}{l}=\frac{y-y_0}{m}=\frac{z-z_0}{n}.} \]

Conversely, if the three quotients are equal, call their common value t. Multiplying by l, m and n recovers the three coordinate equations. So the two forms describe exactly the same points.

Clue 7 · For \(\mathbf b=(2,0,3)\), how should we write the equation without dividing by zero?

Hint: The original equation y = y₀ + t·0 still gives useful information.

Keep the fixed coordinate

The parametric equations are \(x=x_0+2t,\ y=y_0,\ z=z_0+3t\). Only the x- and z-equations can be divided by their direction components. The y-equation remains \(y=y_0\).

For the lab example, the two Cartesian equations are

\[ y=2,\qquad \frac{x-1}{2}=\frac{z+1}{3}, \] \[ \text{or equivalently}\qquad \boxed{y=2,\qquad 3x-2z-5=0.} \]

If two direction components are zero, two coordinates stay fixed. For example, \(\mathbf b=(0,0,3)\) gives \(x=x_0,\ y=y_0\), with z free to range over all real values.

Act 6: How do two points give the direction?

Let A and B be distinct. Write their position vectors as \(\mathbf a\) and \(\mathbf b_0\), so \(\mathbf b_0\) is not confused with the direction vector. Then

\[ \mathbf b=\overrightarrow{AB}=\mathbf b_0-\mathbf a\ne\mathbf0, \qquad \boxed{\mathbf r=\mathbf a+t(\mathbf b_0-\mathbf a).} \]

At \(t=0\) we get A; at \(t=1\) we get B. Between those values, we move from A toward B without passing it.

Values allowed for tPoints reached
\(0\le t\le1\)The closed segment AB, including both endpoints.
\(t\ge0\)The ray starting at A and going through B.
\(t\in\mathbb R\)The complete line through A and B.
Why must the two points be distinct?

If \(A=B\), then \(\mathbf b_0-\mathbf a=\mathbf0\), and the formula stays at A. One point alone does not choose a direction; infinitely many lines can pass through it.

Act 7: Why use two Cartesian equations in space?

Clue 8 · In 3D, what do the equations x = 0 and y = 0 tell us?

Hint: After fixing x and y, can z still vary?

Count the directions still available

The single equation \(x=0\) leaves y and z free: it describes the yz-plane. Adding \(y=0\) leaves only z free: the common points are \((0,0,z)\), the z-axis.

A plane has two independent directions of movement; a line has one. Two independent linear Cartesian equations in three variables describe two nonparallel planes whose intersection is a line.

Not any two equations will do. For example, \(x=0\) and \(2x=0\) are the same condition, so their common points still form a plane. The equations \(x=0\) and \(x=1\) have no common points.

Here we mean linear Cartesian equations. A specially chosen nonlinear equation can describe a line by itself: \(x^2+y^2=0\) also describes the z-axis over the real numbers.

Final report: what does the equation say?

\[ \boxed{\mathbf r=\mathbf a+t\mathbf b,\qquad \mathbf b\ne\mathbf0,\quad t\in\mathbb R.} \]

Start at A. Move a real multiple of one fixed nonzero vector. These are exactly the points of one straight line.
