MATH DETECTIVE: CASE FILE

Subject: Derangements — Count, Correct, and Rebuild

Mission: Count hat assignments in which nobody gets their own hat. Find out why the formula alternates, then build a second proof by turning large assignments into smaller ones.

The case: There are n people and n distinct hats. Person i owns hat i. Give each person exactly one hat and use every hat exactly once. How many assignments leave everyone wearing somebody else’s hat?

A derangement has no fixed matches: no person i receives hat i. Write its count as Dₙ. We use integer n ≥ 0. Work through 12 clues and the small-step questions; the two investigations can be studied separately. First investigation · Why do the factorial terms alternate?

Act 1: Count everything, then find the problem

Record an assignment as a list of hats in person order. For example, [2, 3, 1] means person 1 receives hat 2, person 2 receives hat 3, and person 3 receives hat 1.

Clue 1 · If we ignore the own-hat restriction, how many assignments are there?

Follow this clue: A hat that has already been given away is no longer available.
Open the reasoning

There are n choices for person 1, then n−1 for person 2, continuing down to 1. Thus the unrestricted count is \(n(n-1)\cdots1=n!\). This counts every permutation of the hats once.

Small step 1. For n = 3, is [2, 3, 1] a derangement? What about [1, 3, 2]?

Open the answer
[2, 3, 1] is a derangement: every entry differs from its position. [1, 3, 2] is not: person 1 gets hat 1, even though the other two people avoid their own hats.

To remove the forbidden assignments, focus on one easy condition at a time. Let

\[A_i=\{\text{assignments in which person }i\text{ receives hat }i\}.\]

An assignment is forbidden if it belongs to at least one \(A_i\). So the forbidden set is the union

\[A_1\cup A_2\cup\cdots\cup A_n,\qquad D_n=n!-\left|A_1\cup\cdots\cup A_n\right|.\]

Small step 2. Does membership in \(A_1\) say that person 1 is the only person with their own hat?

Open the answer
No. It requires person 1 to get hat 1, but says nothing about the others. Several own-hat matches may occur in the same assignment.
Why this matters: “Nobody gets their own hat” is difficult to impose directly. “These named people get their own hats” is easy to count. We will combine those simpler counts.

Act 2: Why one subtraction is not enough

Fix one person i with their own hat. The other n−1 hats can be assigned freely, so

\[|A_i|=(n-1)!.\]

There are n choices of i. A first attempt would be \(n!-n(n-1)!\). But the forbidden sets overlap.

Clue 2 · An assignment has exactly two own-hat matches. What happens if we subtract every single bad set once?

Follow this clue: Track this one assignment, not the total number of assignments. How many of the sets Aᵢ contain it?
Open the reasoning

If the matching people are p and q, the assignment lies in both \(A_p\) and \(A_q\). It is counted once initially and subtracted twice: \(1-2=-1\). Adding the intersection \(A_p\cap A_q\) once changes its net weight to zero, as wanted.

Small step 3. Why does this correction have a plus sign?

Open the answer
We subtracted the assignment too many times. The positive pairwise term repairs that over-subtraction. It does not mean that assignments with fixed matches are allowed.

But assignments with three fixed matches belong to three pairwise intersections. They receive \(1-3+3=1\) so far, and need a subtraction for the triple intersection. Larger overlaps continue the same pattern.

\[D_n=n!-\sum_i|A_i|+\sum_{i

This alternating correction is called inclusion–exclusion. We will prove that the corrections finish with the right weight for every assignment, rather than relying on the name alone.

Act 3: Count a specified intersection

Suppose a fixed set of k people must receive their own hats. Remove those k people and those k hats from the remaining task.

Clue 3 · How many completions are allowed in this intersection?

Follow this clue: Read the condition carefully: did it forbid extra own-hat matches?
Open the reasoning

The intersection requires the specified k matches. It does not prohibit other matches. Every permutation of the remaining n−k hats is allowed, giving \((n-k)!\) completions.

These are assignments with at least those specified matches. This is not the same as assignments with exactly k fixed matches.

Small step 4. For n = 4, require people 1 and 2 to get their own hats. Which assignments remain?

Open the answer
[1, 2, 3, 4] and [1, 2, 4, 3]. There are 2! = 2 completions. The first has four fixed matches and is still allowed in this intersection. Using D₂ = 1 would wrongly discard it.
Clue 4 · We know the size of each k-fold intersection. What else must we count?

Follow this clue: An intersection is identified by a set of people. Changing the order of the same people does not create a new intersection.
Open the reasoning

There are \(\binom nk\) different k-person sets, and every corresponding intersection has \((n-k)!\) assignments. So the sum of the intersection sizes is

\[\boxed{\binom nk(n-k)!}.\]

Different intersections can contain the same assignment. That repeated counting is intentional; the alternating signs will correct it.

Small step 5. What does the k = 0 term mean?

Open the answer
Specify no fixed matches. Then every assignment is allowed: \(\binom n0(n-0)!=1\cdot n!=n!\). It is the initial unrestricted count.

Small step 6. What does the k = n term mean?

Open the answer
All people must receive their own hats. There is one assignment. Its count is \(\binom nn0!=1\), using \(0!=1\): there is one way to do nothing with the remaining zero hats.
Why this matters: The factor \(\binom nk\) chooses which matches are imposed. The factor \((n-k)!\) counts the freely assigned hats left over. They answer different questions.

Act 4: Follow one assignment through every correction

Suppose one particular assignment has exactly j fixed matches. It belongs to an intersection specified by k people exactly when all those people are among its j matching people.

Small step 7. How many k-person sets can be chosen from its j matching people?

Open the answer
\(\binom jk\), not \(\binom nk\). Here we are tracking one assignment. Only its actual fixed matches can place it in a bad-set intersection. For k > j it belongs to none.
Clue 5 · If j ≥ 1, what is the assignment’s final weight after all corrections?

Follow this clue: Recognize the binomial expansion of (1−1)ʲ.
Open the reasoning

It starts with weight 1. At level k it contributes \((-1)^k\binom jk\). Since j ≥ 1, the sum is \((1-1)^j=0\). Every forbidden assignment disappears from the final count.

A derangement has j = 0. It is counted once initially and belongs to no bad set at all. Its final weight is 1. We can handle this case directly without asking what a written \(0^0\) means.

Negative intermediate weights are bookkeeping, not negative numbers of actual assignments. Only the final weights tell us which assignments remain counted.

Why this matters: We have now justified the whole inclusion–exclusion count. Each wanted assignment ends with weight 1, and each unwanted assignment ends with weight 0.

Act 5: Why the factorial fractions appear

Substitute the intersection counts into the alternating correction:

\[\begin{aligned} D_n={}&n!-\binom n1(n-1)!+\binom n2(n-2)!\\ &-\cdots+(-1)^n\binom nn0!. \end{aligned}\]

Now simplify one general term. Keep both factorial factors visible until they cancel:

\[\begin{aligned} \binom nk(n-k)! &=\frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}\,(n-k)!\\ &=\frac{n!(n-k)!}{k!(n-k)!}\\ &=\frac{n!}{k!}. \end{aligned}\]

Small step 8. Why is this cancellation valid even at k = n?

Open the answer
The canceled factor is \((n-k)!=0!=1\), not zero. Every factorial here is positive, so the cancellation is valid for \(0\le k\le n\).

Factor n! out of the sum:

\[\boxed{D_n=n!\sum_{k=0}^n\frac{(-1)^k}{k!}}.\]
\[D_n=n!\left(1-\frac1{1!}+\frac1{2!}-\cdots+\frac{(-1)^n}{n!}\right).\]

Small step 9. Why does the first term inside the parentheses equal 1?

Open the answer
At k = 0, \((-1)^0/0!=1/1=1\). It represents the unrestricted n! assignments.

For n = 4, write out every contribution before adding:

\[\begin{aligned} D_4&=4!-\binom41 3!+\binom42 2!-\binom43 1!+\binom44 0!\\ &=24-4\cdot6+6\cdot2-4\cdot1+1\cdot1\\ &=24-24+12-4+1\\ &=0+12-4+1=9. \end{aligned}\]

The intermediate zero \(24-24=0\) is not the answer. The overlap corrections still have to be included.

What happens at the smallest values?

\(D_0=1\): there is one assignment of no hats to no people, the empty assignment. It has no fixed matches. \(D_1=0\): the only person must receive their own hat.

Now change the question: how many assignments have exactly k fixed matches?

Clue 6 · After choosing the k matching people, what must happen among the rest?

Follow this clue: The word exactly rules out every additional fixed match.
Open the reasoning

Choose the exact set of k fixed positions in \(\binom nk\) ways. Remove those people and hats. The rest must avoid their own hats, giving \(D_{n-k}\) possibilities.

\[\boxed{\#\{\text{exactly k fixed matches}\}=\binom nkD_{n-k}.}\]

Each assignment has one definite set of fixed positions, so different choices of that set do not overlap.

Small step 10. Can an assignment have exactly n−1 fixed matches, when n ≥ 1?

Open the answer
No. The last person must receive the last remaining hat, which is their own. The formula agrees: \(\binom n{n-1}D_1=0\).
Why this matters: “Specified matches” gives a factorial. “Exactly these matches” gives a derangement count for the rest. The wording determines the count.
Second investigation · Why do two smaller derangement counts appear?

Act 6: Fix the first person’s hat

For this proof assume n ≥ 2. We will build a count directly from smaller derangements, without using the alternating formula.

Clue 7 · In a derangement, how many choices does person 1 have for their hat?

Follow this clue: The hat given to person 1 must differ from their own label.
Open the reasoning

Person 1 can receive any hat j with \(j\ne1\), giving n−1 choices. Fix one such j while we count the rest. We will multiply by n−1 only after finishing the count for this fixed j.

Write \(i\to r\) to mean “person i receives hat r.” We now have \(1\to j\). Split the assignments into two cases:

Case A
Person j receives hat 1.
\(1\to j,\ j\to1\).
Case B
Person j does not receive hat 1.
Some other person k holds hat 1.

Small step 11. Why do these two cases cover everything without overlap?

Open the answer
Person j either receives hat 1 or does not. An assignment cannot satisfy both conditions, and there is no third possibility.
Why this matters: The split is based on one definite yes-or-no fact. This lets us add the two case counts without another inclusion–exclusion correction.

Act 7: An exchange removes two people

In Case A, persons 1 and j exchange hats. Remove those two people and hats 1 and j.

Clue 8 · What is the exact counting problem that remains?

Follow this clue: The original assignment was a derangement. Does removing the exchanged pair change the restrictions on everyone else?
Open the reasoning

Every remaining person must still avoid their own hat. Their matching labels are still present, so the smaller problem is a derangement of n−2 objects. The labels need not be consecutive; relabeling each remaining person and their own hat together does not change the count.

Conversely, take any derangement on those remaining labels and add \(1\to j\) and \(j\to1\). This gives exactly one Case A assignment. The two operations undo each other, so this case contributes \(D_{n-2}\).

Small step 12. When n = 2, what remains after removing the exchanged pair?

Open the answer
No people and no hats. There is one empty assignment, counted by \(D_0=1\). So the exchange is one valid derangement, not zero possibilities.

Act 8: Remove one person—but repair the assignment

Now consider Case B: \(1\to j\), but \(j\not\to1\). We want to connect these assignments with derangements of n−1 objects. A simple deletion is not enough.

Clue 9 · Why can’t we just erase person 1 and declare the remaining assignment finished?

Follow this clue: We want both the people and the hats left over to have labels 2,…,n.
Open the reasoning

Person 1 holds hat j. Removing them with that hat leaves the wrong collection of hats. We need to remove hat 1 instead and transfer hat j to the person who was holding hat 1.

Let k be the person holding hat 1, so \(k\to1\). The part that changes is

\[k\to1,\qquad1\to j.\]
Clue 10 · Which facts ensure that k can safely receive hat j?

Follow this clue: Use the fixed choice 1→j and the defining condition j↛1 separately.
Open the reasoning

We have \(k\ne1\) and \(k\ne j\). Remove person 1 and hat 1. Give hat j, freed by removing person 1, to person k. Leave every other assignment unchanged.

\[k\to1,\ 1\to j\quad\longmapsto\quad k\to j.\]

Only person k changes hats, and their new hat j is not their own because \(k\ne j\). Everyone else still avoids their own hat.

Small step 13. Are all remaining hats still used exactly once?

Open the answer
Yes. Hat 1 disappears. Hat j moves from the removed person 1 to person k. Every other hat stays with its previous holder. Nothing is duplicated or lost from hats 2,…,n.

Small step 14. Why is proving that the smaller assignment is valid not yet enough to claim the count is Dₙ₋₁?

Open the answer
Different original assignments might lead to the same smaller assignment, or some smaller assignments might never arise. We need a reverse construction to show that every smaller derangement has exactly one partner.

Now run the construction backward

Keep the same j fixed. Start with any derangement of people and hats \(2,\ldots,n\).

Clue 11 · How can we find the one person whose hat must change when we restore person 1?

Follow this clue: Each hat is used once. In a smaller derangement, can person j be holding hat j?
Open the reasoning

The holder k of hat j is unique, and \(k\ne j\). Give k the new hat 1. Introduce person 1 with hat j. Everyone else keeps their hat.

\[k\to j\quad\longmapsto\quad k\to1,\ 1\to j.\]

Person 1 avoids hat 1 because \(j\ne1\). Person k avoids their own hat because \(k\ne1\). Person j does not get hat 1 because \(k\ne j\). Thus the restored assignment belongs to Case B.

This reverses the deletion exactly. Therefore, for our fixed j, Case B has exactly \(D_{n-1}\) assignments.

Small step 15. If we forget which j was fixed, does the smaller assignment alone identify the original one?

Open the answer
No. The reverse construction needs j to locate the holder of hat j. Our correspondence is for each fixed j. We account for the n−1 possible values of j later.
Why this matters: The factor \(D_{n-1}\) comes from a reversible pairing, not from a guess based on the number of people left.

Act 9: Remove and restore real assignments

In the example \(1\to2,\ 2\to3,\ 3\to4,\ 4\to1\), we have j = 2 and k = 4. This is Case B. Replace \(4\to1,\ 1\to2\) by \(4\to2\):

\[2\to3,\qquad3\to4,\qquad4\to2.\]

The result is a derangement of the remaining three labels. Reversing the change restores the original four-person assignment.

Act 10: Put the pieces together

For each fixed j, the cases are disjoint and give

\[D_{n-2}+D_{n-1}.\]
Clue 12 · Why do we multiply this sum by n−1 without an overlap correction?

Follow this clue: One person cannot receive two different hats in the same assignment.
Open the reasoning

Every derangement has exactly one hat j at person 1, with \(j\ne1\). Different choices of j give disjoint classes, and the count in each class is the same. Therefore

\[\boxed{D_n=(n-1)(D_{n-1}+D_{n-2}),\qquad n\ge2.}\]

Small step 16. Why does the recurrence start at n = 2?

Open the answer
At n = 1 it would refer to \(D_{-1}\), outside our counting problem. We start with \(D_0=1\) and \(D_1=0\), then use the recurrence for n ≥ 2.

Use the initial values to compute each count:

\[\begin{aligned} D_2&=(2-1)(D_1+D_0)=1(0+1)=1,\\ D_3&=(3-1)(D_2+D_1)=2(1+0)=2,\\ D_4&=(4-1)(D_3+D_2)=3(2+1)=9,\\ D_5&=(5-1)(D_4+D_3)=4(9+2)=44. \end{aligned}\]

Small step 17. For n = 3 and a fixed j, why does Case A contribute zero?

Open the answer
Removing an exchanged pair leaves one person with their own hat, which cannot be a derangement. So Case A contributes D₁ = 0. Case B contributes D₂ = 1 for each j, giving 2 derangements overall.

Can the alternating formula confirm the recurrence?

For n ≥ 1, separate the final term of the explicit formula:

\[\begin{aligned} D_n &=n!\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{(-1)^k}{k!} +n!\frac{(-1)^n}{n!}\\ &=n\left((n-1)!\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{(-1)^k}{k!}\right)+(-1)^n\\ &=nD_{n-1}+(-1)^n. \end{aligned}\]

Small step 18. Which part is nDₙ₋₁, and why is the leftover just (−1)ⁿ?

Open the answer
The sum ending at n−1, multiplied by \((n-1)!\), is \(D_{n-1}\); the extra factor is n. In the last term, \(n!/n!=1\), leaving only \((-1)^n\).

For n ≥ 2, apply the same identity with n−1 in place of n:

\[\begin{aligned} D_{n-1}&=(n-1)D_{n-2}+(-1)^{n-1},\\ (n-1)D_{n-2}&=D_{n-1}-(-1)^{n-1}. \end{aligned}\]

Substitute this into the proposed recurrence’s right side:

\[\begin{aligned} (n-1)(D_{n-1}+D_{n-2}) &=(n-1)D_{n-1}+(n-1)D_{n-2}\\ &=(n-1)D_{n-1}+D_{n-1}-(-1)^{n-1}\\ &=nD_{n-1}+(-1)^n\\ &=D_n. \end{aligned}\]

Small step 19. Why did the sign change in the third line?

Open the answer
Multiplying \((-1)^{n-1}\) by −1 gives \((-1)^n\). Hence \(-(-1)^{n-1}=(-1)^n\).
Why this matters: The algebra checks that the two answers agree. The hat argument explains why the recurrence has two smaller counts and a factor of n−1.

Final case report

Count and correct:

\[\boxed{D_n=n!\sum_{k=0}^n\frac{(-1)^k}{k!},\qquad n\ge0.}\]

Each forbidden assignment ends with weight 0; each derangement ends with weight 1.

Remove and rebuild:

\[\boxed{D_n=(n-1)(D_{n-1}+D_{n-2}),\qquad n\ge2.}\]

Fix person 1’s hat. An exchange removes two labels; a reversible transfer removes one.

Starting values: \(D_0=1,\ D_1=0\).

Exactly k fixed matches: \(\binom nkD_{n-k}\), for \(0\le k\le n\).

The central questions are: “What is allowed among the remaining hats?” and “Can we undo the counting construction uniquely?”
