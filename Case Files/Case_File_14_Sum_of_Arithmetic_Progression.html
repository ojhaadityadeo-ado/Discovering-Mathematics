MATH DETECTIVE: CASE FILE #14

Subject: Sum of an Arithmetic Progression

Mission: Derive and use \(S_n=\frac{n}{2}[2a+(n-1)d]=\frac{n}{2}(a+l)\) by uncovering why the terms pair so neatly.

Detective Briefing: An arithmetic progression has a constant difference between consecutive terms. Your job is not merely to memorize the sum formula. You will reconstruct it from the evidence, test its logic, and apply it to close a numerical case.

Act 1: Identify the Progression

Examine the sequence \(a,\ a+d,\ a+2d,\ldots,\ a+(n-1)d\). What kind of sequence is it?

🕵️ Detective's Note: Subtract each term from the one that follows it. What remains unchanged?

What is the common difference between consecutive terms?

🕵️ Detective's Note: Compute \((a+d)-a\) and \((a+2d)-(a+d)\).

Select the correct expression for the \(n\)th term, \(T_n\):

🕵️ Detective's Note: Reaching the first term requires zero jumps; reaching the nth term requires how many jumps?

If the last term is called \(l\), complete the evidence record:

\(l=\) .

🕵️ Detective's Note: The last term is also the nth term.

Explain why the nth-term formula contains \(n-1\), not \(n\).

Act 2: The Pairing Trail

Let \(S_n\) be the sum of the first \(n\) terms. Which line records the sum in forward order?

🕵️ Detective's Note: A sum uses addition signs and includes every term of the progression.
Evidence: Two Views of the Same Sum
\[ \begin{aligned} S_n&=a+(a+d)+(a+2d)+\cdots+(l-d)+l,\\ S_n&=l+(l-d)+(l-2d)+\cdots+(a+d)+a. \end{aligned} \] We may write the second line in reverse order because the progression contains a finite number of terms—exactly \(n\). It is the same finite sum: no term is added or removed, and the order of finitely many addends may be changed without changing their total.

In the reversed line, which term appears first?

🕵️ Detective's Note: Reversing a list puts its final entry first.

Add the two lines vertically. What is the sum of every aligned pair?

🕵️ Detective's Note: Check the outside pair \(a+l\), then the next pair \((a+d)+(l-d)\).

There is one aligned pair for each term. How many equal pair sums appear?

There are pairs.

🕵️ Detective's Note: Both copies of the sum contain exactly n positions.
The Pairing Written Explicitly
Adding the two finite sums column by column gives \[ \begin{aligned} 2S_n &=(a+l)+\bigl[(a+d)+(l-d)\bigr]+\cdots+\bigl[(l-d)+(a+d)\bigr]+(l+a)\\ &=\underbrace{(a+l)+(a+l)+\cdots+(a+l)}_{n\text{ times}}\\ &=n(a+l). \end{aligned} \]

Which equation summarizes the explicit pairing above?

🕵️ Detective's Note: The left side contains two copies of the same sum; the right side contains n identical pair sums.

Divide both sides by 2. Which formula is revealed?

🕵️ Detective's Note: Solve \(2S_n=n(a+l)\) for \(S_n\).

Therefore, the sum equals the number of terms multiplied by the average of the first and last terms: \[ \boxed{S_n=\frac{n}{2}(a+l)}. \]

Act 3: Expose the Standard Formula

Sometimes the last term \(l\) is not given. Which expression should replace it?

🕵️ Detective's Note: Reopen the nth-term evidence from Act 1.

Simplify \(a+[a+(n-1)d]\). What does the bracket become?

🕵️ Detective's Note: Combine the two copies of a; the d-term appears once.
Formula Translation
\[ S_n=\frac{n}{2}(a+l) =\frac{n}{2}\bigl[a+a+(n-1)d\bigr] =\boxed{\frac{n}{2}\bigl[2a+(n-1)d\bigr]}. \] Use \(\frac{n}{2}(a+l)\) when the first and last terms are known. Use \(\frac{n}{2}[2a+(n-1)d]\) when the first term, common difference, and number of terms are known.

Does the two-copy pairing argument require \(n\) to be even?

🕵️ Detective's Note: We paired two complete copies of the list position by position, so no term is left unpaired.

For the first \(n\) odd numbers, \(1,3,5,\ldots,2n-1\), we have \(a=1\) and \(d=2\). Use the formula to complete the identity:

\(1+3+5+\cdots+(2n-1)=\) .

🕵️ Detective's Note: Simplify \(\frac{n}{2}[2+(n-1)2]\).

Final Report: Close the Case

The two equivalent formulas uncovered by the investigation are \[ \boxed{S_n=\frac{n}{2}(a+l)} \qquad\text{and}\qquad \boxed{S_n=\frac{n}{2}[2a+(n-1)d]}. \]

Proof Skeleton to Remember
Write the same arithmetic-progression sum once forwards and once backwards. Each aligned pair is \(a+l\), and there are \(n\) pairs across the two copies. Thus \(2S_n=n(a+l)\), so \(S_n=\frac{n}{2}(a+l)\). Substitute \(l=a+(n-1)d\) to obtain the standard formula.
