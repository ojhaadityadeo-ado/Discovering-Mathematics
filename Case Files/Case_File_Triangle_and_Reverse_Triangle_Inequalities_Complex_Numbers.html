MATH DETECTIVE: CASE FILE

Subject: Triangle and Reverse-Triangle Inequalities for Complex Numbers

Mission: Treat complex numbers as planar displacements, uncover the angle hidden inside \(z_1\overline{z_2}\), and prove the two-sided bound \(\bigl||z_1|-|z_2|\bigr|\le |z_1\pm z_2|\le |z_1|+|z_2|\), including the precise equality cases.

The case: Two displacements have fixed lengths but may point in different directions. How far from the origin can their sum—or their difference—possibly reach? The answer is controlled entirely by alignment and cancellation.

Follow the clues in order. Predict each next move before opening the reasoning. Use the dynamic vector reference to test your geometric predictions; the proof itself still comes from the complex-number algebra.

Act 1: Translate modulus into geometry

Write

\[z_1=x_1+iy_1,\qquad z_2=x_2+iy_2.\]

Each complex number can be viewed as a vector in the plane:

\[z_1\leftrightarrow(x_1,y_1),\qquad z_2\leftrightarrow(x_2,y_2).\]

Its modulus is its Euclidean length:

\[|z_j|=\sqrt{x_j^2+y_j^2}.\]
Clue 1 · If the lengths are fixed, which feature can still change the resultant length?

Follow this clue: Keep both lengths unchanged and imagine rotating one vector.

Small step 1. What should you expect when the vectors point in exactly the same direction?

Open the answer

Their lengths reinforce completely, so the sum should have length \(|z_1|+|z_2|\). This suggests the upper bound.

Small step 2. What should you expect when they point in opposite directions?

Open the answer

The shorter displacement cancels part of the longer one, leaving a length \(\bigl||z_1|-|z_2|\bigr|\). This suggests the lower bound.

Act 2: Find the algebraic quantity that remembers the angle

Multiply one complex number by the conjugate of the other:

\[ \begin{aligned} z_1\overline{z_2} &=(x_1+iy_1)(x_2-iy_2)\\ &=(x_1x_2+y_1y_2)+i(y_1x_2-x_1y_2). \end{aligned} \]

Therefore

\[\operatorname{Re}(z_1\overline{z_2})=x_1x_2+y_1y_2.\]

This is exactly the dot product of the corresponding plane vectors.

Clue 2 · If the angle between nonzero vectors is \(\theta\), what does the real part become?

Follow this clue: The real part is the ordinary dot product.
\[\boxed{\operatorname{Re}(z_1\overline{z_2})=|z_1||z_2|\cos\theta.}\]

Small step 3. What range can this real part have if the two lengths are fixed?

Open the answer

Since \(-1\le\cos\theta\le1\), we get \(-|z_1||z_2|\le\operatorname{Re}(z_1\overline{z_2})\le|z_1||z_2|\).

Act 3: Why square the modulus?

The modulus contains a square root. Can we expose the angle more cleanly by squaring first?

Clue 3 · Which identity makes \(|z_1+z_2|^2\) easy to expand?

Follow this clue: Multiplying a complex number by its conjugate removes its imaginary part.
Step 1 · Expand the squared modulus.
\[ \begin{aligned} |z_1+z_2|^2 &=(z_1+z_2)(\overline{z_1}+\overline{z_2})\\ &=z_1\overline{z_1}+z_1\overline{z_2}+z_2\overline{z_1}+z_2\overline{z_2}. \end{aligned} \]

Small step 4. Which two terms immediately become squared lengths?

Open the answer

\(z_1\overline{z_1}=|z_1|^2\) and \(z_2\overline{z_2}=|z_2|^2\).

Step 2 · Combine the cross terms.

The numbers \(z_1\overline{z_2}\) and \(z_2\overline{z_1}\) are conjugates, so their sum is twice the real part:

\[z_1\overline{z_2}+z_2\overline{z_1}=2\operatorname{Re}(z_1\overline{z_2}).\]

Hence

\[ |z_1+z_2|^2=|z_1|^2+|z_2|^2+2\operatorname{Re}(z_1\overline{z_2}). \]

Small step 5. What is the most useful substitution now?

Open the answer

Replace the real part by \(|z_1||z_2|\cos\theta\). That turns the expression into the cosine-rule form.

\[ \boxed{|z_1+z_2|^2=|z_1|^2+|z_2|^2+2|z_1||z_2|\cos\theta.} \]

Observation: This is exactly the cosine rule written in complex-number language. The whole problem has now been reduced to the simple bound \(-1\le\cos\theta\le1\).

Act 4: Force the upper bound

Since

\[\cos\theta\le1,\]

we get

\[ \begin{aligned} |z_1+z_2|^2 &\le |z_1|^2+|z_2|^2+2|z_1||z_2|\\ &=(|z_1|+|z_2|)^2. \end{aligned} \]
Clue 4 · What is the immediate next step?

Follow this clue: Both sides are squares of nonnegative real quantities.

Thus

\[\boxed{|z_1+z_2|\le|z_1|+|z_2|}.\]

Small step 6. Why is taking square roots legitimate here?

Open the answer

Both \(|z_1+z_2|\) and \(|z_1|+|z_2|\) are nonnegative. The square-root function is increasing on nonnegative numbers.

Small step 7. What does the inequality say about two successive physical displacements?

Open the answer

Your straight-line distance from the starting point cannot exceed the total distance travelled along the two displacement legs.

Act 5: Force the reverse-triangle lower bound

Now use the other extreme:

\[\cos\theta\ge-1.\]

Then

\[ \begin{aligned} |z_1+z_2|^2 &\ge |z_1|^2+|z_2|^2-2|z_1||z_2|\\ &=(|z_1|-|z_2|)^2. \end{aligned} \]
Clue 5 · What does the square root of \((|z_1|-|z_2|)^2\) equal?

Follow this clue: For any real number \(u\), \(\sqrt{u^2}=|u|\).

Therefore

\[\boxed{|z_1+z_2|\ge\bigl||z_1|-|z_2|\bigr|}.\]

Small step 8. Why can the inner difference not be left without absolute-value bars?

Open the answer

If \(|z_2|>|z_1|\), then \(|z_1|-|z_2|<0\). The square root of its square is the nonnegative magnitude of the difference, not the signed difference itself.

Two-sided result for the sum:

\[\boxed{\bigl||z_1|-|z_2|\bigr|\le|z_1+z_2|\le|z_1|+|z_2|}.\]

Act 6: Turn the sum result into the subtraction result

Notice

\[z_1-z_2=z_1+(-z_2).\]
Clue 6 · Which fact lets us reuse the sum inequality unchanged?

Follow this clue: Reversing a vector changes its direction, not its length.

Apply the sum inequality to \(z_1\) and \(-z_2\):

\[ \bigl||z_1|-|-z_2|\bigr| \le |z_1+(-z_2)| \le |z_1|+|-z_2|. \]

Since \(|-z_2|=|z_2|\),

\[\boxed{\bigl||z_1|-|z_2|\bigr|\le|z_1-z_2|\le|z_1|+|z_2|}.\]

Small step 9. If \(z_1,z_2\) are viewed as points, what does \(|z_1-z_2|\) mean?

Open the answer

It is the Euclidean distance between the two points in the complex plane.

Act 7: Equality — where are the extreme cases?

For nonzero \(z_1,z_2\), equality comes from the two extreme cosine values:

\[\cos\theta=1\quad\text{or}\quad\cos\theta=-1.\]
Clue 7 · For \(|z_1+z_2|=|z_1|+|z_2|\), how must the vectors point?

Follow this clue: Upper equality came from replacing \(\cos\theta\) by its largest possible value.
ExpressionUpper-bound equalityLower-bound equality
\(|z_1+z_2|\)Same direction: \(z_2=\lambda z_1\) with \(\lambda\ge0\)Opposite directions: \(z_2=\lambda z_1\) with \(\lambda\le0\)
\(|z_1-z_2|\)Opposite directions: \(z_2=\lambda z_1\) with \(\lambda\le0\)Same direction: \(z_2=\lambda z_1\) with \(\lambda\ge0\)

If either complex number is zero, the upper and lower bounds coincide automatically.

Small step 10. Does upper equality for the sum require \(|z_1|=|z_2|\)?

Open the answer

No. Only the directions must agree. One vector may be longer than the other.

Small step 11. When does complete cancellation \(z_1+z_2=0\) occur?

Open the answer

Exactly when \(z_2=-z_1\). Opposite direction alone gives the lower-bound configuration; equal lengths are additionally needed for the lower bound itself to be zero.

Act 8: Recover the reverse inequality from the triangle inequality alone

There is another route that reveals an important logical relationship.

Start by rewriting \(z_1\):

\[z_1=(z_1+z_2)-z_2.\]

Apply the triangle inequality:

\[ |z_1| =|(z_1+z_2)-z_2| \le |z_1+z_2|+|z_2|. \]

Small step 12. What inequality appears after moving \(|z_2|\) to the left?

Open the answer

\(|z_1|-|z_2|\le|z_1+z_2|\).

Interchange \(z_1\) and \(z_2\):

\[|z_2|-|z_1|\le|z_1+z_2|.\]
Clue 8 · What do these two inequalities combine into?

Follow this clue: The absolute value of a real difference is whichever of the two signed differences is nonnegative.

Deduction: The reverse-triangle inequality is already contained inside the triangle inequality. Once the upper bound is known, the lower bound can be recovered by rewriting one vector as a difference and applying the same upper bound again.

Act 9: Stress-test the bounds

Same direction

Take \(z_1=2\) and \(z_2=5\). Then

\[|z_1+z_2|=7=2+5.\]

The upper bound is exact.

Opposite directions

Take \(z_1=2\) and \(z_2=-5\). Then

\[|z_1+z_2|=|-3|=3=|5-2|.\]

The lower bound is exact.

Perpendicular directions

Take \(z_1=3\) and \(z_2=4i\). Then

\[|z_1+z_2|=|3+4i|=5.\]

And indeed

\[1=|4-3|\le5\le7=3+4.\]

Small step 13. Why is the perpendicular example strictly between the two extremes?

Open the answer

Because \(\cos\theta=0\), which lies strictly between \(-1\) and \(1\). Neither complete reinforcement nor maximum cancellation occurs.

Final case report

For all complex numbers \(z_1,z_2\),

\[ \boxed{\bigl||z_1|-|z_2|\bigr|\le|z_1\pm z_2|\le|z_1|+|z_2|}. \]

The key algebra: \(|z_1+z_2|^2=|z_1|^2+|z_2|^2+2\operatorname{Re}(z_1\overline{z_2})\).

The hidden geometry: \(\operatorname{Re}(z_1\overline{z_2})=|z_1||z_2|\cos\theta\).

The upper extreme: complete alignment gives maximum reinforcement.

The lower extreme: opposite alignment gives maximum cancellation.

The subtraction case: replace \(z_2\) by \(-z_2\).

The reverse inequality: it can also be derived directly from the ordinary triangle inequality.
