MATH DETECTIVE: CASE FILE #8

Subject: Proving Mathematical Induction from the Well-Ordering Principle

Mission: Prove the Principle of Mathematical Induction by assuming a counterexample exists and then using the Well-Ordering Principle to hunt down the first one.

Detective Briefing: For this case, take \(\mathbb N=\{1,2,3,\ldots\}\). The proof is by contradiction. We will suppose induction fails, collect all failures into one set, use the Well-Ordering Principle to choose the smallest failure, and then show that this smallest failure cannot actually fail.

Act 1: Know the Two Rules

Well-Ordering Principle (WOP)
Every non-empty subset of the natural numbers has a least element.

Before using WOP, what two facts must we know about the set we want to apply it to?

🕵️ Detective's Note: Read the hypothesis of the Well-Ordering Principle carefully.
Principle of Mathematical Induction (PMI)
Let \(P(n)\) be a statement about natural numbers. Suppose:
  1. Base step: \(P(1)\) is true.
  2. Inductive step: whenever \(P(k)\) is true, \(P(k+1)\) is true.
Then PMI says that \(P(n)\) is true for every \(n\ge1\).

What are we trying to prove from these two hypotheses?

🕵️ Detective's Note: State the conclusion of mathematical induction.

Act 2: Assume There Is a Failure

We prove PMI by contradiction. So we assume that the two induction hypotheses are true, but the final conclusion is false.

What does it mean for the final conclusion to be false?

🕵️ Detective's Note: Negating “true for every \(n\)” means there is at least one counterexample.
Collect every counterexample into one set: \[ S=\{n\in\mathbb N\mid P(n)\text{ is false}\}. \]

Why is \(S\) non-empty?

🕵️ Detective's Note: The contradiction assumption itself gives us an element of \(S\).

And why is \(S\) a subset of \(\mathbb N\)?

🕵️ Detective's Note: Look at the condition \(n\in\mathbb N\) in the definition of \(S\).

Act 3: Find the First Counterexample

Now we know that \(S\) is a non-empty subset of \(\mathbb N\). Therefore the Well-Ordering Principle applies.

What does WOP give us?

Let the least element be \[ k_0=\min(S). \] So \(k_0\in S\), and no smaller natural number belongs to \(S\).

Because \(k_0\in S\), what do we know about \(P(k_0)\)?

🕵️ Detective's Note: Membership in \(S\) means being a counterexample.

Can \(k_0=1\)?

🕵️ Detective's Note: WOP gives a least element of \(S\), not necessarily the number 1.

Act 5: The Counterexample Destroys Itself

The inductive step says: \[ P(k)\text{ true}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad P(k+1)\text{ true}. \] We have just proved that \(P(k_0-1)\) is true.

🕵️ Detective's Note: We want \(k+1\) to become \(k_0\).

Act 6: Close the Case

If our assumption that counterexamples exist is false, what must be true about \(S\)?

🕵️ Detective's Note: \(S\) was defined to contain every counterexample.

And \(S=\varnothing\) means:

🕵️ Detective's Note: An empty counterexample set means there is no natural number where \(P(n)\) fails.
The Whole Proof in One Chain
Assume a counterexample exists \(\Rightarrow S\neq\varnothing\) \(\Rightarrow\) WOP gives a least counterexample \(k_0\) \(\Rightarrow k_0>1\) \(\Rightarrow k_0-1\notin S\) \(\Rightarrow P(k_0-1)\) is true \(\Rightarrow P(k_0)\) is true by the inductive step \(\Rightarrow\) contradiction, because \(k_0\in S\).

Case Closed: Why Induction Works

The Well-Ordering Principle says that if any counterexample exists, then there must be a first counterexample.

The base case makes sure that this first counterexample cannot be 1.

The inductive step says that once the statement is true one step before that first counterexample, it must also be true at the counterexample itself.

So a first counterexample cannot exist. Hence there are no counterexamples at all.

This is the connection between WOP and induction: a supposed first failure is impossible because the truth immediately before it forces truth at that very point.
