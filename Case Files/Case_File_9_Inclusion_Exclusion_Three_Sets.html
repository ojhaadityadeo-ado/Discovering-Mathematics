MATH DETECTIVE: CASE FILE #9

Subject: Principle of Inclusion-Exclusion for Three Sets

Mission: Derive the three-set counting formula using only the two-set formula and familiar set laws.

Detective Briefing: For a finite set $S$, let $n(S)$ mean the number of elements in $S$. We already know \[ n(X\cup Y)=n(X)+n(Y)-n(X\cap Y). \] Our goal is to prove the corresponding formula for three sets $A,B,C$.

Act 1: Start with What We Already Know

Known Formula
For two finite sets, \[ n(X\cup Y)=n(X)+n(Y)-n(X\cap Y). \]

Why do we subtract $n(X\cap Y)$?

🕵️ Detective's Note: An element in both $X$ and $Y$ appears once in $n(X)$ and once again in $n(Y)$.

To handle three sets, what is the useful first move?

🕵️ Detective's Note: We know a formula for two sets, so make the three-set problem look like a two-set problem.
Let \[ X=A\cup B. \] Then \[ A\cup B\cup C=X\cup C. \]

Act 2: Apply the Two-Set Formula Once

Apply the two-set formula to $X$ and $C$:

🕵️ Detective's Note: Use the same formula from Act 1 with $Y=C$.
Substituting $X=A\cup B$ gives \[ n(A\cup B\cup C)=n(A\cup B)+n(C)-n((A\cup B)\cap C). \]

Now expand $n(A\cup B)$:

🕵️ Detective's Note: This is exactly the two-set formula again.
So we now have \[ n(A\cup B\cup C) =n(A)+n(B)-n(A\cap B)+n(C)-n((A\cup B)\cap C). \]

Act 3: Investigate the Difficult Intersection

The only complicated term left is

\[ (A\cup B)\cap C. \]

Which set law lets us rewrite it?

🕵️ Detective's Note: We need to distribute the intersection with $C$ across the union.

Complete the distributive step:

🕵️ Detective's Note: $C$ must meet both branches: $A\cap C$ and $B\cap C$.
Therefore \[ n((A\cup B)\cap C)=n((A\cap C)\cup(B\cap C)). \]

What should we do next?

🕵️ Detective's Note: We again see a union of two sets.

Act 4: Expand That Union

Set \[ U=A\cap C,\qquad V=B\cap C. \] Then \[ n(U\cup V)=n(U)+n(V)-n(U\cap V). \]

So $n((A\cap C)\cup(B\cap C))$ becomes:

🕵️ Detective's Note: Apply the same two-set formula exactly as written.

Now simplify the intersection

\[ (A\cap C)\cap(B\cap C). \]
🕵️ Detective's Note: To be in both $A\cap C$ and $B\cap C$, an element must be in $A$, $B$, and $C$.
Hence \[ n((A\cup B)\cap C) =n(A\cap C)+n(B\cap C)-n(A\cap B\cap C). \]

Act 5: Put Everything Together

Earlier we reached \[ n(A\cup B\cup C) =n(A)+n(B)-n(A\cap B)+n(C)-n((A\cup B)\cap C). \] Now substitute the expression we just found for $n((A\cup B)\cap C)$.
\[ \begin{aligned} n(A\cup B\cup C) &=n(A)+n(B)-n(A\cap B)+n(C)\\ &\quad-[n(A\cap C)+n(B\cap C)-n(A\cap B\cap C)]. \end{aligned} \]

What happens when the negative sign is distributed across the bracket?

🕵️ Detective's Note: $-[p+q-r]=-p-q+r$.
Therefore \[ \boxed{ n(A\cup B\cup C) =n(A)+n(B)+n(C) -n(A\cap B)-n(B\cap C)-n(C\cap A) +n(A\cap B\cap C) } \]

Which sign does the triple-intersection term finally have?

🕵️ Detective's Note: The triple-intersection term was inside a bracket with a minus in front of the bracket and already had a minus sign inside.

What is the pattern of signs in the final formula?

🕵️ Detective's Note: Read the signs from left to right in the boxed formula.

Why the Formula Makes Sense

The algebra above proves the formula. Here is a quick way to understand why the signs are right.

If an element is in exactly one set: it is counted once in $n(A)+n(B)+n(C)$ and is never subtracted. So its final count is \[ 1. \]
If an element is in exactly two sets: it is counted twice at first, then subtracted once in the corresponding pairwise intersection. So its final count is \[ 2-1=1. \]
If an element is in all three sets: it is counted three times, then subtracted three times in the three pairwise intersections. That would leave $0$, so we add the triple intersection once: \[ 3-3+1=1. \]
Key idea: every element of $A\cup B\cup C$ ends up being counted exactly once. That is why the inclusion-exclusion formula gives the correct size of the union.
