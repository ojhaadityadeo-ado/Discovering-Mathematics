MATH DETECTIVE: CASE FILE #19

Subject: Monotonicity — From Local Slopes to an Entire Interval

Mission: Use MVT to connect slopes at individual points to the order of function values, and explain what zero, equal, or bounded derivatives tell us.

Detective Briefing: A derivative describes a rate at a point. To compare two points that may be far apart, we need a bridge. The Mean Value Theorem is that bridge.

Act 1: Connect Two Points

NondecreasingFor every pair \(x<y\),
\[f(x)\le f(y).\]
Equal values are allowed.
Strictly increasingFor every pair \(x<y\),
\[f(x)<f(y).\]
Different inputs must give this strict order.
Our conditions throughout this case file

We work on an interval I. The function is differentiable at every interior point. If an endpoint belongs to I, the function is also continuous at that endpoint.

Endpoint derivatives are not needed. Differentiability gives continuity at interior points, so these conditions let us use MVT on any \([x,y]\subseteq I\).

Choose any two inputs \(x<y\) in I. These need not be close together.

What connects the change \(f(y)-f(x)\) to a derivative somewhere between x and y?

Detective’s Note: We need an exact link between the secant slope and an interior derivative. MVT supplies that link, without saying the point is the midpoint.

MVT gives some \(c\in(x,y)\) with

\[ \frac{f(y)-f(x)}{y-x}=f'(c), \qquad \boxed{f(y)-f(x)=f'(c)(y-x).} \]

The input gap \(y-x\) is positive. So the sign of the height change is the sign of \(f'(c)\).

\[ \begin{aligned} f'\ge0&\ \Longrightarrow\ f(y)\ge f(x),\\ f'>0&\ \Longrightarrow\ f(y)>f(x). \end{aligned} \]

Since x and y were any two inputs, the conclusion holds throughout I. The point c may change when we choose a different pair. The derivative sign must hold throughout the interval, not just at one known point.

Why continuity at included endpoints matters

On \([0,1]\), set \(f(0)=1\) and \(f(t)=t\) for \(0<t\le1\). The interior derivative is 1, but \(f(0)>f(1/2)\), so f is not nondecreasing on the whole closed interval.

The function is not continuous at 0. We cannot apply MVT to a subinterval starting there. This is why our endpoint condition is part of the theorem.

Act 2: Strict Increase Can Have a Zero Slope

Does strict increase force the derivative to be positive at every point?

Detective’s Note: Strict increase compares values at two different inputs. A zero slope at one point does not make two such values equal.
\[ f(t)=t^3,\qquad f'(t)=3t^2,\qquad f'(0)=0. \]

The function is strictly increasing. Its tangent at 0 is horizontal, but the graph does not stay flat around 0.

A positive derivative everywhere is enough for strict increase. It is not needed in every case.

A horizontal tangent at one point is not a flat interval.
Check directly that x³ is strictly increasing

For any \(x<y\),

\[ \begin{aligned} y^3-x^3 &=(y-x)(y^2+xy+x^2)\\ &=(y-x)\left(\frac34(x+y)^2+\frac14(y-x)^2\right)>0. \end{aligned} \]

The first factor is positive. The bracket is also positive because \((y-x)^2>0\). So \(x^3<y^3\), even if the pair crosses 0.

Strictly increasing functions are also nondecreasing. “Nondecreasing” allows equal values; it does not require equal values to occur.

Act 3: Can We Go Back From Order to Slope?

Can a differentiable nondecreasing function have a negative derivative inside?

Detective’s Note: A step left has both a nonpositive height change and a negative input change. Dividing gives a nonnegative quotient, not a negative one.

Let u be an interior point. Take small steps that stay in the interval.

StepOrder of heightsDifference quotient
\(h>0\)\(f(u+h)-f(u)\ge0\)\(\dfrac{f(u+h)-f(u)}h\ge0\)
\(h<0\)\(f(u+h)-f(u)\le0\)\(\dfrac{f(u+h)-f(u)}h\ge0\)

The derivative exists, and all these quotients are nonnegative. Their limit is therefore nonnegative:

\[ f'(u)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(u+h)-f(u)}h\ge0. \]

Since u was any interior point, \(f'\ge0\) throughout the interior.

Both directions are now proved
Under our interval and continuity conditions,
\[ \boxed{f\text{ is nondecreasing}\iff f'\ge0.} \]

Here the derivative condition is at all interior points. For nonincreasing functions, the corresponding condition is \(f'\le0\).

Why strict increase does not force a strictly positive derivative: even positive quotients can have limit zero. For \(f(t)=t^3\) at 0,

\[ \frac{f(h)-f(0)}h=h^2>0\ (h\ne0), \qquad h^2\longrightarrow0. \]

The limit keeps “at least zero”, but need not keep “strictly greater than zero”.

Act 4: Zero Everywhere — But Where?

What if the derivative is zero at every interior point of the interval?

Detective’s Note: For any x < y, MVT gives f(y) − f(x) = f′(c)(y − x). Now f′(c) is zero for every possible interior c.

For any \(x<y\), MVT gives

\[ f(y)-f(x)=f'(c)(y-x)=0. \]

All function values agree. Therefore

\[ \boxed{f'=0\text{ throughout an interval} \ \Longrightarrow\ f\text{ is constant}.} \]

Our continuity condition covers any included endpoints. A zero derivative at one point did not give this result; zero throughout the interval does.

Why does “on an interval” matter?

Consider a function whose domain leaves out 0:

\[ f(t)=\begin{cases}0,&t<0,\\1,&t>0,\end{cases} \qquad I_{\text{domain}}=(-\infty,0)\cup(0,\infty). \]
Detective’s Note: The interval [−1, 1] includes 0, but the function has no value there. We cannot apply MVT across that missing point.
Two separate intervals can carry two different constants.

An interval contains every point between any two of its points. That is exactly what lets us apply MVT between them.

Act 5: Equal Derivatives, Equal Functions?

Let F and G satisfy the same interval conditions, and suppose \(F'=G'\) throughout the interior.

If two functions have the same slopes everywhere, must their heights be identical?

Detective’s Note: (F − G)′ = F′ − G′ = 0. A zero derivative throughout one interval makes the difference constant, not necessarily zero.
\[ (F-G)'=F'-G'=0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{F-G=C,\qquad F=G+C.} \]

Their vertical difference is the same at every input. They may be the same graph shifted up or down.

Move the constant, keep the derivativesExample: G(t) = t² and F(t) = t² + C. Both derivatives are 2t.
G(t) = t² F(t) = t² + C Difference at t = 1 Tangents
F′ = G′ = 2t, while F − G = 1 throughout the interval.

An antiderivative of a function is a function whose derivative equals that given function. If F and G are two antiderivatives of the same function on one interval, their difference is a constant. That is why an additive “+ C” appears.

On separate intervals, the constants can be different. If F and G also agree at one input in the interval, then C = 0 and they agree everywhere on that interval.

Act 6: A Bound on Rate Gives a Bound on Change

Suppose \(|f'|\le M\) at all interior points, where M is a fixed nonnegative number.

Can the total height change exceed M times the input gap?

Detective’s Note: MVT says the total change is one interior derivative times the input gap. The magnitude of that derivative is at most M.
\[ \begin{aligned} |f(y)-f(x)| &=|f'(c)(y-x)|\\ &=|f'(c)|\,|y-x|\\ &\le M|y-x|. \end{aligned} \]
\[\boxed{|f(y)-f(x)|\le M|y-x|.}\]

Absolute values let us write the same bound in either order of x and y. If x = y, both sides are zero.

A bound is a limit on how large the change can be. It is not a claim that the change always equals that limit.

Final Report: Close the Case

Detective’s Note: The derivative sign holds at every possible interior c. We did not choose a special pair x, y. Different pairs may use different points c.
The main proof in short

Choose any x < y in I. Our interval conditions let us apply MVT on [x, y]. It gives

\[f(y)-f(x)=f'(c)(y-x).\]

The gap y − x is positive. If f′ ≥ 0 throughout the interior, the height change is nonnegative. If f′ > 0, the change is positive.

This works for every pair, so f is nondecreasing or strictly increasing, respectively. Negative derivative signs give the corresponding decreasing results.
