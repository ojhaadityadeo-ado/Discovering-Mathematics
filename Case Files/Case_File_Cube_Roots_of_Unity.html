MATH DETECTIVE: CASE FILE #38

Subject: Cube Roots of Unity — Three Turns, One Return

Mission: Find all complex multipliers that return every point after three applications. Use their geometry to explain their sum, their conjugates, and the factors of a sum of cubes.

The case: A complex multiplier acts on every point of the complex plane. Apply it three times and every point must return to where it began. The multiplier 1 clearly works. Are there any others?

Follow 9 main clues and the small-step questions. Each green “Why this matters” note connects the current step to our main goal. The two visual labs and answer checking work offline; equation formatting and display fonts use the internet. Print your notes before closing if you want to keep them.

Act 1: Where could the multiplier be?

A point with complex coordinate q moves to zq when we multiply by z. Three applications give \(q\mapsto zq\mapsto z^2q\mapsto z^3q\). “Unity” means the number 1, so a cube root of unity is a number satisfying

\[z^3=1.\]

Small step 1. Why does returning every point lead to this equation?

Open the answer
Take the starting point q = 1. Returning that point requires \(z^3=1\). Conversely, if \(z^3=1\), then \(z^3q=q\) for every complex q. Checking only q = 0 would tell us nothing: every multiplier keeps 0 at 0.

Before finding z exactly, can we locate it? For \(z=x+iy\), its modulus \(|z|=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\) is its distance from the origin. Moduli multiply: \(|uv|=|u||v|\).

Clue 1 · What immediate observation follows from \(z^3=1\)?

Follow this clue: Take the modulus on both sides. Remember that a modulus is a nonnegative real number.
Open the reasoning
\[|z|^3=|z^3|=|1|=1.\]

The only nonnegative real number with cube 1 is 1. Hence \(|z|=1\). Also z cannot be zero, since \(0^3\ne1\).

Small step 2. Have we found the roots, or only narrowed down where they can be?

Open the answer
Only narrowed them down. For example, \(|i|=1\), but \(i^3=-i\ne1\). Being on the unit circle is necessary; it is not enough.
Why this matters: The multiplier cannot stretch or shrink distances from the origin. We now need to find which turns return a point after three applications.

Act 2: Find every root without guessing angles

We already know that z = 1 works. So we should use it to simplify the equation.

Clue 2 · What is a useful next step if we want all the other roots?

Follow this clue: A known root suggests a factor. But dividing by z−1 without a separate case would lose the root z = 1.
Open the reasoning
\[z^3-1=(z-1)(z^2+z+1).\]

Expanding the right side gives \(z^3+z^2+z-z^2-z-1=z^3-1\). A product of complex numbers is zero only if at least one factor is zero. Therefore

\[z=1\quad\text{or}\quad z^2+z+1=0.\]

Now apply the quadratic formula with coefficients 1, 1, 1:

\[\begin{aligned} z&=\frac{-1\pm\sqrt{1^2-4(1)(1)}}{2(1)}\\ &=\frac{-1\pm\sqrt{-3}}2 =\frac{-1\pm i\sqrt3}{2}. \end{aligned}\]

Small step 3. Why does \(i\sqrt3\) appear?

Open the answer
Because \((i\sqrt3)^2=i^2\cdot3=-3\). The two square roots of −3 are \(i\sqrt3\) and \(-i\sqrt3\). This is where the two nonreal roots enter.

Choose the root above the real axis and name it

\[\omega=-\frac12+\frac{\sqrt3}{2}i.\]

Do not assume the other root is \(\omega^2\) yet. Check by multiplying:

\[\begin{aligned} \omega^2 &=\left(-\frac12+\frac{\sqrt3}{2}i\right)^2\\ &=\left(-\frac12\right)^2 +2\left(-\frac12\right)\left(\frac{\sqrt3}{2}i\right) +\left(\frac{\sqrt3}{2}i\right)^2\\ &=\frac14-\frac{\sqrt3}{2}i+\frac34i^2\\ &=\frac14-\frac34-\frac{\sqrt3}{2}i\\ &=-\frac12-\frac{\sqrt3}{2}i. \end{aligned}\]

Small step 4. Which step makes the real part \(-\tfrac12\), rather than 1?

Open the answer
Using \(i^2=-1\). The last squared term is \(-3/4\), so the real part is \(1/4-3/4=-1/2\).

The three distinct roots are

\[\boxed{1,\ \omega,\ \omega^2}.\]

The factorization gave all possibilities: one root from the linear factor and two from the quadratic. So no roots are missing.

Why this matters: We have exact coordinates. Now we can explain the shape and the turns from those coordinates, instead of relying on a picture alone.

Act 3: Read the coordinates as turns

On the unit circle, a point at angle \(\theta\) has coordinate \(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta\). Angles here are measured counterclockwise from the positive real axis.

RootPoint in the planeAngle, modulo 360°
1\((1,0)\)0°
\(\omega\)\(\left(-\tfrac12,\tfrac{\sqrt3}2\right)\)120°
\(\omega^2\)\(\left(-\tfrac12,-\tfrac{\sqrt3}2\right)\)240° (also −120°)

Small step 5. Why is the angle of \(\omega\) 120°, not 60°?

Open the answer
Its real part is negative and its imaginary part is positive, so it is in the second quadrant. Here \(\cos120^\circ=-1/2\) and \(\sin120^\circ=\sqrt3/2\). At 60°, the real part would be positive.

The gaps are 120°, 120°, and another 120° from 240° back to 360° = 0°. Equal central angles in the same circle give equal chords. Thus the roots form an equilateral triangle.

Small step 6. Can we check one side length from the coordinates?

Open the answer
Yes. The side from \(\omega\) to \(\omega^2\) is vertical and has length \(\sqrt3\). Also \(|1-\omega|^2=(3/2)^2+(\sqrt3/2)^2=3\), and similarly \(|1-\omega^2|^2=3\). All three sides have length \(\sqrt3\).

Multiplying complex numbers multiplies their moduli and adds their angles. For example, multiplying \(\rho(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)\) by \(\cos120^\circ+i\sin120^\circ\) gives

\[\rho\bigl(\cos(\theta+120^\circ)+i\sin(\theta+120^\circ)\bigr).\]

This follows by expanding and using the sine and cosine addition formulas. The length \(\rho\) stays the same.

Clue 3 · What does multiplying an arbitrary point by ω do?

Follow this clue: Use both pieces of information: the modulus tells you the scale, and the angle tells you the turn.
Open the reasoning

Since \(|\omega|=1\), multiplication does not change length. Its angle is 120°, so it rotates each nonzero point through 120°. The origin stays fixed. Applying this three times makes a full 360° turn.

Multiplication by \(\omega^2\) gives a 240° counterclockwise turn, or a 120° clockwise turn. Three such turns give 720°, again returning every point. Multiplication by 1 changes nothing.

Why this matters: The equation is now a motion we can see. The three roots represent the three possible turns that become a whole number of full turns after three applications.

Act 4: Why do the three roots add to zero?

We want to explain \(1+\omega+\omega^2\). A useful clue is the factorization we already have:

\[0=\omega^3-1=(\omega-1)(\omega^2+\omega+1).\]
Clue 4 · Which fact lets us conclude that the second factor is zero?

Follow this clue: Could we divide by the first factor if we had chosen the root 1?
Open the reasoning

Our chosen \(\omega\) is nonreal, so \(\omega-1\ne0\). Divide by that nonzero factor:

\[\boxed{1+\omega+\omega^2=0}.\]

If we chose z = 1 instead, the first factor would be zero. The second would be \(1+1+1=3\), not zero. In these identities, ω means a nonreal cube root of unity.

Small step 7. Can we see the cancellation directly in the coordinates?

Open the answer
Yes. The real parts add to \(1-\tfrac12-\tfrac12=0\). The imaginary parts add to \(0+\tfrac{\sqrt3}2-\tfrac{\sqrt3}2=0\). Both coordinates of the sum are zero.

Adding complex numbers adds their position vectors. Place the vector for 1 first, then move a copy of the vector for ω to its endpoint, then do the same for \(\omega^2\). The final endpoint is their sum.

Clue 5 · If three complex numbers add to zero, what can we safely conclude?

Follow this clue: Try 1, 2 and −3. Their sum is zero, but where are their points?
Open the reasoning

The points 1, 2 and −3 all lie on the real axis. They add to zero but do not form a nondegenerate triangle. For our roots, the equilateral shape comes from their unit lengths and 120° spacing, not from the zero sum alone.

What does the zero sum say about the center?

The centroid is the average of the three position vectors:

\[G=\frac{1+\omega+\omega^2}{3}=0.\]

So the origin is the centroid of the root triangle. There is another way to see why the sum has to be zero.

Small step 8. If \(S=1+\omega+\omega^2\), what happens when we multiply S by ω?

Open the answer
\(\omega S=\omega+\omega^2+\omega^3=\omega+\omega^2+1=S\). The multiplication just moves each root to the next one, so the sum is unchanged.

Yet multiplying by ω turns a vector through 120°. The only vector that remains in the same position under that turn is the zero vector. Algebraically, \(\omega S=S\) gives \((\omega-1)S=0\); since \(\omega\ne1\), we get S = 0.

Why this matters: We now have two useful facts, \(\omega+\omega^2=-1\) and \(\omega^3=1\). They will simplify the coefficients when we multiply the complex factors later.

Act 5: A reflection is also an inverse

The conjugate of \(x+iy\) is \(x-iy\). Geometrically, this reflects a point across the real axis. So reflecting the point at 120° gives the point at −120°, which is also 240°.

\[\overline\omega=-\frac12-\frac{\sqrt3}{2}i=\omega^2.\]

Can we explain the same fact without using the exact coordinates? For any complex z, \(z\overline z=|z|^2\). Since ω has modulus 1,

\[\omega\overline\omega=1,\qquad \omega\omega^2=\omega^3=1.\]
Clue 6 · Both products equal 1. What is the useful next observation?

Follow this clue: A nonzero number has only one multiplicative inverse.
Open the reasoning

Subtract the two product equations:

\[\omega(\overline\omega-\omega^2)=0.\]

Since \(\omega\ne0\), we can divide by it and get \(\overline\omega=\omega^2\). Thus

\[\boxed{\overline\omega=\omega^{-1}=\omega^2}.\]

Small step 9. Is \(\overline z=z^2\) true for every point on the unit circle?

Open the answer
No. On the unit circle, \(\overline z=1/z\). For z = i, the conjugate is −i, while \(z^2=-1\). For ω we also use \(\omega^3=1\), which gives \(1/\omega=\omega^2\).
Why this matters: The two nonreal roots form a conjugate pair. This will explain how two complex factors can multiply to give a real expression.

Act 6: Turn the roots into factors

We found ω and \(\omega^2\) by solving \(x^2+x+1=0\). So the natural candidate is \((x-\omega)(x-\omega^2)\). To prove equality for every x, we should expand it.

\[\begin{aligned} (x-\omega)(x-\omega^2) &=x^2-x\omega^2-x\omega+\omega\omega^2\\ &=x^2-(\omega+\omega^2)x+\omega^3. \end{aligned}\]
Clue 7 · Which earlier facts finish this calculation?

Follow this clue: We need one fact for the x coefficient and another for the constant term.
Open the reasoning
\[x^2-(\omega+\omega^2)x+\omega^3=x^2-(-1)x+1=x^2+x+1.\]

Therefore

\[\boxed{x^2+x+1=(x-\omega)(x-\omega^2)}.\]

Small step 10. Why does the middle term become +x rather than −x?

Open the answer
There are two minus signs: the expansion has \(-(\omega+\omega^2)x\), and the sum inside the parentheses is −1. Thus \(-(-1)x=+x\).

Both sides have leading coefficient 1. This matters: knowing the zeros alone would not distinguish \(x^2+x+1\) from \(5(x^2+x+1)\). The direct expansion checks the coefficient as well as the roots.

Why this matters: We can now turn a quadratic with no real roots into two linear factors over the complex numbers. The same idea will explain the sum of cubes.

Act 7: Discover the factors of a³ + b³

Let a and b be complex numbers. We want an identity for all their values. First treat b as fixed and ask which values of a make \(a^3+b^3=0\).

Clue 8 · We want to connect this equation to cube roots of unity. What can we do?

Follow this clue: We know the roots of z³ = 1. Can a ratio of a and b play the role of z?
Open the reasoning

For \(b\ne0\),

\[\begin{aligned} a^3+b^3=0 &\iff a^3=-b^3\\ &\iff \frac{a^3}{b^3}=-1\\ &\iff \left(-\frac ab\right)^3=1. \end{aligned}\]

The minus sign is important: \((a/b)^3=-1\), whereas \((-a/b)^3=1\). Therefore

\[-\frac ab\in\{1,\omega,\omega^2\}.\]

Small step 11. If \(-a/b=\omega\), what is a, and which factor is zero?

Open the answer
Multiply by b and then by −1: \(a=-b\omega\). Hence \(a+b\omega=0\). The other roots similarly suggest \(a+b\) and \(a+b\omega^2\).

So the three zeros are \(-b,-b\omega,-b\omega^2\). This suggests

\[(a+b)(a+b\omega)(a+b\omega^2).\]

Geometrically, these three zeros are the original root triangle multiplied by \(-b\). For \(b\ne0\), this rotates it and scales every length by \(|b|\); it is still equilateral and centered at 0.

The roots suggest the factors. How can we verify the whole identity, including the case b = 0?

Multiply directly. Start with the two nonreal factors:

\[\begin{aligned} (a+b\omega)(a+b\omega^2) &=a^2+ab\omega^2+ab\omega+b^2\omega\omega^2\\ &=a^2+ab(\omega+\omega^2)+b^2\omega^3\\ &=a^2-ab+b^2. \end{aligned}\]

Small step 12. Why do we get \(b^2\), not b, in the last term?

Open the answer
The last term comes from \((b\omega)(b\omega^2)\). The two b factors multiply to \(b^2\), while \(\omega\omega^2=\omega^3=1\).

Now include the remaining factor:

\[\begin{aligned} (a+b)(a^2-ab+b^2) &=a(a^2-ab+b^2)+b(a^2-ab+b^2)\\ &=a^3-a^2b+ab^2+a^2b-ab^2+b^3\\ &=a^3+b^3. \end{aligned}\]

Small step 13. Which terms cancel, and why is this calculation stronger than just checking a few values?

Open the answer
The terms \(-a^2b\) and \(+a^2b\) cancel, as do \(+ab^2\) and \(-ab^2\). We used algebraic identities, with no division by b. So the equality holds for every complex a and b, including b = 0.
\[\boxed{a^3+b^3=(a+b)(a+b\omega)(a+b\omega^2)}.\]

At b = 0, the right side is \(a\cdot a\cdot a=a^3\). The three zeros in a merge at 0; the identity still holds.

Why this matters: The root equation tells us where to look for factors. Multiplication checks the exact identity and includes the case that the division argument could not cover.

Act 8: How do complex factors give a real result?

Now suppose a and b are real. The familiar real factorization is

\[a^3+b^3=(a+b)(a^2-ab+b^2).\]
Clue 9 · Why is the product of the other two complex factors real?

Follow this clue: Conjugation reflects the imaginary part. What does it do to the real numbers a and b?
Open the reasoning
\[\overline{a+b\omega}=\overline a+\overline b\,\overline\omega=a+b\omega^2.\]

Writing the factors in coordinates makes the cancellation visible:

\[\begin{aligned} a+b\omega&=\left(a-\frac b2\right)+i\frac{\sqrt3 b}{2},\\ a+b\omega^2&=\left(a-\frac b2\right)-i\frac{\sqrt3 b}{2}. \end{aligned}\]

Multiply these conjugates:

\[\begin{aligned} (a+b\omega)(a+b\omega^2) &=\left(a-\frac b2\right)^2+\left(\frac{\sqrt3 b}{2}\right)^2\\ &=a^2-ab+\frac{b^2}{4}+\frac{3b^2}{4}\\ &=a^2-ab+b^2. \end{aligned}\]

Geometrically, this is \(|a+b\omega|^2\): the squared distance of that complex point from the origin.

Small step 14. Where did we use the assumption that a and b are real?

Open the answer
We used \(\overline a=a\) and \(\overline b=b\). For arbitrary complex a and b, the two factors need not be conjugates. But the sum-of-cubes factorization remains true, because its direct expansion did not use that assumption.

For real a and b, the two-square expression is nonnegative and is zero only if a = b = 0. If \(b\ne0\), it is positive, so the only real zero of \(a^3+b^3\), viewed as an equation in a, is \(a=-b\). The other two zeros are nonreal.

Final case report

The roots: \(1,\omega,\omega^2\), where \(\omega=-\tfrac12+\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}i\).

The geometry: Their angles are 0°, 120°, 240°. They form an equilateral triangle on the unit circle. Multiplication by ω rotates every point by 120°.

The useful identities:

\[\omega^3=1,\qquad1+\omega+\omega^2=0,\qquad\overline\omega=\omega^2.\]

The factors:

\[x^2+x+1=(x-\omega)(x-\omega^2),\]
\[a^3+b^3=(a+b)(a+b\omega)(a+b\omega^2).\]

What holds the proof together? Modulus locates the roots. Factoring finds them all. Angles explain the motion. The zero sum and product simplify the factors. A direct expansion proves the final identity for every a and b.
