MATH DETECTIVE: CASE FILE #40

Subject: Even-Sized and Odd-Sized Subsets

Mission: Explain why a nonempty n-element set has exactly as many even-sized subsets as odd-sized subsets. Prove it by toggling one fixed element, calculate the number in each class, and then verify the alternating-sum identity with the binomial theorem.

The case: A school permits every possible committee from n students, including the empty committee. Are there more committees of even size or odd size—or are the two numbers exactly equal?

Follow 7 clues and the small-step questions. Try each clue before opening its reasoning. The parity-pairing lab works offline; equation formatting and display fonts use an internet connection.

Act 1: Decode the alternating sum

For an n-student set, \(\binom nr\) counts the committees with exactly r members. Consider

\[\sum_{r=0}^{n}(-1)^r\binom nr.\]

What is the factor \((-1)^r\) doing?

Clue 1 · Which terms are added, and which are subtracted?

Follow this clue: Check \((-1)^0,(-1)^1,(-1)^2,(-1)^3\).
Open the reasoning

If r is even, \((-1)^r=1\). If r is odd, \((-1)^r=-1\). Therefore

\[\sum_{r=0}^{n}(-1)^r\binom nr=\sum_{\substack{0\le r\le n\\r\text{ even}}}\binom nr-\sum_{\substack{0\le r\le n\\r\text{ odd}}}\binom nr.\]

So the alternating sum measures

\[\text{number of even-sized committees}-\text{number of odd-sized committees}.\]

Small step 1. Is the empty committee counted as even or odd?

Open the answer

Even. Its size is 0, and 0 is even. Thus the term \(\binom n0=1\) belongs to the even-sized side.

Act 2: Toggle one fixed student

Now assume \(n\ge1\), so there is at least one student. Choose one student and call her Alice.

Given any committee, make exactly one change: reverse Alice's membership.

Clue 2 · Why does toggling Alice always reverse parity?

Follow this clue: Consecutive integers always have opposite parity.
Open the reasoning

Adding or removing Alice changes the size by exactly one. If the original committee has size k, the partner has size k+1 or k−1. Consecutive integers have opposite parity, so even becomes odd and odd becomes even.

Small step 2. If a committee has 4 members and Alice is absent, what size does its partner have?

Open the answer

5 members. Adding Alice changes the size from even to odd.

Act 3: Check that the pairing is exact

Changing parity is not enough by itself. To prove equal counts, every committee must have exactly one partner, with no committees missed and no repeats.

Clue 3 · What happens if we toggle Alice twice?

Follow this clue: If you add Alice, the next toggle removes her; if you remove her, the next toggle adds her back.
Open the reasoning

The operation is its own inverse. Toggling Alice twice restores her original membership status and leaves every other student unchanged. Thus each committee belongs to one reversible pair.

No committee can be paired with itself, because Alice's membership is different in the two partners.

Small step 3. Why can no committee be its own partner?

Open the answer

The two partners disagree about whether Alice belongs to the committee. A committee cannot both contain Alice and not contain Alice.

Act 4: Count the two parity classes

Each reversible pair has exactly two committees: one even-sized and one odd-sized. Therefore the two parity classes contain the same number of committees.

\[E=O,\]

where E is the number of even-sized committees and O is the number of odd-sized committees.

Clue 4 · What does E = O tell us about the alternating sum?

Follow this clue: Subtract a number from an equal number.

There are \(2^n\) committees altogether. Since the two parity classes are equal,

\[E+O=2^n,\qquad E=O.\]

Substitute O = E:

\[2E=2^n\quad\Longrightarrow\quad E=2^{n-1}.\]

The same is true for O.

\(2^{n-1}\)even-sized subsets
\(2^{n-1}\)odd-sized subsets
Clue 5 · For n = 5, how many committees have each parity?

Follow this clue: Use \(2^{n-1}\) with n = 5.

Small step 4. Why is the empty committee not a problem for equal parity counts?

Open the answer

The empty committee is even, but it is paired with the one-person committee {Alice}, which is odd. The pairing balances it just like every other committee.

Act 5: Stress-test n = 0

Why does the theorem require \(n\ge1\)?

If n = 0, there is no student available to designate as Alice, so the toggle construction cannot even begin.

More importantly, there is exactly one subset of the empty set:

\[\varnothing.\]

Its size is 0, which is even. Thus

\[E=1,\qquad O=0,\qquad E-O=1.\]

Indeed, the alternating sum is

\[\sum_{r=0}^{0}(-1)^r\binom0r=(-1)^0\binom00=1.\]
Clue 6 · What exactly fails at n = 0?

Follow this clue: The empty set still has one subset—namely itself.

Act 6: Follow the algebraic proof in detail

The pairing argument already proves the identity. Now verify the same result algebraically with the binomial theorem, assuming \(n\ge1\).

Step 1 · Start with the binomial theorem.
\[(x+y)^n=\sum_{r=0}^{n}\binom nr x^{\,n-r}y^r.\]
What the index means: r runs from 0 to n, and the rth term carries the coefficient \(\binom nr\).

Small step 5. Which choice of x and y will create the factor \((-1)^r\)?

Open the answer

Choose x = 1 and y = −1. Then the factor \(y^r\) becomes \((-1)^r\), producing the alternating signs.

Step 2 · Substitute x = 1 and y = −1.
\[(1+(-1))^n=\sum_{r=0}^{n}\binom nr 1^{\,n-r}(-1)^r.\]

Equivalently,

\[(1-1)^n=\sum_{r=0}^{n}\binom nr 1^{\,n-r}(-1)^r.\]
Why is substitution allowed? The binomial theorem is an identity in x and y, so it remains true after replacing x and y by specific values.

Small step 6. What happens to the factor \(1^{n-r}\) in every term?

Open the answer

It becomes 1. Since \(0\le r\le n\), the exponent n−r is a nonnegative integer, and every such power of 1 equals 1.

Step 3 · Simplify the left-hand side.

Because \(n\ge1\),

\[(1-1)^n=0^n=0.\]
Why is the condition n ≥ 1 used here? For a positive exponent, \(0^n=0\). The n=0 case was separated earlier and gives the value 1 instead.

Small step 7. Why should we not silently use the same conclusion at n = 0?

Open the answer

The polynomial \((x+y)^0\) is identically 1, so at x = 1 and y = −1 it still equals 1. The n=0 case therefore does not give a zero alternating sum.

Step 4 · Simplify the right-hand side.

Since \(1^{n-r}=1\), each term becomes

\[\binom nr 1^{\,n-r}(-1)^r=(-1)^r\binom nr.\]

Therefore

\[\sum_{r=0}^{n}\binom nr 1^{\,n-r}(-1)^r=\sum_{r=0}^{n}(-1)^r\binom nr.\]
What remains? The powers of 1 disappear, while \((-1)^r\) stays and determines the sign of each binomial coefficient.

Small step 8. Why does \((-1)^r\) turn the coefficient sum into “even minus odd”?

Open the answer

When r is even, \((-1)^r=1\), so \(\binom nr\) is added. When r is odd, \((-1)^r=-1\), so \(\binom nr\) is subtracted.

Step 5 · Put the simplified sides together.

The left side is 0, and the right side is the alternating sum. Hence

\[0=\sum_{r=0}^{n}(-1)^r\binom nr.\]

Reversing the two sides gives

\[\boxed{\sum_{r=0}^{n}(-1)^r\binom nr=0},\qquad n\ge1.\]

Small step 9. How does this algebraic result match the pairing proof?

Open the answer

The alternating sum is E−O. The algebra says E−O=0, so E=O—the same conclusion obtained by pairing each committee with the one formed by toggling Alice.

Full algebra in one continuous chain

\[ \begin{aligned} (x+y)^n &=\sum_{r=0}^{n}\binom nr x^{\,n-r}y^r\\[4pt] (1-1)^n &=\sum_{r=0}^{n}\binom nr 1^{\,n-r}(-1)^r\\[4pt] 0 &=\sum_{r=0}^{n}(-1)^r\binom nr\\[4pt] \therefore\qquad \sum_{r=0}^{n}(-1)^r\binom nr &=0,\qquad n\ge1. \end{aligned} \]

Clue 7 · What common statement are both proofs establishing?

Follow this clue: One proof says E and O can be paired; the other says E−O=0.

Final case report

For every integer \(n\ge1\),

\[\boxed{\sum_{r=0}^{n}(-1)^r\binom nr=0}.\]

What the sum measures: even-sized subsets minus odd-sized subsets.

Why the counts are equal: toggling one fixed element changes the size by exactly one and gives a reversible pairing.

How many of each: since there are \(2^n\) subsets altogether, each parity class contains \(2^{n-1}\) subsets.

Why n ≥ 1 matters: when n=0, the empty set is the only subset; it has even size, so the alternating sum is 1.

Why the algebra works: setting x=1 and y=−1 in the binomial theorem makes the left side 0 for n≥1 and turns the right side into the alternating sum.
