CASE FILE

The Product Rule

Subject: The derivative of a product.

Mission: Find what changes when both factors change. Then find which parts remain in the rate.

Before we begin

Let \(f\) and \(g\) be differentiable at \(a\). This means that \(f'(a)\) and \(g'(a)\) both exist.

When the input moves from \(a\) to \(a+h\), write

\[ \Delta f=f(a+h)-f(a),\qquad \Delta g=g(a+h)-g(a). \]

The symbol \(\Delta\) means “change”. A derivative tells us the limiting rate of change, not just the change itself.

Choose an answer before reading the next clue. Use the yellow spaces to explain your thinking. The answer check gives hints; written notes are for discussion.

Act 1 · Look at the change

If both factors change, what happens to their product?

For a moment, let \(f=f(a)\) and \(g=g(a)\). The old product is \(fg\). The new product is \((f+\Delta f)(g+\Delta g)\).

Expand the new product. After subtracting the old product, there are three terms, not two.

\[ (f+\Delta f)(g+\Delta g)-fg =\underbrace{g\,\Delta f}_{\text{first strip}} +\underbrace{f\,\Delta g}_{\text{second strip}} +\underbrace{\Delta f\,\Delta g}_{\text{corner}}. \]

The first strip comes from changing the first factor. The second comes from changing the second factor. The corner comes from both changes together.

A rectangle you can change

Example: f(x) = x + 2, g(x) = x, at a = 2. The old sides are 4 and 2. Each side increases by h.

Second strip \(f\,\Delta g=4h\) 4

Act 2 · From change to rate

There are three pieces. Why does the derivative have only two terms?

Do not remove the corner yet. A small area is not the same thing as a small rate. First divide the whole change by \(h\), where \(h\ne0\).

\[ \frac{f(a+h)g(a+h)-f(a)g(a)}{h} = g(a)\frac{\Delta f}{h} +f(a)\frac{\Delta g}{h} +\underbrace{\frac{\Delta f}{h}\,\Delta g} _{\text{corner's contribution to the rate}}. \]

Look at the corner area after division by h. We need its limit, not its value at a single small step.

Because both derivatives exist,

\[ \frac{\Delta f}{h}\longrightarrow f'(a), \qquad \frac{\Delta g}{h}\longrightarrow g'(a). \]

Also, differentiability of \(g\) gives continuity at \(a\). So \(\Delta g\to0\). You can see this directly:

\[ \Delta g=h\frac{\Delta g}{h} \longrightarrow 0\cdot g'(a)=0. \]

Therefore the corner's contribution has limit

\[ \frac{\Delta f}{h}\,\Delta g \longrightarrow f'(a)\cdot0=0. \]

One factor approaches a finite number. The other approaches zero. Their product approaches zero.

A differentiable function is continuous at that point. Its nearby values approach its value at the point; that value need not be zero.

The same rectangle, now divided by h

First strip: \(2h/h=2\). Second strip: \(4h/h=4\).

Corner: \(h^2/h=h\) = 1.

Total rate for this step: \(6+h\) = 7.

As h approaches 0, the strip rates stay at 2 and 4. The corner rate approaches 0. The total approaches 6.

Act 3 · Test a tempting guess

Why isn't the rule simply \(f'g'\)?

Try \(f(x)=g(x)=x\). Both factors change at rate 1, so \(f'(x)g'(x)=1\).

But their product is \(x^2\). Its rate at \(a\) is

\[ \lim_{h\to0}\frac{(a+h)^2-a^2}{h} =\lim_{h\to0}(2a+h)=2a. \]

At \(a=3\), the product's rate is 6, not 1. The two strips each contribute 3.

A strip's area is its width times its height. A factor's change is multiplied by the current size of the other factor.

If the first factor changes, its rate is multiplied by the current size \(g(a)\). This gives \(f'(a)g(a)\).

If the second factor changes, its rate is multiplied by the current size \(f(a)\). This gives \(f(a)g'(a)\).

The product's rate depends on both the sizes and the rates of its factors.

Act 4 · Check the assumptions

If a product has a derivative, must both factors have derivatives?

Try f(x) = g(x) = |x| at 0. Compare the slopes of each factor with the slope of their product.

Take \(f(x)=g(x)=|x|\) and look at \(0\).

For either factor, the difference quotient is

\[ \frac{|0+h|-|0|}{h} =\frac{|h|}{h} =\begin{cases} 1,&h>0,\\ -1,&h<0. \end{cases} \]

The slopes from the two sides do not agree. Neither factor has a derivative at 0.

Yet their product is \(|x|\,|x|=x^2\), and

\[ \lim_{h\to0}\frac{h^2-0}{h} =\lim_{h\to0}h=0. \]

So the product does have a derivative at 0.

The theorem says: if both factors have derivatives, the product rule applies. It does not say that every product with a derivative must have two differentiable factors.

In this example, we cannot use the product rule at 0, because \(f'(0)\) and \(g'(0)\) do not exist. We find the product's derivative directly instead.

Final report · Put the proof together

Two changing factors. Two terms in the limiting rate.

Start with all three parts of the change, divide by \(h\ne0\), then take limits. The corner term has limit zero. This leaves

\[ \boxed{(fg)'(a)=f'(a)g(a)+f(a)g'(a).} \]

Each factor's rate is multiplied by the current size of the other factor. Then add the two contributions.

The formula uses f′(a) and g′(a), so both must exist. Continuity alone does not give us these derivatives.

Last check: “the corner is zero” and “the corner's contribution tends to zero” are not the same statement.
