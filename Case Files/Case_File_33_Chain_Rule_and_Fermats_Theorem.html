Subject: The Chain Rule and Fermat’s Theorem

Mission: Explain why rates multiply through two functions, then find why a smooth peak or valley inside an interval must have zero slope.

Detective Briefing: These are two different results with a shared tool: the derivative limit. Follow the input through two functions in Part I. Follow the signs from both sides of a point in Part II.

Part I · Chain rule

Act 1: Follow the changing input

Suppose g is differentiable at a and f is differentiable at \(b=g(a)\). The composite \(F=f\circ g\) means \(F(x)=f(g(x))\): apply g first, then f.

Our target is

\[\boxed{F'(a)=f'(g(a))\,g'(a).}\]

The two derivatives are measured at the inputs their functions actually receive.

Input\(a\longrightarrow a+h\)
→
After g\(b\longrightarrow b+k\)
→
After f\(f(b)\longrightarrow f(b+k)\)

Here \(h\) is the change in x, and \(k=g(a+h)-g(a)\) is the change in the input received by f.

Clue 1 · We need the output change per unit input change. What do the two stages suggest?

Hint: Write “change in g” first, then use it as the input change for f.

\[\Delta g\approx g'(a)h,\qquad \Delta F\approx f'(b)\Delta g \approx f'(b)g'(a)h.\]

This is the idea behind the rule. The approximation is not yet a proof: we must show the error disappears after division by h.

Small step 1 · If \(g'(a)=3\) and \(f'(b)=-2\), what overall rate do we expect?

Check this step
\[(-2)\cdot3=-6.\]

A small positive input change is first enlarged by about 3, then reversed and enlarged by about 2.

Small step 2 · If \(g(x)=x^2\) and \(a=2\), what is b? Where must we evaluate \(f'\)?

Check this step
\[b=g(2)=4,\qquad \text{use }f'(4),\text{ not }f'(2).\]

Act 2: Can we cancel the middle change?

A tempting calculation is

\[\frac{f(g(a+h))-f(g(a))}{h} = \frac{f(b+k)-f(b)}{k}\,\frac{k}{h}.\]
Clue 2 · What must we check before writing the right-hand side?

Hint: The right-hand side divides by the change in g. Is that change always nonzero?

Find the missing case

If g is constant near a, then \(k=0\) for every small h. The composite is constant there too, so its derivative is zero, but the displayed factorization contains \(0/0\).

Even a nonconstant g may return to the same value. For \(g(x)=x^2\), take \(a=1/4\) and \(h=-1/2\). Then \(a+h=-1/4\), so the two squared values agree.

We need a proof that still works when \(k=0\).

Small step 3 · In that example, calculate k and the composite output change. Is the original quotient divided by h still defined?

Check this step
\[k=(-1/4)^2-(1/4)^2=0,\qquad f(b+k)-f(b)=0.\]
\[\frac{\Delta F}{h}=\frac{0}{-1/2}=0.\]

The original quotient is fine. Only the extra division by k fails.

Act 3: Keep the small error, without dividing by k

We know f is differentiable at b. For \(k\ne0\), define the error in its difference quotient by

\[r(k)=\frac{f(b+k)-f(b)}{k}-f'(b).\]

By the definition of derivative, \(r(k)\to0\) as \(k\to0\). Now define \(r(0)=0\).

Small step 4 · How can we rearrange this into a statement about the output change itself?

Check this step
\[f(b+k)-f(b)=f'(b)k+k\,r(k).\]

Multiply by k when k is nonzero. The linear part is \(f'(b)k\); the remaining part is \(k\,r(k)\).

Small step 5 · Does this new identity also hold when k = 0?

Check this step
\[f(b)-f(b)=0,\qquad f'(b)\cdot0+0\cdot r(0)=0.\]

Yes. We did not define a quotient \(0/0\). We assigned a value to the new error function r at zero.

Substitute \(k=g(a+h)-g(a)\). For \(h\ne0\), divide the identity by h:

\[\frac{F(a+h)-F(a)}{h} =f'(b)\frac{k}{h}+r(k)\frac{k}{h}.\]

Small step 6 · What tells us that k tends to zero as h tends to zero?

Check this step

Differentiability of g at a implies continuity there. Thus

\[g(a+h)\to g(a)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad k=g(a+h)-g(a)\to0.\]

Because \(r(k)\to0\) and \(r(0)=0\), the error also tends to zero along changes for which k is sometimes exactly zero.

Also, differentiability of g gives

\[\frac{k}{h}=\frac{g(a+h)-g(a)}h\longrightarrow g'(a).\]

Small step 7 · We still have \(r(k)\,k/h\). Why must it tend to zero?

Check this step

The factor \(k/h\) tends to the finite number \(g'(a)\), so it stays bounded for sufficiently small nonzero h. For example, eventually \(|k/h|\le |g'(a)|+1\). Hence

\[\left|r(k)\frac{k}{h}\right| \le (|g'(a)|+1)|r(k)|\longrightarrow0.\]

This is the precise reason the error disappears. We did not merely assume that every product of a small number and another factor is small.

Taking the limit now gives

\[\boxed{(f\circ g)'(a)=f'(b)g'(a)=f'(g(a))g'(a).}\]

The proof includes a constant inner function and any other case where k vanishes. Only differentiability at the stated points was used.

Act 4: See the two stages at work

Clue 3 · For \(F(x)=\sin(x^2)\), which derivative follows the actual inputs?

Hint: The input to \(\sin u\) is \(u=x^2\). Evaluate its derivative there, then multiply by the inner rate.

Read the derivative from the inside out
\[g(x)=x^2,\quad g'(x)=2x;\qquad f(u)=\sin u,\quad f'(u)=\cos u.\]
\[F'(x)=f'(g(x))g'(x)=\cos(x^2)\cdot2x.\]

At a particular point a, this becomes \(\cos(a^2)\cdot2a\).

Clue 4 · If \(g'(a)=0\), what does the chain rule allow us to conclude?

Hint: Try \(g(x)=x^2\), \(f(u)=u\), at \(a=0\).

Zero rate at one point is not local constancy
\[f(g(x))=x^2,\qquad (f\circ g)'(0)=1\cdot0=0.\]

Yet (x^2>0) whenever (x e0), while the value at 0 is 0. The function is not constant on any interval around 0. In the lab, with outer function (u^2), the composite (x^4) gives another example.

Part II · Fermat’s theorem

Act 5: What must happen at an interior peak?

A local maximum means f(c) is at least as large as all sufficiently nearby values. A local minimum reverses that comparison. Either is called a local extremum.

If c is an interior point, f has a local maximum or minimum there, and f is differentiable at c, then

\[\boxed{f'(c)=0.}\]

“Interior” means we can approach c from both sides within the domain.

Clue 5 · We want to use the derivative definition at a local maximum. What is our first useful observation?

Hint: Compare function values first. The sign of the quotient comes later.

Small step 8 · Does “local maximum” mean f(c) is the largest value over the entire domain?

Check this step

No. It only compares f(c) with values close enough to c. That is enough: a derivative also depends only on nearby values.

Write the difference quotient:

\[q(h)=\frac{f(c+h)-f(c)}{h},\qquad h\ne0.\]

Small step 9 · The numerator is nonpositive. What sign does q(h) have when h is positive?

Check this step
\[h>0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad q(h)\le0.\]

Dividing by a positive number keeps the inequality direction.

Small step 10 · What changes when h is negative? Why?

Check this step
\[h<0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad q(h)\ge0.\]

Dividing by a negative number reverses the inequality. For example, ((-0.04)/(-0.2)=0.2).

At a local maximumFrom the left: h < 0From the right: h > 0
Value changeNonpositiveNonpositive
Difference quotientNonnegativeNonpositive

These are secant slopes joining \((c,f(c))\) to a nearby graph point. The left and right slopes have opposite weak sign restrictions.

Act 6: Make the two limits agree

Clue 6 · What does differentiability at c add to the sign information?

Hint: The common limit has to be both nonnegative and nonpositive.

\[\lim_{h\to0^-}q(h)=f'(c)\ge0,\qquad \lim_{h\to0^+}q(h)=f'(c)\le0.\]

Small step 11 · Which real number can be both at least zero and at most zero?

Check this step
\[f'(c)\ge0\text{ and }f'(c)\le0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad f'(c)=0.\]

The nearby quotients need not be zero. Their shared limit must be.

Small step 12 · At a local minimum, how do the signs change?

Check this step
\[f(c+h)-f(c)\ge0.\]
\[h<0\Rightarrow q(h)\le0,\qquad h>0\Rightarrow q(h)\ge0.\]

The common derivative is again forced to be both nonpositive and nonnegative. It is zero.

We have proved

\[\boxed{\text{differentiable interior extremum}\ \Longrightarrow\ f'(c)=0.}\]

At such a point, the tangent is horizontal. The proof uses both sides and the existence of one shared derivative.

Act 8: Which conditions can we drop?

Clue 7 · Why do the endpoint and corner examples not contradict Fermat’s theorem?

Hint: Check the hypotheses before checking the conclusion.

An endpoint needs only one side

For \(f(x)=x\) restricted to \([0,1]\), the minimum is at 0 and the maximum is at 1. The interior derivative is 1, and the available one-sided endpoint derivatives are also 1.

The formula x extends to a differentiable function on the whole real line with derivative 1, but then 0 and 1 are no longer local extrema. On the restricted interval, we do not have both sides at the endpoints.

Small step 13 · For \(f(x)=|x|\) at 0, calculate both one-sided quotients. Why is a minimum not enough?

Check this step
\[\frac{|h|-|0|}{h}= \begin{cases}-1,&h<0,\\1,&h>0.\end{cases}\]

There is a minimum, but the limits disagree. No two-sided derivative exists, so Fermat’s differentiability condition fails.

Clue 8 · We have found \(f'(c)=0\). Have we proved that c is a maximum or minimum?

Hint: Compare \(x^2\), \(-x^2\), and \(x^3\) at 0.

Small step 14 · For \(f(x)=x^3\), verify the zero derivative and check values on both sides.

Check this step
\[f'(0)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{h^3}{h} =\lim_{h\to0}h^2=0.\]
\[h<0\Rightarrow h^3<0=f(0),\qquad h>0\Rightarrow h^3>0=f(0).\]

Every neighborhood contains values smaller and larger than f(0). Thus 0 is neither a local maximum nor a local minimum. Its tangent is horizontal, but the graph keeps increasing.

Keep the direction of the statement: A differentiable interior extremum has zero derivative. A zero derivative alone does not prove an extremum.

This is the part used in Rolle’s theorem: once an extremum has been found strictly inside the interval, differentiability makes its derivative zero.
