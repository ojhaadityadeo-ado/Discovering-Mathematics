MATH DETECTIVE: CASE FILE #15

Subject: Quadratic Equations & Vieta's Theorem

Mission: Decide what truly makes an equation quadratic, expose its factors from its roots, and discover why the coefficients already know the roots' sum and product.

Detective Briefing: Let \(P(x)=ax^2+bx+c\), with \(a,b,c\in\mathbb R\) or \(\mathbb C\). This investigation uses a small number of decisive questions. Read the evidence between them: each answer should follow from a definition or an earlier clue.

Act 1: Is It Really Quadratic?

Initial Description
A quadratic equation in one variable has the standard form \[ ax^2+bx+c=0, \] where \(a,b,c\) are known constants. Its degree must be exactly \(2\): the highest power of \(x\) with a non-zero coefficient must be \(x^2\).

Can \(a\) be zero in a quadratic equation?

🕵️ Detective's Note: Ask what happens to the only second-degree term when its coefficient is zero.

Why is that answer forced by the definition rather than merely adopted as a convention?

🕵️ Detective's Note: The deepest reason comes from degree; legal division by \(a\) will be a consequence.
Follow \(a=0\) to Every Possible Ending
Then \(ax^2+bx+c=0\) reduces to \(bx+c=0\):
\(b\neq0\)It is linear and has the single solution \(x=-c/b\).
\(b=0,\ c\neq0\)It says \(c=0\), a contradiction; there is no solution.
\(b=0,\ c=0\)It becomes \(0=0\), an identity true for every \(x\).
None of these is a quadratic equation.

A student writes \(0x^2+5x-1=0\) and says, “It contains \(x^2\), so it is quadratic.” What is the precise flaw?

🕵️ Detective's Note: Degree depends on non-zero coefficients, not on symbols that disappear after simplification.

In your own words, follow the equation through all three possibilities when \(a=0\), and explain why none remains quadratic.

Act 2: Normalize Without Moving a Single Zero

Because \(a\neq0\), division by \(a\) is legal:

\[ ax^2+bx+c=0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x^2+\frac ba x+\frac ca=0. \] Calling \(p=\frac ba\) and \(q=\frac ca\), we obtain the monic equation \[ x^2+px+q=0. \] “Monic” means that the leading coefficient is \(1\).

What can we say about the zeros of this new quadratic equation? Do they change?

🕵️ Detective's Note: Test what happens specifically when the polynomial value is zero.

If the zeros do not change, why not?

🕵️ Detective's Note: The polynomial values are generally scaled, not equal. Focus on the condition “equals zero.”
Zero-Preserving Scale
For every candidate root \(r\), \[ ar^2+br+c=0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad r^2+\frac ba r+\frac ca=0. \] Dividing by \(a\) vertically scales the graph \(y=P(x)\), but a vertical scale by a non-zero number cannot move an \(x\)-intercept.

Which statement accurately records the normalized equation and its relation to the original?

🕵️ Detective's Note: Divide every coefficient by \(a\), while distinguishing equal zeros from equal function values.

Explain carefully why division by \(a\) changes the height of most graph points but cannot create, remove, or move a zero.

Act 3: How Many Roots Can Hide Here?

Geometric Evidence
The real roots of \(P(x)=0\) are precisely the \(x\)-coordinates where the parabola \(y=P(x)\) meets the horizontal line \(y=0\), the \(x\)-axis. Non-real complex roots do not appear as points on the ordinary real Cartesian plane.

What does the statement \(P(\alpha)=0\) mean geometrically when \(\alpha\) is real?

🕵️ Detective's Note: Substituting \(x=\alpha\) gives the vertical coordinate \(y=0\).

Could a genuine quadratic have three distinct roots?

🕵️ Detective's Note: Test a supposed third distinct root against the two-root factorization.
Suppose \(\alpha,\beta,\gamma\) were three distinct roots. Once \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) are accounted for, \[ P(x)=a(x-\alpha)(x-\beta). \] Then the third root would require \[ 0=P(\gamma)=a(\gamma-\alpha)(\gamma-\beta). \]

Where exactly does the three-root claim collapse?

🕵️ Detective's Note: \(a\neq0\), while distinctness gives \(\gamma-\alpha\neq0\) and \(\gamma-\beta\neq0\).
Equation Versus Identity
If an expression \(ax^2+bx+c\) vanishes for more than two distinct values, it cannot be a genuine quadratic. The argument forces \(a=0\); two distinct zeros of the remaining \(bx+c\) then force \(b=0\), and finally \(c=0\). What remains is the zero-polynomial identity \(0=0\), true for every \(x\)—not a quadratic equation.

Retell the three-root contradiction without relying only on the picture of a parabola.

Act 4: Make the Factors Confess

Let \(\alpha\) be a root. Why is the apparently strange move \(P(x)=P(x)-P(\alpha)\) legitimate—and useful?

🕵️ Detective's Note: Notice both facts: \(P(\alpha)=0\), and differences such as \(x^2-\alpha^2\) and \(x-\alpha\).
\[ \begin{aligned} P(x) &=P(x)-P(\alpha)\\ &=(ax^2+bx+c)-(a\alpha^2+b\alpha+c)\\ &=a(x^2-\alpha^2)+b(x-\alpha)\\ &=(x-\alpha)\big[a(x+\alpha)+b\big]\\ &=(x-\alpha)\big[ax+(a\alpha+b)\big]. \end{aligned} \] The constants cancel, and \(x^2-\alpha^2=(x-\alpha)(x+\alpha)\).

What structural fact has this derivation uncovered from the single condition \(P(\alpha)=0\)?

🕵️ Detective's Note: The whole polynomial is now written as \(x-\alpha\) multiplied by a linear expression.
Bring in the Second Root
If \(\beta\neq\alpha\) is also a root, then \[ 0=P(\beta)=(\beta-\alpha)\big[a(\beta+\alpha)+b\big]. \] Since \(\beta-\alpha\neq0\), the bracket must vanish: \[ a(\beta+\alpha)+b=0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad a\alpha+b=-a\beta. \] Hence \[ ax+(a\alpha+b)=ax-a\beta=a(x-\beta). \]

Which conclusion now follows, and why is the leading factor \(a\) still necessary?

🕵️ Detective's Note: The product \((x-\alpha)(x-\beta)\) is monic; multiply by what to recover the coefficient of \(x^2\)?
Repeated-Root Check
If \(\alpha=\beta\) is a double root, we do not divide by \(\beta-\alpha=0\). Instead, the remaining linear quotient also vanishes at \(\alpha\), giving \(2a\alpha+b=0\). The same formula remains valid: \[ P(x)=a(x-\alpha)^2=a(x-\alpha)(x-\beta). \]

Reconstruct how one root produces one factor and how the second root identifies the remaining linear factor.

Act 5: Let the Coefficients Reveal the Roots

Expand the factored form: \[ \begin{aligned} P(x) &=a(x-\alpha)(x-\beta)\\ &=a\big[x^2-(\alpha+\beta)x+\alpha\beta\big]\\ &=ax^2-a(\alpha+\beta)x+a\alpha\beta. \end{aligned} \] But the same polynomial was originally \(ax^2+bx+c\).

Why does equality of these two expressions allow us to match their \(x\)-coefficients and constant terms?

🕵️ Detective's Note: This is a polynomial identity, not an equation known only at the roots.
Coefficient Interrogation
Matching the coefficients of \(x\) gives \[ -a(\alpha+\beta)=b. \] Matching the constant terms gives \[ a\alpha\beta=c. \] Since \(a\neq0\), both equations may be divided by \(a\).

What do the coefficients finally confess about the roots?

🕵️ Detective's Note: Preserve the minus sign in the \(x\)-coefficient; the constant term carries no new minus sign.
Vieta's Evidence Card
\[ \boxed{\alpha+\beta=-\frac ba}, \qquad \boxed{\alpha\beta=\frac ca}. \] For the equivalent monic equation \(x^2+px+q=0\), where \(p=b/a\) and \(q=c/a\), \[ \boxed{\alpha+\beta=-p}, \qquad \boxed{\alpha\beta=q}. \]

Without solving \(2x^2-7x+3=0\), use Vieta to find the sum and product of its roots. Then explain why the sum is positive even though the coefficient of \(x\) is negative.

Final Report: Close the Case

\[ \boxed{ \begin{gathered} a\neq0 \Longrightarrow x^2+\frac ba x+\frac ca=0 \text{ has the same zeros},\\[2mm] P(x)=a(x-\alpha)(x-\beta) \Longrightarrow \alpha+\beta=-\frac ba,\quad \alpha\beta=\frac ca. \end{gathered}} \]

Proof Skeleton to Remember
The condition \(a\neq0\) protects the degree and licenses division. Division by \(a\) scales polynomial values but preserves every zero. Each root creates a linear factor. Expanding those factors and comparing the resulting polynomial identity with \(ax^2+bx+c\) forces Vieta's formulas.
