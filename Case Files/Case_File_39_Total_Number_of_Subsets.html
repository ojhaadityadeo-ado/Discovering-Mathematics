MATH DETECTIVE: CASE FILE #39

Subject: The Total Number of Subsets

Mission: Count all subsets of an n-element set in two different ways. First make an include/exclude decision for each element. Then regroup the same subsets by their sizes. Finally verify the identity with the binomial theorem, one algebraic step at a time.

The case: A café offers n optional toppings. A customer may choose any subset of those toppings—including no toppings at all. How many different topping selections are possible?

Follow 7 clues and the small-step questions. Try each clue before opening its reasoning. The subset lab works offline; equation formatting and display fonts use an internet connection.

Act 1: One decision for each topping

Suppose the toppings are fixed and distinct. For every topping, the customer must decide whether that topping belongs to the selection.

What are the only two possibilities for one topping?

Clue 1 · What decision is made for each topping?

Follow this clue: For a fixed topping, ask whether it appears in the chosen set.
Open the reasoning

Each topping either belongs to the chosen subset or does not. There is no third possibility. These two outcomes are exactly “include” and “exclude.”

Small step 1. If there are 3 toppings, how many include/exclude positions are there in one complete decision pattern?

Open the answer

There are 3 positions—one decision for each topping. For example, include/exclude/include is one complete pattern.

Act 2: Use the multiplication principle

Each of the n toppings has 2 permitted choices. So the total number of complete include/exclude patterns is

\[\underbrace{2\cdot2\cdot\ldots\cdot2}_{n\text{ factors}}=2^n.\]
Clue 2 · Why do we multiply the 2's?

Follow this clue: This is a counting rule, not a statement about customer behavior.
Open the reasoning

The multiplication principle says that if a process has 2 choices at each of n stages, and every choice at one stage can be followed by every choice at the next, then there are 2×2×⋯×2 complete outcomes.

Clue 3 · Does “independent choices” require a probability assumption here?

Follow this clue: We are counting possible selections, not measuring how often people make them.
Important distinction: “Independent” here is being used in the counting sense: each topping may be included or excluded without forbidding any choice for another topping. No randomness or probability model is required.

Act 3: Organize the same subsets by size

Now count the same topping selections in a different way. Instead of recording the full include/exclude pattern first, group selections according to how many toppings they contain.

Clue 4 · How many selections contain exactly r toppings?

Follow this clue: We are choosing which r toppings appear, and order does not matter.
Open the reasoning

A selection with exactly r toppings is an r-element subset of the n toppings. The number of such subsets is \(\binom nr\).

Possible subset sizes are

\[0,1,2,\ldots,n.\]

So the groups contain

\[\binom n0,\binom n1,\binom n2,\ldots,\binom nn\]

subsets respectively.

Small step 2. Can one subset belong to two different size groups?

Open the answer

No. A subset has one definite number of elements. A 2-topping selection cannot also be a 3-topping selection.

Act 4: Put all size groups together

Every subset belongs to exactly one size group, and the groups do not overlap. Therefore the total number of subsets is the sum of the counts of all the size groups:

\[\binom n0+\binom n1+\binom n2+\cdots+\binom nn=\sum_{r=0}^{n}\binom nr.\]

But Act 2 counted the same full collection as \(2^n\). Hence

\[\boxed{\sum_{r=0}^{n}\binom nr=2^n}.\]
Clue 5 · Why are we allowed to add the size-group counts?

Follow this clue: The size groups form a partition of the full collection of subsets.
The counting proof: We counted one collection in two ways. Include/exclude decisions give \(2^n\). Grouping by subset size gives \(\sum_{r=0}^{n}\binom nr\). Because both counts refer to the same collection, they are equal.

Small step 3. In the lab, what do the size-group counts add up to?

Open the answer

They add up to the total number of subsets, which is \(2^n\). The lab is displaying the identity numerically for the chosen value of n.

Act 5: Stress-test the empty subset and n = 0

Why must the empty subset be included?

“Choose no toppings” is a genuine permitted selection. It is the unique subset of size 0, so

\[\binom n0=1.\]
Clue 6 · What happens if we deliberately exclude the empty subset?

Follow this clue: You are removing exactly one valid subset from the full collection.

The extreme case n = 0

If there are no toppings, there are no inclusion decisions to make. Yet there is still one subset: the empty subset.

\[\sum_{r=0}^{0}\binom 0r=\binom00=1=2^0.\]

Small step 4. Why is \(2^0=1\) consistent with the multiplication principle?

Open the answer

With zero decision stages there is exactly one complete pattern: the empty pattern. This is the counting reason behind the empty product being 1.

Act 6: Follow the algebraic proof in detail

The counting argument already proves the identity. Now verify it algebraically using the binomial theorem.

Step 1 · Start with the binomial theorem.
\[(x+y)^n=\sum_{r=0}^{n}\binom nr x^{\,n-r}y^r.\]
What this says: When \((x+y)^n\) is expanded, the coefficient of \(x^{n-r}y^r\) is \(\binom nr\).

Small step 5. In the binomial theorem, what values does r run through?

Open the answer

It runs through every integer from 0 to n. Those are exactly the possible numbers of y-factors chosen from the n copies of \((x+y)\).

Step 2 · Substitute x = 1 and y = 1.
\[(1+1)^n=\sum_{r=0}^{n}\binom nr 1^{\,n-r}1^r.\]
Why is substitution allowed? The binomial theorem is an identity in x and y, so it remains true for the particular values x=1 and y=1.

Small step 6. After substituting x = y = 1, what happens to the powers \(1^{n-r}\) and \(1^r\)?

Open the answer

Each equals 1. Any nonnegative integer power of 1 is 1, including \(1^0=1\).

Step 3 · Simplify the left-hand side.
\[(1+1)^n=2^n.\]
Why? First add inside the parentheses: 1+1=2. Then raise the result to the nth power.
Step 4 · Simplify every term on the right-hand side.

Since

\[1^{\,n-r}=1\qquad\text{and}\qquad 1^r=1,\]

each summand becomes

\[\binom nr 1^{\,n-r}1^r=\binom nr\cdot1\cdot1=\binom nr.\]

Therefore

\[\sum_{r=0}^{n}\binom nr 1^{\,n-r}1^r=\sum_{r=0}^{n}\binom nr.\]
What disappeared? Only factors equal to 1 were removed. The binomial coefficients themselves remain unchanged.

Small step 7. Why does substituting 1 for both x and y isolate the sum of the coefficients?

Open the answer

Because every monomial factor becomes 1. So each term contributes only its coefficient \(\binom nr\), and adding all terms becomes the sum of all binomial coefficients.

Step 5 · Put the two simplified sides together.

The left side became \(2^n\), while the right side became \(\sum_{r=0}^{n}\binom nr\). Hence

\[2^n=\sum_{r=0}^{n}\binom nr.\]

Reversing the two sides gives the usual presentation:

\[\boxed{\sum_{r=0}^{n}\binom nr=2^n}.\]

Small step 8. Does this algebraic proof require n > 0?

Open the answer

No. For n=0, the binomial theorem gives \((1+1)^0=\binom00 1^0 1^0\), so \(1=1\). The identity still holds.

Full algebra in one continuous chain

\[ \begin{aligned} (x+y)^n &=\sum_{r=0}^{n}\binom nr x^{\,n-r}y^r\\[4pt] (1+1)^n &=\sum_{r=0}^{n}\binom nr 1^{\,n-r}1^r\\[4pt] 2^n &=\sum_{r=0}^{n}\binom nr\\[4pt] \therefore\qquad \sum_{r=0}^{n}\binom nr &=2^n. \end{aligned} \]

Act 7: Read the identity as a counting statement

The identity does not merely say that two formulas happen to be equal. It says that the same collection of subsets can be organized in two different ways.

ViewpointHow the subsets are countedTotal
Decision patternFor each of n elements: include or exclude.\(2^n\)
Subset sizeFor each r from 0 to n: choose exactly r elements.\(\sum_{r=0}^{n}\binom nr\)
Clue 7 · What does the identity ultimately express?

Follow this clue: One count uses binary decisions; the other uses subset sizes.

Final case report

For every nonnegative integer n,

\[\boxed{\sum_{r=0}^{n}\binom nr=2^n}.\]

Why it makes sense: Every element has two allowed states: included or excluded.

Why the sum appears: The same subsets can be partitioned by their sizes r=0,1,…,n.

Why the empty subset matters: It is the unique subset of size 0 and contributes the term \(\binom n0=1\).

Why n=0 works: The empty set has exactly one subset—namely itself—so \(2^0=1\).

Why the algebra works: In the binomial theorem, setting x=y=1 turns every monomial factor into 1, leaving only the sum of the binomial coefficients.
