MATH DETECTIVE: CASE FILE #38

Subject: Pascal's Identity

Mission: Prove that an r-person committee chosen from n+1 candidates can be counted in two disjoint cases: Alice is excluded or Alice is included. Then verify every algebraic step carefully with factorials.

The case: Choose an r-person committee from n+1 candidates. One candidate is Alice. Instead of counting all committees at once, track just one fact: is Alice on the committee?

Follow 7 clues and the small-step questions. Try each clue before opening its reasoning. The committee lab works offline; equation formatting and display fonts use an internet connection.

Act 1: Split the committees into two cases

Let n be a nonnegative integer. We have n+1 candidates in total: Alice and n other candidates. We want a committee of r people, where normally

\[0\le r\le n+1.\]

What are the only two possibilities for Alice?

Clue 1 · Which partition is complete?

Follow this clue: A committee either contains Alice or does not contain Alice. There is no third possibility.
Open the reasoning

Every committee falls into exactly one of two classes:

\[\text{Alice excluded}\qquad\text{or}\qquad\text{Alice included}.\]

The cases are exhaustive because one of them must happen. They are disjoint because a single committee cannot both contain and not contain Alice.

Small step 1. Why is “disjoint” important when we add the two counts?

Open the answer

If a committee appeared in both cases, adding the case totals would count it twice. Here that cannot happen, so addition counts every committee exactly once.

Act 2: Count the committees that exclude Alice

If Alice is excluded, all r committee members must come from the n people other than Alice.

Clue 2 · How many committees exclude Alice?

Follow this clue: Once Alice is unavailable, how many candidates remain, and how many must be chosen?
Open the reasoning

After excluding Alice, n candidates remain. We must choose all r members from these n candidates, so the number of committees is

\[\boxed{\binom nr}.\]

Small step 2. In the excluding-Alice case, have we made any decision about the order of the r people?

Open the answer

No. A committee is a set, not an ordered list. The same r people written in a different order do not form a new committee.

Act 3: Count the committees that include Alice

If Alice is included, one of the r committee memberships has already been settled. We do not choose Alice again.

Clue 3 · After Alice is included, what remains to be chosen?

Follow this clue: The final committee has r people total, and Alice already occupies one membership.
Open the reasoning

Alice contributes one member. Therefore only r−1 members still need to be chosen, all from the remaining n candidates:

\[\boxed{\binom n{r-1}}.\]
Clue 4 · Why is there no extra factor of r?

Follow this clue: Ask whether “Alice, Ben, Chen” and “Ben, Alice, Chen” are different committees.
Open the reasoning

There is no “slot” to assign to Alice. We are selecting an unordered set. Once the set contains Alice, nothing remains to be decided about where Alice sits in the committee. Multiplying by r would incorrectly treat different written positions as different committees.

Act 4: Put the two cases together

The two cases do not overlap, and together they contain every r-person committee from the n+1 candidates.

\[\underbrace{\binom nr}_{\text{Alice excluded}}+\underbrace{\binom n{r-1}}_{\text{Alice included}}=\underbrace{\binom{n+1}{r}}_{\text{all r-person committees}}.\]
Clue 5 · Why are we allowed to add?

Follow this clue: Every committee appears once in exactly one of the two cases.
The counting proof: One collection of committees has been partitioned into two non-overlapping collections. Counting the whole collection equals the sum of the counts of the two parts.

Small step 3. What is Pascal's triangle recording here?

Open the answer

Each entry on level n+1 counts a collection that can be split according to whether the new candidate is excluded or included. The two entries above are exactly the counts of those two parts.

Act 5: Stress-test the boundary cases

Does the identity still make sense when the committee size is at an edge?

Use the standard convention

\[\binom nk=0\quad\text{when }k<0\text{ or }k>n.\]

Boundary 1: r = 0

There is exactly one 0-person committee: the empty committee. It excludes Alice.

\[\binom n0+\binom n{-1}=1+0=1=\binom{n+1}{0}.\]

Boundary 2: r = n+1

There is exactly one committee of size n+1: the full set of candidates. It includes Alice.

\[\binom n{n+1}+\binom nn=0+1=1=\binom{n+1}{n+1}.\]
Clue 6 · At r = n+1, which case contributes the one full committee?

Follow this clue: A committee containing everybody certainly contains Alice.

Act 6: Follow the factorial proof in detail

The counting argument already proves Pascal's identity. Now verify the same identity algebraically. For the factorial calculation, first assume the interior range

\[1\le r\le n.\]

This keeps every factorial below defined by the ordinary formula without needing the outside-range convention.

Step 1 · Expand both binomial coefficients.
\[\binom nr+\binom n{r-1}=\frac{n!}{r!(n-r)!}+\frac{n!}{(r-1)!\,[n-(r-1)]!}.\]
Why? We use \(\displaystyle \binom ab=\frac{a!}{b!(a-b)!}\) once with \(a=n,b=r\), and again with \(a=n,b=r-1\).
Step 2 · Simplify the second remaining-size expression.
\[n-(r-1)=n-r+1.\]

Therefore

\[\binom nr+\binom n{r-1}=\frac{n!}{r!(n-r)!}+\frac{n!}{(r-1)!(n-r+1)!}.\]
Why? Subtracting the bracket gives \(n-r+1\). The parentheses matter: \(n-(r-1)\neq n-r-1\).

Small step 4. After simplifying \(n-(r-1)\), what does the second denominator become?

Open the answer

It becomes \((r-1)!(n-r+1)!\). The second factorial changes because \(n-(r-1)=n-r+1\).

Step 3 · Decide on a common denominator.

The first denominator contains \(r!\) but only \((n-r)!\). The second contains \((n-r+1)!\) but only \((r-1)!\). A denominator containing the larger factorial from each side is

\[r!(n-r+1)!.\]
Why? Since \(r!=r(r-1)!\) and \((n-r+1)!=(n-r+1)(n-r)!\), this denominator is a multiple of both original denominators.
Step 4 · Rewrite the first fraction with the common denominator.

We need to change \((n-r)!\) into \((n-r+1)!\). Use

\[(n-r+1)!=(n-r+1)(n-r)!.\]

So multiply the first fraction by \(\dfrac{n-r+1}{n-r+1}\), which is 1:

\[ \frac{n!}{r!(n-r)!} =\frac{n!}{r!(n-r)!}\cdot\frac{n-r+1}{n-r+1} =\frac{n!(n-r+1)}{r!(n-r+1)!}. \]
Why is the value unchanged? We multiplied numerator and denominator by the same nonzero number. In the interior range, \(n-r+1\ge1\).
Step 5 · Rewrite the second fraction with the same denominator.

We need to change \((r-1)!\) into \(r!\). Use

\[r!=r(r-1)!.\]

Multiply the second fraction by \(\dfrac r r\), which is 1:

\[ \frac{n!}{(r-1)!(n-r+1)!} =\frac{n!}{(r-1)!(n-r+1)!}\cdot\frac r r =\frac{n!r}{r!(n-r+1)!}. \]
Why is the value unchanged? Again we multiplied by 1. Here \(r\ge1\), so division by r is valid.

Small step 5. Why does the first fraction need the factor \(\dfrac{n-r+1}{n-r+1}\), but not the factor \(\dfrac rr\)?

Open the answer

The first fraction already has \(r!\) in its denominator. What it is missing is the extra factor that turns \((n-r)!\) into \((n-r+1)!\). So only \(\dfrac{n-r+1}{n-r+1}\) is needed there.

Step 6 · Add the fractions.

Now the denominators are identical:

\[ \begin{aligned} \binom nr+\binom n{r-1} &=\frac{n!(n-r+1)}{r!(n-r+1)!}+\frac{n!r}{r!(n-r+1)!}\\ &=\frac{n!(n-r+1)+n!r}{r!(n-r+1)!}. \end{aligned} \]
Why can we add the numerators? Fractions with the same denominator add by keeping that denominator and adding their numerators.

Small step 6. Once the denominators match, what is the numerator of the combined fraction before factoring?

Open the answer

It is \(n!(n-r+1)+n!r\). Only after writing this sum do we factor out the common \(n!\).

Step 7 · Factor out n! from the numerator.
\[ \frac{n!(n-r+1)+n!r}{r!(n-r+1)!} =\frac{n!\big[(n-r+1)+r\big]}{r!(n-r+1)!}. \]
Why? Both numerator terms contain the common factor \(n!\). This is the reverse distributive law: \(ab+ac=a(b+c)\).
Step 8 · Simplify the bracket.
\[(n-r+1)+r=n+1.\]

Hence

\[\frac{n!\big[(n-r+1)+r\big]}{r!(n-r+1)!}=\frac{n!(n+1)}{r!(n-r+1)!}.\]
What happened to r? The terms \(-r\) and \(+r\) cancel.
Step 9 · Recognize the two factorials needed for the target.

First,

\[(n+1)n!=(n+1)!.\]

Second,

\[n-r+1=n+1-r.\]

Therefore

\[ \frac{n!(n+1)}{r!(n-r+1)!} =\frac{(n+1)!}{r!(n+1-r)!}. \]
Why is this the target form? The numerator is the factorial of the new total number of candidates, and the second denominator factorial counts how many of those n+1 candidates are not chosen.
Step 10 · Convert back to binomial notation.
\[ \frac{(n+1)!}{r!(n+1-r)!}=\binom{n+1}{r}. \]

Combining all steps gives

\[ \boxed{\binom nr+\binom n{r-1}=\binom{n+1}{r}}. \]

Full algebra in one continuous chain

\[ \begin{aligned} \binom nr+\binom n{r-1} &=\frac{n!}{r!(n-r)!}+\frac{n!}{(r-1)!(n-r+1)!}\\[4pt] &=\frac{n!(n-r+1)}{r!(n-r+1)!}+\frac{n!r}{r!(n-r+1)!}\\[4pt] &=\frac{n!(n-r+1)+n!r}{r!(n-r+1)!}\\[4pt] &=\frac{n!\big((n-r+1)+r\big)}{r!(n-r+1)!}\\[4pt] &=\frac{n!(n+1)}{r!(n-r+1)!}\\[4pt] &=\frac{(n+1)!}{r!(n+1-r)!}\\[4pt] &=\binom{n+1}{r}. \end{aligned} \]

Small step 7. Why did the detailed proof restrict first to \(1\le r\le n\)?

Open the answer

The transformations multiply by r/r and by (n−r+1)/(n−r+1). In the interior range these denominators are positive. The edge values r=0 and r=n+1 are easier to verify directly using the committee interpretation and the convention that out-of-range binomial coefficients are 0.

Act 7: Read Pascal's triangle as a counting story

Suppose a row for n candidates is already known. Add one new candidate—Alice—to make n+1 candidates. For a fixed committee size r, every committee at the new level comes from exactly one of two sources:

CaseDecisionCount
Alice excludedChoose all r members from the old n candidates.\(\binom nr\)
Alice includedChoose Alice, then choose r−1 more from the old n.\(\binom n{r-1}\)
TotalAll r-person committees from n+1 candidates.\(\binom{n+1}{r}\)
Clue 7 · What does “add the two entries above” really mean?

Follow this clue: The two upper entries count two non-overlapping types of committees at the next level.

Final case report

For nonnegative n and integer r, with the usual convention that \(\binom nk=0\) outside \(0\le k\le n\),

\[\boxed{\binom nr+\binom n{r-1}=\binom{n+1}{r}}.\]

Why it makes sense: Track Alice. Every r-person committee either excludes her or includes her.

Why the counts are correct: Excluding Alice gives \(\binom nr\); including Alice leaves \(r-1\) places, giving \(\binom n{r-1}\).

Why there is no factor r: A committee is unordered, so Alice has no position to choose.

Why Pascal's triangle adds: The entry below counts a whole collection split into two disjoint cases counted by the entries above.

Why the algebra works: Put the two factorial fractions over the common denominator \(r!(n-r+1)!\). The resulting numerator simplifies to \(n!(n+1)=(n+1)!\).
