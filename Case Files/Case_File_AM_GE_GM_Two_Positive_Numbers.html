MATH DETECTIVE: CASE FILE

Subject: AM ≥ GM for Two Positive Numbers

Mission: Prove that the arithmetic mean of two positive real numbers is at least their geometric mean, but do it by uncovering why a nonnegative square is the right starting point, how each algebraic move transforms that fact toward the target, and exactly when equality can occur.

The case: For positive real numbers a and b, define the arithmetic mean and geometric mean by \(A=\frac{a+b}{2}\) and \(G=\sqrt{ab}\). We want to establish \(A\ge G\) without assuming it in advance.

Follow 7 clues and the small-step questions. Several prompts ask what the next immediate move should be and what that move contributes to the global proof. Try each before opening its reasoning.

Act 1: Identify the target before choosing a method

We are comparing

\[A=\frac{a+b}{2},\qquad G=\sqrt{ab}.\]

The desired inequality is

\[\boxed{\frac{a+b}{2}\ge\sqrt{ab}}.\]
Clue 1 · What makes the two sides difficult to compare directly?

Follow this clue: Compare the algebraic shapes of \(a+b\) and \(\sqrt{ab}\).
Open the reasoning

The expressions have different forms. One is additive, the other multiplicative with a square root. A useful proof strategy is therefore to search for an identity that naturally produces both \(a+b\) and \(2\sqrt{ab}\) in the same expression.

Small step 1. If we cleared the denominator 2 in the target, what equivalent inequality would we want?

Open the answer

Because 2 is positive, multiplying both sides by 2 preserves the inequality. The target becomes \(a+b\ge2\sqrt{ab}\). This form hints at the expansion of a square involving \(\sqrt a\) and \(\sqrt b\).

Act 2: Manufacture something that cannot be negative

What kind of expression do we know is always nonnegative without needing any special comparison theorem?

Clue 2 · Which starting point gives us a guaranteed inequality?

Follow this clue: Which elementary expression can never be negative for real inputs?

Take

\[(\sqrt a-\sqrt b)^2.\]

Since it is the square of a real number,

\[\boxed{(\sqrt a-\sqrt b)^2\ge0}.\]

Small step 2. Why choose \(\sqrt a-\sqrt b\), rather than simply \(a-b\)?

Open the answer

Squaring \(\sqrt a-\sqrt b\) produces \(a+b-2\sqrt{ab}\), which contains exactly the two structures in our target: the sum \(a+b\) and the product term \(\sqrt{ab}\). Squaring \(a-b\) would instead produce \(a^2-2ab+b^2\), which is farther from the desired inequality.

Global purpose of the move: We have converted the problem from “compare two unfamiliar means” into “expand an expression already known to be nonnegative.” The proof will now be algebraic rearrangement rather than guesswork.

Act 3: Expand the square and look for the target

Clue 3 · What should the immediate next step be?

Follow this clue: The point of choosing this square was what its expansion would reveal.
Step 1 · Expand.
\[ (\sqrt a-\sqrt b)^2 =a-2\sqrt a\sqrt b+b. \]
Why is this useful? The middle term joins the square roots. Since \(a,b>0\), \(\sqrt a\sqrt b=\sqrt{ab}\), so the target expression appears.
Step 2 · Rewrite the product of square roots.
\[ a-2\sqrt a\sqrt b+b =a-2\sqrt{ab}+b. \]

Since the original square is nonnegative,

\[ a-2\sqrt{ab}+b\ge0. \]

Small step 3. Which part of the desired inequality is now visible?

Open the answer

Both parts are present: \(a+b\) and \(2\sqrt{ab}\). We are now only one rearrangement away from \(a+b\ge2\sqrt{ab}\).

Small step 4. Why was the expansion step important for the global result?

Open the answer

It transformed the universal fact “a square is nonnegative” into an inequality containing exactly the expressions we need to compare. Without this connection, nonnegativity of the square would not yet say anything about AM and GM.

Act 4: Rearrange toward the means

From the previous act,

\[a-2\sqrt{ab}+b\ge0.\]
Clue 4 · What rearrangement takes us closest to the target?

Follow this clue: The desired intermediate target is \(a+b\ge2\sqrt{ab}\).
Step 3 · Isolate the comparison.
\[ a+b\ge2\sqrt{ab}. \]
Why is this the key bridge? The left side is twice the arithmetic mean, and the right side is twice the geometric mean. The inequality between the means is now visible after one common positive factor is removed.

Small step 5. What should the immediate next step be now?

Open the answer

Divide both sides by 2, because the definitions of AM and GM differ from the current expressions by exactly that common factor.

Step 4 · Divide by 2.
\[ \frac{a+b}{2}\ge\sqrt{ab}. \]
Why does the inequality direction stay the same? We divide by the positive number 2. Dividing an inequality by a positive number preserves its direction.

Recognizing the definitions,

\[ \boxed{A\ge G}. \]
Main result: For positive real numbers \(a,b\),
\[ \boxed{\frac{a+b}{2}\ge\sqrt{ab}}. \]
The proof came entirely from the nonnegativity of \((\sqrt a-\sqrt b)^2\).

Act 5: Find exactly when equality occurs

The inequality is proved, but a complete inequality proof should also ask when equality can occur.

Clue 5 · Where in the proof could a strict inequality become equality?

Follow this clue: All later steps were equivalence-preserving rearrangements. Trace equality back to the first inequality.

Equality in AM ≥ GM requires

\[(\sqrt a-\sqrt b)^2=0.\]

A real square is zero exactly when its base is zero:

\[ \sqrt a-\sqrt b=0. \]
Hence

\[\sqrt a=\sqrt b.\]
Squaring both sides gives

\[\boxed{a=b}.\]

Small step 6. Why can we trace equality all the way back to the first square?

Open the answer

After the initial inequality, every later operation—expanding an identity, rearranging, and dividing by positive 2—preserves equivalence. So equality at the end occurs exactly when the starting square was zero.

Small step 7. What happens to the two means when a=b=c?

Open the answer

Then \(A=(c+c)/2=c\) and \(G=\sqrt{c^2}=c\) for positive c. So the equality condition is consistent with the definitions.

Clue 6 · What does the equality condition tell us conceptually?

Follow this clue: Equality required \(\sqrt a=\sqrt b\).

Small step 8. If a and b move farther apart, what happens to \((\sqrt a-\sqrt b)^2\)?

Open the answer

It becomes positive rather than zero. The starting square therefore records the failure of equality: unequal inputs create a positive gap that ultimately yields strict AM > GM.

Small step 9. Does the proof merely show that AM and GM are different?

Open the answer

No. It establishes an ordered comparison: AM can never fall below GM for positive a and b, and equality is completely characterized by a=b.

Act 7: Stress-test the proof

Clue 7 · Which numerical pair should give equality?

Follow this clue: Equality occurs exactly when a=b.

Stress-test A · Unequal positive numbers

Take \(a=1\) and \(b=9\). Then

\[ A=\frac{1+9}{2}=5, \qquad G=\sqrt9=3. \]

Indeed,

\[5>3.\]

The starting square is

\[(1-3)^2=4>0,\]

so strict inequality is exactly what we should expect.

Stress-test B · Equal positive numbers

Take \(a=b=9\). Then

\[ A=\frac{9+9}{2}=9, \qquad G=\sqrt{81}=9. \]

The starting square is

\[(3-3)^2=0,\]

so equality occurs.

Small step 10. Why are numerical checks useful if the symbolic proof is already complete?

Open the answer

They test whether the symbolic conclusion behaves as expected in both the strict and equality cases. They do not replace the proof, but they make the equality condition and the role of the starting square concrete.

Boundary note: The stated theorem assumes positive real numbers. The same square argument also works for nonnegative a and b, including zero, but the present case file keeps the theorem in the positive-real setting given in the statement.

Final case report

Target:

\[\boxed{\frac{a+b}{2}\ge\sqrt{ab}}.\]

Strategic starting point: choose a square whose expansion contains both sides of the desired comparison:

\[(\sqrt a-\sqrt b)^2\ge0.\]

Expansion:

\[a-2\sqrt{ab}+b\ge0.\]

Rearrangement:

\[a+b\ge2\sqrt{ab}.\]

Divide by 2:

\[\boxed{A\ge G}.\]

Equality: every later step preserves equivalence, so equality occurs exactly when

\[(\sqrt a-\sqrt b)^2=0\iff a=b.\]
