MATH DETECTIVE: CASE FILE #37

Subject: Symmetry of Binomial Coefficients

Mission: Find out why choosing who goes and choosing who stays give equal numbers of possible lists. Prove it by pairing groups, then check every step using factorials.

The case: From n applicants, a school selects r students for a trip. One teacher records the selected students. Another records the rejected students. Would either teacher have more possible lists to record?

Follow 7 clues and the small-step questions. Try each question before opening its answer. The visual lab and answer checking work offline; equation formatting and display fonts use an internet connection. Notes stay on this page only—print them before closing if you want to keep them.

Act 1: What are we counting?

Keep one fixed list of all applicants. Call this set U. Let n be a nonnegative integer and let r be an integer with \(0\le r\le n\).

The symbol \(\binom nr\), read “n choose r,” counts the groups of r applicants we can select. Order does not matter: the list “Asha, Ben” and the list “Ben, Asha” describe one group, not two.

Small step 1. Why do we need \(0\le r\le n\)?

Open the answer

We cannot select a negative number of applicants, or more applicants than there are. The values r = 0 and r = n are allowed: select nobody, or select everybody.

So we have to compare two collections of lists. What can we do without listing every possibility?

Try one decision first. Suppose U = {Asha, Ben, Chen, Diya, Esha}. The selected group is S = {Asha, Diya}.

Clue 1 · Can we find the rejected group without making another choice?

Follow this clue: Cross out the selected names from the full applicant list. Which names remain?
Open the reasoning

The decision already tells us both groups. Selecting Asha and Diya leaves Ben, Chen and Esha out. The rejected group is not an extra choice.

We call the group outside S its complement, written \(S^c\). Here the complement is always taken inside the fixed applicant set U.

\[S^c=U\setminus S,\qquad |S|=r,\qquad |S^c|=n-r.\]

The vertical bars here count members of a set. Every applicant belongs to exactly one of the two groups.

Small step 2. Why must we keep the full applicant list fixed?

Open the answer

If a sixth applicant, Farah, were added to U, the same selected group would leave a different rejected group. A complement makes sense only after we know the full set.

Act 2: Turn the list around

We have a way to turn a selected list into a rejected list. What must we check before saying the two collections have the same size?

We need an exact pairing: no two selected lists should lead to the same rejected list, and no possible rejected list should be left without a partner.

Small step 3. Predict before clicking: if you take the complement twice, which group should come back?

Open the answer

The original group. First keep exactly the applicants outside S. Then keep exactly the applicants outside that new group. This returns S: \((S^c)^c=S\).

Clue 2 · Could two different selected groups produce exactly the same rejected group?

Follow this clue: If the rejected names are known, can there be any doubt about which names remain selected?
Open the reasoning

Suppose \(S_1^c=S_2^c\). Take the complement of both sides:

\[(S_1^c)^c=(S_2^c)^c\quad\Longrightarrow\quad S_1=S_2.\]

So if the selected groups were different, their rejected groups could not be the same. No two starting groups share a partner.

Clue 3 · We have ruled out repeated partners. How can we check that no rejected group is missed?

Follow this clue: Run the rule backward, starting with any rejected group you want.
Open the reasoning

Take any rejected group R with \(n-r\) members. The applicants outside R form a selected group S with

\[n-(n-r)=n-n+r=r\]

members. The rejected group of this S is exactly R. So every possible rejected group is reached.

This reversible, one-to-one pairing is called a bijection. The word simply means that each group on either side has exactly one partner on the other.

Act 3: Equal counts, not identical groups

We have now checked both parts of the pairing. Count its rows from either side:

Count the selected listsCount the rejected lists
Every r-person group appears once.
There are \(\binom nr\) such groups.		Every (n−r)-person group appears once.
There are \(\binom n{n-r}\) such groups.

Both counts count the same number of pairs. Therefore

\[\boxed{\binom nr=\binom n{n-r}}.\]
Clue 4 · If n = 5 and r = 2, what does \(\binom52=\binom53\) mean?

Follow this clue: Compare the number of groups, not the number of people inside one group.
Open the reasoning

The selected group has 2 members and its partner has 3. They are not the same group. Yet there are 10 possible selected groups and 10 possible rejected groups. Their sizes differ, but the numbers of groups are equal.

Small step 4. Does one selected group give many rejected groups because we could write the rejected names in different orders?

Open the answer

No. We count groups, not ordered lists. {B, C, E}, {E, B, C} and {C, E, B} are the same rejected group.

The main idea: Choosing who goes also chooses who stays. We record the same decision from two sides.

Act 4: Check the ends and the middle

What immediate observation can we make when nobody is selected?

Clue 5 · If r = 0, how many selected groups and rejected groups are possible?

Follow this clue: Not selecting anyone is one specific choice. It is not the absence of a possible choice.
Open the reasoning
\[\varnothing\longleftrightarrow U,\qquad \binom n0=\binom nn=1.\]

If r = n, the same pair is read the other way around. If n = 0 as well, U is empty: the only group is the empty group, whose complement in U is itself. The equality still holds: \(\binom00=1\).

Small step 5. If n = 6 and r = 3, do a selected group and its complement become the same group?

Open the answer

No. For example, {A, B, C} pairs with {D, E, F}. Both have three members, but they share none. Equal size is not the same as equal membership.

Small step 6. For n = 6 and r = 3 there are 20 selected groups. Why are there not just 10 choices?

Open the answer

There are 10 unordered complement pairs, but 20 choices of who is selected. Selecting {A, B, C} is a different trip decision from selecting {D, E, F}. The roles of selected and rejected matter.

For any nonempty U, a group cannot equal its own complement: an applicant would have to be both in the group and outside it. The empty universe is the one special case.

Act 5: Follow the factorials, one step at a time

The pairing already proves the theorem. Now we will check it by algebra and explain why every step works.

First clue: why does the counting formula have two factorials below?

For a positive integer k, \(k!=k(k-1)\cdots2\cdot1\), and \(0!=1\). Imagine lining up all n applicants. There are \(n!\) orders. Take the first r people as the selected group and the remaining \(n-r\) as the rejected group.

One fixed selected group is counted many times: its r members can be arranged in \(r!\) orders, and the other \(n-r\) members in \((n-r)!\) orders. None of these reorderings changes who is selected.

\[\text{orders for each fixed selected group}=r!(n-r)!.\]

Every selected group is repeated exactly that many times. Dividing removes these repeats:

\[\binom nr=\frac{n!}{r!(n-r)!}.\]

Small step 7. For n = 5 and r = 2, how many full lineups give the same fixed selected pair?

Open the answer

There are \(2!\times3!=2\times6=12\). The two selected people can swap places; the three others can be arranged in six ways. So \(5!/12=120/12=10\) selected groups.

Next clue: write the formula for the other group size

Use a temporary letter k so that the substitution is easy to see:

\[\binom nk=\frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}.\]

We now want groups of size \(n-r\). So put \(k=n-r\) everywhere k appears. Do not change n.

Clue 6 · What do we get when we substitute \(k=n-r\)?

Follow this clue: In the general formula, one factorial is k! and the other is (n−k)!. Replace k in both places.
Open the reasoning

The first denominator becomes \((n-r)!\). The second becomes \([n-(n-r)]!\). The numerator stays \(n!\), since we still have the same n applicants.

Small step 8. What is \(n-(n-r)\)? Why do we keep the parentheses until we simplify?

Open the answer

Subtracting the whole bracket changes both signs: \(n-(n-r)=n-n+r=r\). Writing \(n-n-r\) would be a sign error.

Now simplify the second factorial:

\[\binom n{n-r}=\frac{n!}{(n-r)!\,[n-(n-r)]!}=\frac{n!}{(n-r)!\,r!}.\]

Small step 9. We want to compare this with the formula for n choose r. What immediate observation do you make?

Open the answer

The numerator is the same. The denominator contains the same two factors, but their order is reversed. Multiplication gives the same product in either order.

Thus, step by step,

\[\begin{aligned} \binom n{n-r} &=\frac{n!}{(n-r)!\,[n-(n-r)]!}\\ &=\frac{n!}{(n-r)!\,r!} &&\text{because } n-(n-r)=r\\ &=\frac{n!}{r!\,(n-r)!} &&\text{swap the two factors}\\ &=\binom nr. \end{aligned}\]

We have not swapped r with n, and we have not treated a factorial as a difference. In particular, \((n-r)!\) means “subtract first, then take the factorial”; it does not mean \(n!-r!\).

Small step 10. Can any denominator here be zero, even when r = 0 or r = n?

Open the answer

No. The factorials of nonnegative integers are positive, including \(0!=1\). All the divisions are valid.

Check the calculation with n = 6 and r = 2

\[\begin{aligned} \binom62&=\frac{6!}{2!\,4!} =\frac{720}{2\cdot24} =\frac{720}{48}=15,\\ \binom64&=\frac{6!}{4!\,2!} =\frac{720}{24\cdot2} =\frac{720}{48}=15. \end{aligned}\]

The two calculations have the same numerator and the same denominator. In the trip story, each selected pair is matched with the four applicants left out.

Act 6: What have we proved—and what have we not?

For n = 5, the row of coefficients is

\[1,\quad5,\quad10,\quad10,\quad5,\quad1.\]

The partner of index r is index \(n-r\). These positions are equally far from opposite ends.

Small step 11. If n = 8, which entry must equal the entry at r = 2?

Open the answer

The entry at \(r=8-2=6\): \(\binom82=\binom86=28\). Since indexing starts at r = 0, these are the third and seventh terms of the nine-entry row.

Clue 7 · Does this symmetry alone prove that the middle coefficients are largest?

Follow this clue: Think about the symmetric sequence 5, 1, 5. Where are its largest entries?
Open the reasoning

Symmetry means opposite entries match. It does not say which entry is larger than its neighbor. To locate the peak, we need a further argument, such as the neighboring-coefficient comparison from Case File 36.

Final case report

For nonnegative integer n and integer \(0\le r\le n\),

\[\boxed{\binom nr=\binom n{n-r}}.\]

Why it makes sense: Selecting r applicants leaves exactly n−r applicants out. Either group determines the other.

Why the pairing proves it: It can be reversed. No groups are merged, and no group is missed.

Why the algebra agrees: The denominators \(r!(n-r)!\) and \((n-r)!r!\) are the same product.

Keep the distinction: Equal numbers of groups do not mean the groups themselves are equal.
