MATH DETECTIVE: CASE FILE #31

Subject: Why the Three Medians Meet at One Point

Mission: Find where two medians meet. Then check the third and explain the ratio \(2:1\).

Detective Briefing: Two lines may meet, but a third line need not go through their meeting point. What is special about joining each vertex of a triangle to the midpoint of the opposite side?

Choose an answer before opening its reasoning. Questions and the visual lab work offline; equation formatting and display fonts need internet access.

Act 1: What do we need to prove?

A median joins a vertex to the midpoint of the opposite side. Lines are concurrent when they all pass through one common point.

For a nondegenerate triangle ABC, we want to prove that all three medians meet at one point G, and

\[ \boxed{AG:GM=BG:GN=CG:GK=2:1,} \]

where M, N and K are the midpoints of BC, CA and AB. The longer part is next to the vertex.

Clue 1 · So we have three medians. What is a useful first step?

Hint: An example suggests a result, but the third median still needs its own check.

Open the plan

We will write equations for two medians and solve for their common point. Then we will substitute that point into the third median’s equation. The same calculation will tell us how far along each median the point lies.

“Nondegenerate” means the three vertices are not on one straight line. The triangle has positive area.

Act 2: Which coordinates make the work easier?

Place A at the origin. Write the position vectors of B and C as \(\mathbf b\) and \(\mathbf c\). Moving the origin changes the coordinate labels, not the triangle or its ratios.

\[A:\mathbf0,\qquad B:\mathbf b,\qquad C:\mathbf c.\]
Clue 2 · What does the nondegenerate condition tell us about \(\mathbf b\) and \(\mathbf c\)?

Hint: If both vectors pointed along the same line through A, where would B and C lie?

What does independence mean here?

If \(\mathbf b\) and \(\mathbf c\) were proportional, A, B and C would be collinear. Since they are not,

\[ u\mathbf b+v\mathbf c=\mathbf0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad u=v=0. \]

That is linear independence. It will let us compare coefficients later.

Moving halfway from B to C gives M:

\[ \mathbf m=\mathbf b+\frac12(\mathbf c-\mathbf b) =\frac{\mathbf b+\mathbf c}{2}. \]

Similarly, halfway from C to A gives N:

\[ \mathbf n=\mathbf c+\frac12(\mathbf0-\mathbf c)=\frac{\mathbf c}{2}. \]

Lowercase bold letters denote position vectors of the matching uppercase points.

Act 3: How do we find the first meeting point?

A point on a line from X toward Y has position \(\mathbf x+t(\mathbf y-\mathbf x)\). Use this on the two median lines, initially allowing real parameters t and s:

\[ AM:\quad \mathbf p=\mathbf0+t(\mathbf m-\mathbf0) =\frac t2(\mathbf b+\mathbf c), \] \[ BN:\quad \mathbf q=\mathbf b+s(\mathbf n-\mathbf b) =(1-s)\mathbf b+\frac s2\mathbf c. \]
Clue 3 · At the intersection, what must we set equal?

Hint: A meeting point is one point described in two different ways.

Write the intersection condition

At a common point, \(\mathbf p=\mathbf q\). We do not yet know that \(t=s\); that must come from solving the vector equation.

\[ \frac t2\mathbf b+\frac t2\mathbf c =(1-s)\mathbf b+\frac s2\mathbf c. \]
Clue 4 · We want two scalar equations from this vector equation. Why may we compare the coefficients?

Hint: Move all terms to one side and use the meaning of independence.

Show every step
\[ \left(\frac t2-1+s\right)\mathbf b+ \left(\frac t2-\frac s2\right)\mathbf c=\mathbf0. \]

Independence forces each coefficient to be zero:

\[ \frac t2=1-s,\qquad \frac t2=\frac s2. \]

The second equation gives \(t=s\). Substitute into the first:

\[ \frac t2=1-t \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{3t}{2}=1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{t=s=\frac23.} \]

There is a solution, and it is unique. Substituting it back gives the common point:

\[ \boxed{\mathbf g=\frac{1}{3}(\mathbf b+\mathbf c).} \]

We have found the unique intersection G of the first two median lines.

Act 4: Where is G on each median?

Clue 5 · The parameters are t = s = 2/3. What immediate observation gives both position and ratio?

Hint: The parameter measures a fraction of the full vertex-to-midpoint displacement.

Turn the fraction into a ratio
\[ \overrightarrow{AG}=\frac23\overrightarrow{AM}, \qquad \overrightarrow{GM}=\frac13\overrightarrow{AM}. \]

Thus

\[ AG=\frac23 AM,\qquad GM=\frac13 AM,\qquad AG:GM=\frac23:\frac13=\boxed{2:1}. \]

Exactly the same reasoning on BN gives \(BG:GN=2:1\). We are dividing each median into two parts; we are not comparing the lengths of different medians.

Act 5: Does the third median reach the same point?

Let K be the midpoint of AB. Since A is the origin, \(\mathbf k=\mathbf b/2\).

Clue 6 · We already know G. What can we do to check the third median CK?

Hint: Try the point two-thirds of the way from C to K.

Calculate the third point
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf c+\frac23\left(\frac{\mathbf b}{2}-\mathbf c\right) &=\mathbf c+\frac{\mathbf b}{3}-\frac{2\mathbf c}{3}\\ &=\frac{\mathbf b}{3}+\frac{\mathbf c}{3}\\ &=\frac{\mathbf b+\mathbf c}{3}\\ &=\mathbf g. \end{aligned} \]

It is the very same point. Therefore G lies on CK. Since the parameter is \(2/3\), \(CG:GK=2:1\) as well.

All three medians pass through G, and G divides each in the ratio \(2:1\) measured from the vertex. Their common point is called the centroid.

Because the first two medians already have a unique intersection, the three cannot have a second common point.

Act 7: Why does the shape not change the ratio?

Clue 7 · Which feature of the calculation forced 2/3, regardless of side lengths and angles?

Hint: Look at t/2 = 1 − s and t/2 = s/2. Where did the halves come from?

Read what the equations used

The equations were \(\tfrac t2=1-s\) and \(t=s\). They came from going halfway along the opposite sides. They contain no angle and no side length. Solving them gives \(t=s=2/3\) for every nondegenerate triangle.

A median need not be an altitude, an angle bisector, or equal in length to another median. None of those properties was used.

What happens under a stretch?

A reversible affine change can stretch, shear, turn or move the plane without bending straight lines. “Reversible” means the plane is not flattened onto a line.

Write such a change as \(T(\mathbf x)=L\mathbf x+\mathbf d\), where L is an invertible linear map. For any real u,

\[ T\bigl((1-u)\mathbf x+u\mathbf y\bigr) =(1-u)T(\mathbf x)+uT(\mathbf y). \]

So a point halfway along a side (\(u=1/2\)) remains a midpoint, and a point two-thirds along a median (\(u=2/3\)) remains two-thirds along the transformed median. This explains why the meeting point and its ratio survive the change, even when angles and lengths change.

In the lab, the horizontal stretch is \(T(x,y)=(kx,y)\) with \(k>0\), so it is reversible.

Act 8: What if the vertices are on one line?

Clue 8 · If A, B and C are collinear, which step of our proof is no longer justified?

Hint: Midpoint formulas still work on a straight line. The lost condition is independence.

Try a concrete example

Take \(A=(0,0)\), \(B=(2,0)\), \(C=(4,0)\). Then \(M=(3,0)\), \(N=(2,0)=B\), and \(K=(1,0)\).

AM and CK lie on the same x-axis. BN is just a point because its vertex and midpoint coincide. There are no longer three genuine median lines with one unique common intersection in the usual sense.

The average point \((A+B+C)/3=(2,0)\) still exists, and the two-thirds vector identities still hold. But a collapsed median has length zero, so the usual positive-length ratio and unique-line-intersection statement need the nondegenerate condition.

Final report: one point, three medians

With A at the origin,

\[ \boxed{\mathbf g=\frac{\mathbf b+\mathbf c}{3},\qquad AG:GM=BG:GN=CG:GK=2:1.} \]

With any origin, the same point has position

\[ \boxed{\mathbf g=\frac{\mathbf a+\mathbf b+\mathbf c}{3}.} \]

Indeed, starting at A gives \(\mathbf a+\bigl((\mathbf b-\mathbf a)+(\mathbf c-\mathbf a)\bigr)/3\), which simplifies to this average.
