MATH DETECTIVE: CASE FILE #32

Subject: Why the Centroid’s Coordinates Are Averages

Mission: Turn the median ratio into a coordinate formula, then find why the same point minimizes a sum of squared distances.

Detective Briefing: We already know that the medians meet at a point two-thirds of the way from each vertex to the opposite midpoint. Why should adding the three vertex coordinates and dividing by 3 find that point?

Choose an answer to each main clue. Pause at the short “Small step” questions before opening their answers. These calculation checks are for practice, not part of the score. The questions and labs work offline; equation formatting and display fonts need internet access.

Act 1: Start from what the geometry tells us

Let ABC be a nondegenerate triangle: its three vertices are not on one straight line. Write

\[A=(x_1,y_1),\quad B=(x_2,y_2),\quad C=(x_3,y_3).\]

Let M be the midpoint of BC. The previous theorem gives \(AG:GM=2:1\), so \(AG=\tfrac23 AM\).

Clue 1 · We know the median ratio. What should we use to locate G?

Hint: Start at A, then add two-thirds of the displacement from A to M.

Set up the calculation
\[ M=\left(\frac{x_2+x_3}{2},\frac{y_2+y_3}{2}\right), \qquad G=A+\frac23(M-A). \]

When we add or subtract points in these formulas, we mean their position vectors, all measured from the same origin.

Small step 1 · Why is the fraction \(2/3\), not \(2/1\)?

Check this step

The two parts of AM have lengths \(2k\) and \(k\). The whole median is \(3k\), so \(AG/AM=2k/(3k)=2/3\).

Small step 2 · What is the x-component of the displacement \(M-A\)?

Check this step
\[\frac{x_2+x_3}{2}-x_1.\]

Subtract the starting coordinate from the ending coordinate.

Act 2: Simplify one coordinate at a time

For the x-coordinate, substitute the midpoint coordinate:

\[ x_G=x_1+\frac23\left(\frac{x_2+x_3}{2}-x_1\right). \]

Small step 3 · Distribute \(2/3\). What happens to the factor \(1/2\)?

Check this step
\[ \frac23\cdot\frac{x_2+x_3}{2}=\frac{x_2+x_3}{3}, \qquad \frac23(-x_1)=-\frac23x_1. \]

Therefore

\[ \begin{aligned} x_G &=x_1+\frac{x_2+x_3}{3}-\frac23x_1\\ &=\frac13x_1+\frac13x_2+\frac13x_3\\ &=\frac{x_1+x_2+x_3}{3}. \end{aligned} \]

Small step 4 · Why does \(x_1-\frac23x_1\) become \(\frac13x_1\)?

Check this step

Write \(x_1=\frac33x_1\). Then subtract: \((3/3-2/3)x_1=x_1/3\).

Do exactly the same calculation for y:

\[ \begin{aligned} y_G &=y_1+\frac23\left(\frac{y_2+y_3}{2}-y_1\right)\\ &=\frac13y_1+\frac13y_2+\frac13y_3\\ &=\frac{y_1+y_2+y_3}{3}. \end{aligned} \]
\[ \boxed{G=\left(\frac{x_1+x_2+x_3}{3},\frac{y_1+y_2+y_3}{3}\right) =\frac{A+B+C}{3}.} \]

The formula follows from the median ratio. We did not assume that averaging would give the centroid.

Try a numerical check

For \(A=(0,0)\), \(B=(6,0)\), \(C=(0,6)\), the midpoint is \(M=(3,3)\).

Small step 5 · Find G once using the median and once using the coordinate averages.

Check this step
\[ A+\frac23(M-A)=(0,0)+\frac23(3,3)=(2,2), \] \[ \left(\frac{0+6+0}{3},\frac{0+0+6}{3}\right)=(2,2). \]

Both methods give the same point.

Act 3: Why is halfway along the median wrong?

Clue 2 · Suppose we take the midpoint H of A and M. How much weight does each original vertex get?

Hint: Replace M by (B+C)/2 before simplifying (A+M)/2.

Expand the nested midpoint
\[ H=\frac{A+M}{2} =\frac12A+\frac12\left(\frac{B+C}{2}\right) =\frac12A+\frac14B+\frac14C. \]

This treats A differently from B and C. For our numerical triangle, it gives \(H=(1.5,1.5)\), not \(G=(2,2)\).

More generally, a point a fraction t along AM is

\[ P=A+t(M-A)=(1-t)A+\frac t2B+\frac t2C. \]

Small step 6 · To make all three weights equal, which equation should t satisfy? Solve it.

Check this step
\[ 1-t=\frac t2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 2-2t=t \quad\Longrightarrow\quad t=\frac23. \]

Then \(1-t=t/2=1/3\). This is why the halfway construction must be replaced by the two-thirds construction.

Act 4: Does the average really lie on every median?

Clue 3 · The average treats all three vertices equally. Is that symmetry alone a proof that it is the centroid?

Hint: Other triangle centers also treat the vertices symmetrically. We must check which geometric property this formula satisfies.

Start with the candidate \(G=(A+B+C)/3\), without assuming it is the median intersection.

Small step 7 · Write \(G-A\) with denominator 3. Why is the coefficient of A now −2?

Check this step
\[ G-A=\frac{A+B+C}{3}-\frac{3A}{3} =\frac{B+C-2A}{3}. \]

One copy of A minus three copies leaves minus two copies.

Meanwhile, \(M-A=(B+C-2A)/2\), so

\[ G-A=\frac23(M-A). \]

The candidate is on AM at the required fraction. For the other two midpoints \(N=(C+A)/2\) and \(K=(A+B)/2\),

\[ G-B=\frac{A+C-2B}{3}=\frac23(N-B), \] \[ G-C=\frac{A+B-2C}{3}=\frac23(K-C). \]

So the average is on all three medians, and we have both directions:

\[ \boxed{G\text{ is the median intersection}\iff G=\frac{A+B+C}{3}.} \]
Clue 4 · Why is G strictly inside a nondegenerate triangle?

Hint: The parameter is strictly between 0 and 1, not just any real number.

Locate the point, not just its line

The open segment from A to M lies inside the triangle. Its endpoints A and M are on the boundary, but the point two-thirds along it is neither endpoint.

Equivalently, G is a combination of the three noncollinear vertices with positive weights \(1/3,1/3,1/3\) adding to 1. This puts it in the interior. Its coordinate signs depend on where we place the axes and are not the reason.

Act 5: What happens if we move the axes?

The numerical coordinates may change, but the geometric rule should still find the same corresponding point. A rotation and a translation act on coordinate vectors as

\[T(X)=RX+\mathbf d.\]

R is a linear rotation map, and \(\mathbf d\) is a fixed translation vector. Moving the axes instead of the triangle also gives a coordinate change of this form, with the appropriate R and \(\mathbf d\).

Clue 5 · When we average T(A), T(B) and T(C), what happens to the three translation vectors?

Hint: Write all three transformed points before taking their average.

Follow the calculation
\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{T(A)+T(B)+T(C)}3 &=\frac{(RA+\mathbf d)+(RB+\mathbf d)+(RC+\mathbf d)}3\\ &=\frac{R(A+B+C)+3\mathbf d}{3}\\ &=R\left(\frac{A+B+C}{3}\right)+\mathbf d\\ &=T(G). \end{aligned} \]

R distributes over addition because it is linear. The average of the transformed vertices is exactly the transformed centroid. The formula respects the geometry.

Small step 8 · Why does it matter that the three weights add to 1?

Check this step

For weights \(w_1,w_2,w_3\), the translation contribution is \((w_1+w_2+w_3)\mathbf d\). It equals one copy of \(\mathbf d\) when their sum is 1. Here \(1/3+1/3+1/3=1\).

Act 6: Which point makes the squared-distance sum smallest?

Let X be any point in the plane, inside or outside the triangle. Define

\[ S(X)=XA^2+XB^2+XC^2. \]

We will compare \(S(X)\) with \(S(G)\). Put \(\mathbf u=X-G=\overrightarrow{GX}\), the displacement from G to X.

Small step 9 · Fill in the displacement: \(X-A=(G-A)+\ \underline{\phantom{u}}\).

Check this step

The missing vector is \(\mathbf u=X-G\):

\[ (G-A)+(X-G)=X-A. \]
Clue 6 · We need to expand \(|(G-A)+\mathbf u|^2\). Which rule is correct?

Hint: Square a vector length by taking the vector’s dot product with itself.

What is a dot product, and where does the cross term come from?
\[ \begin{aligned} |\mathbf v+\mathbf u|^2 &=(\mathbf v+\mathbf u)\cdot(\mathbf v+\mathbf u)\\ &=\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf v+\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf u+ \mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v+\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf u\\ &=|\mathbf v|^2+2\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v+|\mathbf u|^2. \end{aligned} \]

For two nonzero vectors, \(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v=|\mathbf u||\mathbf v|\cos\theta\). In coordinates it is \(u_xv_x+u_yv_y\). It may be positive, negative, or zero, so we cannot simply discard the cross term.

Write the expansion once for each vertex:

\[ \begin{aligned} XA^2&=GA^2+2\mathbf u\cdot(G-A)+|\mathbf u|^2,\\ XB^2&=GB^2+2\mathbf u\cdot(G-B)+|\mathbf u|^2,\\ XC^2&=GC^2+2\mathbf u\cdot(G-C)+|\mathbf u|^2. \end{aligned} \]

Small step 10 · On adding these three lines, how many copies of \(|\mathbf u|^2\) do we get?

Check this step

Three copies, one from each vertex. Their sum is \(3|\mathbf u|^2=3XG^2\).

Add and collect the dot products:

\[ \begin{aligned} S(X)=S(G)+3|\mathbf u|^2 +2\mathbf u\cdot\bigl((G-A)+(G-B)+(G-C)\bigr). \end{aligned} \]

Small step 11 · Why can the three dot products be combined into one?

Check this step

The dot product distributes over addition:

\[ \mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v_1+\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v_2+\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v_3 =\mathbf u\cdot(\mathbf v_1+\mathbf v_2+\mathbf v_3). \]

The same vector \(\mathbf u\) and the factor 2 occur in all three terms.

Clue 7 · Which fact makes the combined cross term vanish?

Hint: The vectors cancel as a sum. They do not need to vanish one by one.

Show the cancellation
\[ \begin{aligned} (G-A)+(G-B)+(G-C) &=3G-(A+B+C)\\ &=3\left(\frac{A+B+C}{3}\right)-(A+B+C)\\ &=\mathbf0. \end{aligned} \]

Thus the combined cross term is \(2\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf0=0\). This gives the exact identity

\[ \boxed{XA^2+XB^2+XC^2=GA^2+GB^2+GC^2+3XG^2.} \]

Small step 12 · The extra term is \(3XG^2\). Can it be negative? When is it zero?

Check this step

A squared distance is nonnegative. It is zero exactly when \(XG=0\), which means \(X=G\).

Consequently \(S(X)\ge S(G)\), with equality exactly when \(X=G\). So the centroid is the unique point minimizing the sum of squared distances.

Small step 13 · Why have we proved a minimum over the whole plane, not just inside the triangle?

Check this step

X was arbitrary. No step assumed that X lies inside the triangle. Every X different from G adds a strictly positive amount \(3XG^2\).

Act 7: Move X and measure the extra amount

Return to \(A=(0,0), B=(6,0), C=(0,6)\), so \(G=(2,2)\). At G,

\[ GA^2=8,\qquad GB^2=20,\qquad GC^2=20,\qquad S(G)=48. \]

The identity predicts \(S(X)=48+3XG^2\) everywhere.

Act 8: Does the same point minimize ordinary distances?

Clue 8 · May we replace \(XA^2+XB^2+XC^2\) by \(XA+XB+XC\) and keep the conclusion?

Hint: A smaller sum of squares does not automatically mean a smaller sum of the unsquared quantities.

A concrete check in our triangle

At \(G=(2,2)\), the ordinary distance sum is

\[ GA+GB+GC=\sqrt8+\sqrt{20}+\sqrt{20} =2\sqrt2+4\sqrt5\approx11.7727. \]

Try \(X=(1,1)\) instead:

\[ XA+XB+XC=\sqrt2+\sqrt{26}+\sqrt{26} =\sqrt2+2\sqrt{26}\approx11.6123. \]

This is smaller. Thus G does not minimize ordinary distances for this triangle. We do not need to find the actual ordinary-distance minimizer to disprove that claim.

But the squared sums are \(S(G)=48\) and \(S(X)=54\). The two objectives give different comparisons.

Small step 14 · For \(X=(1,1)\), verify \(S(X)=48+3XG^2\) directly.

Check this step
\[ XG^2=(1-2)^2+(1-2)^2=2, \qquad S(X)=1^2+1^2+(1-6)^2+1^2+1^2+(1-6)^2=54. \] \[ 48+3\cdot2=54. \]

The squared-distance theorem still works perfectly.

Final report: why the average works

\[ \boxed{G=\frac{A+B+C}{3}} \qquad \boxed{S(X)=S(G)+3XG^2.} \]

The first formula comes from the median’s two-thirds ratio. The second comes from expanding squared lengths and using \(3G=A+B+C\).
