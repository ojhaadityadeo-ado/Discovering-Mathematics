MATH DETECTIVE: CASE FILE

Subject: Volume of a Parallelepiped

Mission: Start only from “volume = base area × perpendicular height.” Discover why a cross product gives the base area, why a dot product extracts the correct height, and why the final formula is the absolute value of a scalar triple product.

The case: Three non-coplanar vectors \(\mathbf a,\mathbf b,\mathbf c\) start from one point and form the three adjacent edges of a parallelepiped. Prove, rather than merely memorize, that its volume is \(\left|\mathbf a\cdot(\mathbf b\times\mathbf c)\right|\).

Follow the clues in order. The proof is deliberately broken into small decisions. Try each conceptual question before opening the reasoning. The diagram is schematic: it is a two-dimensional drawing of a three-dimensional object.

Act 1: What should we try first?

Let the three edges from one vertex be

\[\mathbf a,\qquad \mathbf b,\qquad \mathbf c.\]

We need a three-dimensional volume, but the most familiar prism formula is much simpler:

\[\boxed{\text{Volume}=\text{area of base}\times\text{perpendicular height}.}\]
Clue 1 · What is the most useful immediate next step?

Follow this clue: A parallelepiped is an oblique prism. Which prism formula survives even when the side edges are slanted?
Open the reasoning

Choose the face generated by \(\mathbf b\) and \(\mathbf c\) as the base. Then \(\mathbf a\) connects that base plane to the opposite parallel face. The volume problem becomes two smaller questions: what is the area of the \(\mathbf b,\mathbf c\) parallelogram, and what part of \(\mathbf a\) is perpendicular to that base?

Small step 1. What does “choose the \(\mathbf b,\mathbf c\) face as the base” actually mean?

Open the answer

It means we regard the parallelogram spanned by \(\mathbf b\) and \(\mathbf c\) as one face of an oblique prism. The opposite face is a translated copy of it, shifted by \(\mathbf a\). We are not claiming \(\mathbf a\) is perpendicular to the base.

Small step 2. Why is multiplying \(|\mathbf a||\mathbf b||\mathbf c|\) usually wrong?

Open the answer

That product is correct for a rectangular box, where all three edges supply mutually perpendicular dimensions. In a slanted parallelepiped, part of an edge can run sideways along the base plane. Only its perpendicular component contributes to height.

Act 2: Solve the base-area part

The chosen base is the parallelogram generated by \(\mathbf b\) and \(\mathbf c\). Let \(\theta\) be the angle between them.

If \(|\mathbf b|\) is used as the base length of that parallelogram, its ordinary planar altitude is

\[|\mathbf c|\sin\theta.\]

Therefore its area is

\[A_{\text{base}}=|\mathbf b|\,|\mathbf c|\sin\theta.\]
Clue 2 · Which vector operation already contains exactly this area?

Follow this clue: Recall which vector product has magnitude \(|\mathbf b||\mathbf c|\sin\theta\).
Open the reasoning

By definition of the cross-product magnitude, \(|\mathbf b\times\mathbf c|=|\mathbf b||\mathbf c|\sin\theta\). That is exactly the area of the parallelogram spanned by \(\mathbf b\) and \(\mathbf c\).

\[\boxed{A_{\text{base}}=|\mathbf b\times\mathbf c|}.\]

Small step 3. Besides its magnitude, what useful information does \(\mathbf b\times\mathbf c\) contain?

Open the answer

Its direction is perpendicular to both \(\mathbf b\) and \(\mathbf c\), hence perpendicular to the base plane. So the same cross product that gives the base area also gives the direction in which the true height must be measured.

Small step 4. What should happen if \(\mathbf b\) and \(\mathbf c\) are parallel?

Open the answer

Then \(\theta=0\) or \(\pi\), so \(\sin\theta=0\). Hence \(\mathbf b\times\mathbf c=\mathbf0\), the base area is zero, and no genuine three-dimensional parallelepiped is formed.

Act 3: What is the true height?

The side edge \(\mathbf a\) reaches the opposite face. Why is its full length not necessarily the height?

Height means perpendicular distance between the two parallel base planes. So we need only the component of \(\mathbf a\) perpendicular to the \(\mathbf b,\mathbf c\) plane.

Clue 3 · What direction should we project \(\mathbf a\) onto?

Follow this clue: The height is measured normal to the base, not along the base.

A unit normal to the base is obtained by normalizing the cross product:

\[\boxed{\mathbf n=\frac{\mathbf b\times\mathbf c}{|\mathbf b\times\mathbf c|}}.\]

Now take the scalar projection of \(\mathbf a\) onto \(\mathbf n\).

Clue 4 · If \(\phi\) is the angle between \(\mathbf a\) and the unit normal \(\mathbf n\), which expression is the height?

Follow this clue: The dot product with a unit vector gives a signed scalar projection.
Open the reasoning

Because \(|\mathbf n|=1\), we have \(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf n=|\mathbf a|\cos\phi\). This is the signed component of \(\mathbf a\) in the perpendicular direction. Geometric height is nonnegative, so we take the absolute value.

\[ \begin{aligned} h &=|\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf n|\\[3pt] &=\left|\mathbf a\cdot\frac{\mathbf b\times\mathbf c}{|\mathbf b\times\mathbf c|}\right|\\[3pt] &=\frac{|\mathbf a\cdot(\mathbf b\times\mathbf c)|}{|\mathbf b\times\mathbf c|}. \end{aligned} \]

Small step 5. What does the dot product do here geometrically?

Open the answer

It discards the part of \(\mathbf a\) that runs parallel to the base and keeps only the signed component in the normal direction. That surviving component is precisely the perpendicular separation of the two faces.

Small step 6. Why is an absolute value necessary even though a physical height cannot be negative?

Open the answer

The normal direction has two choices: \(\mathbf n\) and \(-\mathbf n\). The scalar projection changes sign when we reverse the normal. The geometric distance must be independent of that arbitrary orientation choice, so we take its magnitude.

Act 4: Put base area and height together

We now possess both ingredients:

\[ A_{\text{base}}=|\mathbf b\times\mathbf c|, \qquad h=\frac{|\mathbf a\cdot(\mathbf b\times\mathbf c)|}{|\mathbf b\times\mathbf c|}. \]

Small step 7. What should be the immediate next line?

Open the answer

Substitute these two expressions into \(V=A_{\text{base}}h\). At this stage there is no new theorem to invent; the proof is now just simplification.

Step 1 · Substitute into the prism formula.
\[ V =|\mathbf b\times\mathbf c|\, \frac{|\mathbf a\cdot(\mathbf b\times\mathbf c)|}{|\mathbf b\times\mathbf c|}. \]
Meaning: the first factor is literally the base area; the fraction is literally the perpendicular height.
Step 2 · Cancel the common nonzero base-area factor.
\[ V=|\mathbf a\cdot(\mathbf b\times\mathbf c)|. \]
Why is cancellation legitimate? For a genuine three-dimensional parallelepiped, \(\mathbf b\) and \(\mathbf c\) are not parallel, so \(|\mathbf b\times\mathbf c|>0\). Degenerate cases are handled separately and also give volume zero.
Clue 5 · What is the final volume formula?

Follow this clue: The base-area factor cancels from the product of base area and height.

The geometric proof in one sentence: \(\mathbf b\times\mathbf c\) packages the base area together with the base normal, and the dot product with \(\mathbf a\) extracts the perpendicular part of the third edge. Therefore the scalar triple product is exactly base area times signed height.

\[\boxed{V=|\mathbf a\cdot(\mathbf b\times\mathbf c)|}.\]

Small step 8. Why is this called a scalar triple product?

Open the answer

Three vectors are involved: first \(\mathbf b\) and \(\mathbf c\) are combined by a cross product to make a vector, then that vector is dotted with \(\mathbf a\), producing a scalar. Hence “scalar triple product.”

Act 5: Where does the determinant formula come from?

Write the vectors in coordinates:

\[ \mathbf a=(a_1,a_2,a_3),\quad \mathbf b=(b_1,b_2,b_3),\quad \mathbf c=(c_1,c_2,c_3). \]

First calculate the cross product:

\[ \mathbf b\times\mathbf c = \bigl( b_2c_3-b_3c_2, b_3c_1-b_1c_3, b_1c_2-b_2c_1 \bigr). \]

Now dot with \(\mathbf a\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf a\cdot(\mathbf b\times\mathbf c) &=a_1(b_2c_3-b_3c_2)\\ &\quad+a_2(b_3c_1-b_1c_3)\\ &\quad+a_3(b_1c_2-b_2c_1). \end{aligned} \]

Small step 9. Before reading on, what familiar algebraic object has exactly this three-term expansion?

Open the answer

It is the cofactor expansion along the first row of a \(3\times3\) determinant.

Clue 6 · Which determinant equals the scalar triple product?

Follow this clue: Compare the displayed dot-product expansion with a first-row cofactor expansion.
\[ \mathbf a\cdot(\mathbf b\times\mathbf c) = \begin{vmatrix} a_1&a_2&a_3\\ b_1&b_2&b_3\\ c_1&c_2&c_3 \end{vmatrix}. \]

Therefore

\[ \boxed{ V= \left| \begin{vmatrix} a_1&a_2&a_3\\ b_1&b_2&b_3\\ c_1&c_2&c_3 \end{vmatrix} \right|. } \]

Small step 10. What does the determinant sign tell us that ordinary volume does not?

Open the answer

The determinant is a signed volume. Its sign records the orientation of the ordered triple \((\mathbf a,\mathbf b,\mathbf c)\). The absolute value removes orientation and leaves geometric volume.

Act 6: Does the answer depend on which face we call the base?

Geometrically, the same solid must have the same volume whether we choose the \(\mathbf b,\mathbf c\) face, the \(\mathbf c,\mathbf a\) face, or the \(\mathbf a,\mathbf b\) face as the base.

The scalar triple product agrees:

\[ \mathbf a\cdot(\mathbf b\times\mathbf c) = \mathbf b\cdot(\mathbf c\times\mathbf a) = \mathbf c\cdot(\mathbf a\times\mathbf b). \]

These are cyclic rearrangements. They preserve the sign and therefore certainly preserve the absolute value.

Clue 7 · What happens if we swap just two vectors?

Follow this clue: Remember that \(\mathbf c\times\mathbf b=-(\mathbf b\times\mathbf c)\).
Open the reasoning

For example, \(\mathbf a\cdot(\mathbf c\times\mathbf b)=-\mathbf a\cdot(\mathbf b\times\mathbf c)\). The signed orientation reverses, but the physical solid is unchanged. Therefore ordinary volume uses an absolute value.

Small step 11. What does the cyclic identity mean geometrically?

Open the answer

It means “base area × corresponding height” gives the same result no matter which pair of edge vectors is chosen to generate the base. The determinant packages that geometric consistency automatically.

Act 7: Stress-test the formula

Test A · A rectangular box

Suppose \(\mathbf a,\mathbf b,\mathbf c\) are mutually perpendicular. Then \(\mathbf b\times\mathbf c\) is parallel to \(\mathbf a\), and

\[ |\mathbf b\times\mathbf c|=|\mathbf b||\mathbf c|. \]

Therefore

\[ V=|\mathbf a||\mathbf b||\mathbf c|, \]

which is the familiar length × breadth × height formula.

Small step 12. Why is this an important check rather than a new proof?

Open the answer

A general formula should reduce to a known special case. Recovering the ordinary rectangular-box formula shows that the scalar triple product behaves correctly when all slant disappears.

Test B · All three vectors lie in one plane

If \(\mathbf a\) lies in the plane generated by \(\mathbf b\) and \(\mathbf c\), then \(\mathbf b\times\mathbf c\) is perpendicular to \(\mathbf a\). Hence

\[\mathbf a\cdot(\mathbf b\times\mathbf c)=0.\]

The parallelepiped has collapsed flat, so its volume should indeed be zero.

Clue 8 · What geometric condition does a zero scalar triple product detect?

Follow this clue: A determinant is zero precisely when its row vectors are linearly dependent.

Test C · Two base vectors are parallel

If \(\mathbf b\parallel\mathbf c\), then

\[\mathbf b\times\mathbf c=\mathbf0,\]

so the base itself has area zero and the volume is zero. Again the formula agrees.

Small step 13. What units should \(|\mathbf a\cdot(\mathbf b\times\mathbf c)|\) have?

Open the answer

The cross product magnitude has units of length squared because it is an area. Dotting with \(\mathbf a\), which has units of length, produces length cubed—the correct units for volume.

Act 8: Work one example carefully

Let

\[ \mathbf a=(1,2,0),\qquad \mathbf b=(2,0,1),\qquad \mathbf c=(1,1,3). \]

What is the most direct computational route now that the theorem is proved?

Compute one cross product, then one dot product.

Step 1 · Find \(\mathbf b\times\mathbf c\).
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf b\times\mathbf c &= \begin{vmatrix} \mathbf i&\mathbf j&\mathbf k\\ 2&0&1\\ 1&1&3 \end{vmatrix}\\[4pt] &=(0\cdot3-1\cdot1,\;1\cdot1-2\cdot3,\;2\cdot1-0\cdot1)\\[4pt] &=(-1,-5,2). \end{aligned} \]

Small step 14. What should you do next—take the magnitude of this cross product, or dot it with \(\mathbf a\)?

Open the answer

For the scalar-triple-product formula, dot it with \(\mathbf a\). Taking the cross-product magnitude first would give only the base area; we would still need the height separately.

Step 2 · Take the dot product with \(\mathbf a\).
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf a\cdot(\mathbf b\times\mathbf c) &=(1,2,0)\cdot(-1,-5,2)\\ &=-1-10+0\\ &=-11. \end{aligned} \]
Step 3 · Convert signed volume to geometric volume.
\[\boxed{V=|-11|=11}.\]
Interpretation: the negative sign records orientation only. The physical volume is \(11\) cubic units.

Act 9: Reconstruct the proof without formulas

Before looking at the final report, try to tell the story in words:

Small step 15. Which part of the proof would fail if we replaced the dot product by another cross product?

Open the answer

We would no longer extract a scalar perpendicular component of \(\mathbf a\). A second cross product would produce another vector, whereas volume must be a scalar and the prism formula needs a scalar height.

Final case report

Start: choose the parallelogram generated by \(\mathbf b\) and \(\mathbf c\) as the base.

\[A_{\text{base}}=|\mathbf b\times\mathbf c|.\]

Normal direction:

\[\mathbf n=\frac{\mathbf b\times\mathbf c}{|\mathbf b\times\mathbf c|}.\]

Perpendicular height:

\[ h=|\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf n| =\frac{|\mathbf a\cdot(\mathbf b\times\mathbf c)|}{|\mathbf b\times\mathbf c|}. \]

Multiply base area by height:

\[ \boxed{V=|\mathbf a\cdot(\mathbf b\times\mathbf c)|}. \]

Coordinate form:

\[ \boxed{ V= \left| \begin{vmatrix} a_1&a_2&a_3\\ b_1&b_2&b_3\\ c_1&c_2&c_3 \end{vmatrix} \right|. } \]

Conceptual meaning: the cross product supplies “base area + perpendicular direction”; the dot product supplies “perpendicular component of the third edge.” Their scalar triple product is therefore exactly base area × height.
