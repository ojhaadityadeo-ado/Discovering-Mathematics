MATH DETECTIVE: CASE FILE #41

Subject: Committees with a President

Mission: Count committee–president outcomes in two different ways. First group by committee size; then choose the president before choosing the rest of the committee. Finally verify the identity by differentiating the binomial theorem.

The case: From n people, form a nonempty committee of any size and appoint exactly one member as president. How many distinct committee–president outcomes are possible?

Follow 7 clues and the small-step questions. Try each clue before opening its reasoning. The counting lab works offline; equation formatting and display fonts use an internet connection.

Act 1: What counts as one outcome?

We are not counting committees alone. An outcome records two pieces of information:

Outcome = a nonempty committee + one named president who belongs to that committee.

If the committee stays the same but its president changes, do we have a new outcome?

Clue 1 · Same members, different president?

Follow this clue: Compare “{Alice, Bob}, president Alice” with “{Alice, Bob}, president Bob.”
Open the reasoning

They are different outcomes. The same committee can produce several outcomes because each member could be appointed president. We therefore should not divide away the president choice later.

Small step 1. Can the empty committee contribute an outcome?

Open the answer

No. It has no member who can serve as president. This is why the factor r will make the r=0 contribution equal to 0 automatically.

Act 2: First count by committee size

Fix a committee size r. We make two decisions in order.

DecisionNumber of choices
Choose the r committee members from n people.\(\binom nr\)
Choose one of those r members as president.\(r\)

By the multiplication principle, the number of size-r committee–president outcomes is

\[r\binom nr.\]
Clue 2 · Why do the two factors multiply?

Follow this clue: After one particular committee has been chosen, how many members of that committee could be president?

Now allow every possible committee size. Since outcomes of different committee sizes cannot be the same outcome, we add:

\[\sum_{r=0}^{n}r\binom nr.\]

Small step 2. Why is it harmless to start the sum at r=0 even though committees must be nonempty?

Open the answer

The r=0 term is \(0\binom n0=0\). The empty committee contributes no president-bearing outcome, exactly as it should.

Act 3: Count the same outcomes president first

Can we avoid choosing a committee size in advance?

Yes. Reverse the order of decisions.

DecisionNumber of choices
Choose the president from the n people.\(n\)
For each of the remaining n−1 people, decide independently whether that person joins the committee.\(2^{n-1}\)

The president is already guaranteed to be in the committee, so the committee cannot be empty. Therefore

\[n\cdot 2^{n-1}\]

outcomes are possible.

Clue 3 · Why are there \(2^{n-1}\) ways to complete the committee after choosing the president?

Follow this clue: Once the president is fixed, focus separately on each person who is not president.

Small step 3. Does “independently” here mean we are making a probability assumption?

Open the answer

No. It means every include/exclude pattern for the remaining people is permitted. This is the multiplication principle, not a claim about random behavior.

Act 4: Watch both counts agree

The two methods count exactly the same collection of committee–president outcomes. Therefore their totals must agree:

\[\boxed{\sum_{r=0}^{n}r\binom nr=n2^{n-1}},\qquad n\ge1.\]
Clue 4 · Why do we not divide by the number of possible presidents?

Follow this clue: The president is an office, not an ordering artifact.

Small step 4. For n=4, what total should both methods give?

Open the answer

They give \(4\cdot2^3=32\). By committee size: \(1\binom41+2\binom42+3\binom43+4\binom44=4+12+12+4=32\).

Act 5: Stress-test the edges

Check n = 1

There is one person, Alice. The only valid outcome is the one-person committee with Alice as president.

\[\sum_{r=0}^{1}r\binom1r=0\binom10+1\binom11=1.\]

The other count gives

\[1\cdot2^{0}=1.\]
Clue 5 · What role does the r=0 term play?

Follow this clue: Look at the factor r when r=0.
Why the statement is written for n ≥ 1: the committee story begins by choosing a president from n people, so we need at least one person. The n=0 derivative calculation can be handled separately, but it is outside the stated combinatorial setup.

Act 6: Follow the derivative proof in detail

The double-counting argument already proves the identity. Now verify it algebraically. Assume \(n\ge1\).

Step 1 · Start with the binomial theorem.
\[(1+t)^n=\sum_{r=0}^{n}\binom nr t^r.\]
Why this form? We want the factor r to appear. Differentiating \(t^r\) produces exactly that factor.

Small step 5. What is the derivative of \(t^r\) when r≥1?

Open the answer

By the power rule, \(\dfrac{d}{dt}t^r=rt^{r-1}\). The coefficient r appearing in our target sum is created by this derivative.

Step 2 · Differentiate both sides with respect to t.

On the left, the power rule gives

\[\frac{d}{dt}(1+t)^n=n(1+t)^{n-1}.\]

On the right, the finite sum may be differentiated term by term:

\[ \frac{d}{dt}\left(\sum_{r=0}^{n}\binom nr t^r\right) =\sum_{r=1}^{n}r\binom nr t^{r-1}. \]

Therefore

\[n(1+t)^{n-1}=\sum_{r=1}^{n}r\binom nr t^{r-1}.\]
Why does the sum now start at r=1? The r=0 term is \(\binom n0t^0=1\), whose derivative is 0, so it disappears.

Small step 6. Why is writing the differentiated sum from r=1 cleaner than keeping r=0?

Open the answer

If we mechanically wrote \(rt^{r-1}\) at r=0, it would look like \(0\cdot t^{-1}\), which is not a useful expression at t=0. The actual derivative of the constant r=0 term is simply 0, so starting at r=1 is precise and clean.

Step 3 · Set t = 1.
\[n(1+1)^{n-1}=\sum_{r=1}^{n}r\binom nr 1^{r-1}.\]
Why choose t=1? It makes every remaining power \(1^{r-1}\) equal to 1, leaving only the factors \(r\binom nr\) that we want to sum.

Small step 7. What happens to \(1^{r-1}\) for every r in this sum?

Open the answer

Because r starts at 1, the exponent r−1 is a nonnegative integer. Therefore \(1^{r-1}=1\) in every term.

Step 4 · Simplify both sides.

The left-hand side becomes

\[n(2)^{n-1}=n2^{n-1}.\]

The right-hand side becomes

\[\sum_{r=1}^{n}r\binom nr.\]

Hence

\[n2^{n-1}=\sum_{r=1}^{n}r\binom nr.\]

Small step 8. Why can we now change the lower limit from r=1 back to r=0?

Open the answer

Adding the r=0 term adds \(0\binom n0=0\), so the value of the sum does not change.

Step 5 · Reinsert the zero term and write the target identity.
\[ \sum_{r=1}^{n}r\binom nr =0\binom n0+\sum_{r=1}^{n}r\binom nr =\sum_{r=0}^{n}r\binom nr. \]

Therefore

\[\boxed{\sum_{r=0}^{n}r\binom nr=n2^{n-1}},\qquad n\ge1.\]

Small step 9. In words, what job did differentiation perform?

Open the answer

It attached a factor r to the coefficient \(\binom nr\). Combinatorially, that same factor r counts the possible presidents inside an r-person committee.

Full algebra in one continuous chain

\[ \begin{aligned} (1+t)^n &=\sum_{r=0}^{n}\binom nr t^r\\[4pt] \frac{d}{dt}(1+t)^n &=\frac{d}{dt}\left(\sum_{r=0}^{n}\binom nr t^r\right)\\[4pt] n(1+t)^{n-1} &=\sum_{r=1}^{n}r\binom nr t^{r-1}\\[4pt] n(1+1)^{n-1} &=\sum_{r=1}^{n}r\binom nr 1^{r-1}\\[4pt] n2^{n-1} &=\sum_{r=1}^{n}r\binom nr\\[4pt] &=\sum_{r=0}^{n}r\binom nr. \end{aligned} \]

Act 7: Extend the idea — add a secretary

Now appoint both a president and a different secretary. The two offices are ordered: “Alice president, Bob secretary” is not the same as “Bob president, Alice secretary.”

Office-holders first: choose the president in n ways and the secretary in n−1 ways. Then each of the remaining n−2 people may be included or excluded.

\[n(n-1)2^{n-2}.\]

Committee size first: for an r-person committee, there are \(r\) choices for president and then \(r-1\) choices for secretary.

\[r(r-1)\binom nr.\]

Summing over committee sizes gives

\[\boxed{\sum_{r=0}^{n}r(r-1)\binom nr=n(n-1)2^{n-2}},\qquad n\ge2.\]
Clue 6 · Why is the factor for two offices r(r−1), not \(\binom r2\)?

Follow this clue: Ask whether swapping the two office holders changes the outcome.
Clue 7 · What broader pattern is suggested by these identities?

Follow this clue: Compare the one-office factor r with the two-office factor r(r−1).

Final case report

For every integer \(n\ge1\),

\[\boxed{\sum_{r=0}^{n}r\binom nr=n2^{n-1}}.\]

Count by committee size: an r-person committee can be chosen in \(\binom nr\) ways, and then its president in r ways.

Count president first: choose one of n presidents, then freely include or exclude each of the remaining n−1 people.

Why no division: changing the president changes the outcome.

Why r=0 causes no trouble: the factor r makes the empty-committee contribution zero.

Why the algebra works: differentiation turns \(t^r\) into \(rt^{r-1}\), creating the same factor r that counts possible presidents.

Extension: two distinct ordered offices lead to the factor \(r(r-1)\) and the identity \(\sum r(r-1)\binom nr=n(n-1)2^{n-2}\).
