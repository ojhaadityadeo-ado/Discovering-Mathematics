MATH DETECTIVE: CASE FILE #17

Subject: Rolle’s Theorem

Mission: Explain why a graph that starts and ends at the same height must have a horizontal tangent somewhere between the ends, when Rolle’s conditions hold.

Detective Briefing: Do not just remember the statement. Find the job of each condition: why a highest or lowest value exists, why it is reached inside the interval, and why the slope there is zero.

Act 1: The Same Starting and Ending Height

Let \(f\) be a real-valued function on \([a,b]\), where \(a<b\). We are given three conditions:

1. Continuous on \([a,b]\)No breaks on the whole interval, including the endpoints.
2. Differentiable on \((a,b)\)A finite derivative exists at every point strictly between the endpoints.
3. \(f(a)=f(b)\)The graph starts and ends at the same height.
The statement to explain
There is at least one point \(c\) strictly between \(a\) and \(b\) such that
\[\boxed{f'(c)=0,\qquad a<c<b.}\]
A zero derivative means that the tangent is horizontal.

If the graph starts and ends at height \(H\), what can happen in between?

Detective’s Note: If every value equals H, the graph is flat. Otherwise, some value differs from H. Is that value above H or below H?
Try the three simple examplesf(x) = 4 − x² on [−2, 2]

The flat case is already solved. If \(f(x)=H\) throughout \([a,b]\), then \(f'(x)=0\) at every interior point.

Otherwise, some value is above H or below H. A graph may do both. Either one is enough for the next step of the proof.

These are examples, not all possible graph shapes. A graph can have many hills and valleys. Rolle’s theorem promises at least one horizontal tangent, not exactly one.

Act 2: Find a Point Inside the Interval

Suppose there is a point \(u\in(a,b)\) with \(f(u)>H\), where \(H=f(a)=f(b)\).

Could the highest value on \([a,b]\) be reached at an endpoint?

Detective’s Note: Both endpoint values are H, but f(u) is greater than H. Also, continuity on the closed interval guarantees that a highest value is reached.
Clue 1: The Extreme Value Theorem (EVT)

A continuous function on a closed, bounded interval \([a,b]\) reaches both a highest value and a lowest value. It does not merely get close to them.

Let a highest value occur at \(c\). Then

\[f(c)\ge f(u)>H=f(a)=f(b).\]

So \(c\) is neither \(a\) nor \(b\). It lies strictly inside the interval.

If a value lies below H instead, use the lowest value. It also cannot occur at either endpoint.

Why is the slope at that interior point zero?

At a highest point \(c\), nearby values cannot be larger than \(f(c)\). So \(f(c+h)-f(c)\le0\) for small steps in either direction.

Step from cChange in heightChange ÷ step
\(h>0\): step right\(f(c+h)-f(c)\le0\)\(\dfrac{f(c+h)-f(c)}h\le0\)
\(h<0\): step left\(f(c+h)-f(c)\le0\)\(\dfrac{f(c+h)-f(c)}h\ge0\)
Detective’s Note: The derivative must be at most zero from the right and at least zero from the left. The two limits agree because the derivative exists.
Clue 2: Fermat’s Theorem

At an interior local maximum or minimum, if the derivative exists, it is zero.

\[f'(c)\le0\quad\text{and}\quad f'(c)\ge0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{f'(c)=0}.\]

For a lowest point, the two signs reverse, but the conclusion is the same.

Why inside matters: We can take small steps to both the left and the right while staying in the interval.

Act 3: What If a Condition Is Missing?

Is continuity alone enough, even when the endpoint heights agree?

Detective’s Note: Look at |x| on [−1, 1]. Its lowest point is inside, but do the slopes from the two sides agree there?
Example 1: A corner\(f(x)=|x|\) on \([-1,1]\)
\[ f'(x)=\begin{cases}-1,&x<0,\\1,&x>0.\end{cases} \]

At 0, the left and right slopes disagree. Elsewhere inside the interval, the slope is either −1 or 1. There is no interior point where the derivative is zero.

Now keep differentiability inside, but allow an endpoint gap.

Can we ignore continuity at the endpoints?

Detective’s Note: In the next example, both endpoint values are 1, but every interior slope is 1. The value at 0 does not match the values approaching 0 from the right.
Example 2: An endpoint gap
On \([0,1]\), define
\[ f(x)=\begin{cases}1,&x=0,\\x,&0<x\le1.\end{cases} \]

Then \(f(0)=f(1)=1\), but \(f'(x)=1\) throughout \((0,1)\).

Same endpoint values, but the left endpoint is not joined to the line.

See exactly where the proof stops. Interior values are below the endpoint height 1. But there is no lowest value on the whole interval: values get close to 0 without reaching 0.

We cannot use EVT to guarantee a lowest value here, because the function is not continuous on \([0,1]\). Continuity only inside the interval is not enough.

Act 4: Why Only Differentiate Inside?

Do derivatives at the endpoints need to exist?

Detective’s Note: Equal endpoint heights put the needed highest or lowest point strictly inside. Endpoint derivatives play no part in Fermat’s step.
An example that meets all three conditions
\[f(x)=\sqrt{1-x^2}\quad\text{on }[-1,1].\]

The graph is continuous on the whole interval. Its endpoint values are both 0. For \(-1<x<1\),

\[ f'(x)=\frac{-x}{\sqrt{1-x^2}},\qquad f'(0)=0. \]
The upper half of a circle: steep at the ends, horizontal at the top.
See why the slope at the right endpoint is not finite

Step left from 1 by a positive amount \(t\). Since \(f(1)=0\), the quotient is

\[ \frac{f(1-t)-f(1)}{-t} =-\frac{\sqrt{2t-t^2}}{t} =-\sqrt{\frac{2-t}{t}} \longrightarrow-\infty\quad(t\to0^+). \]

So there is no finite left derivative at 1. At −1, the right quotient tends to \(+\infty\). Neither endpoint derivative is required by Rolle’s theorem.

Keep the two requirements separate: continuity includes the endpoints; differentiability is required only strictly between them.

Act 5: A Guarantee Is Not a Requirement

Must the endpoint heights agree for a horizontal tangent to occur somewhere inside?

Detective’s Note: Try x² on [−1, 2]. Its endpoint values are 1 and 4, but what is its derivative at 0?
Take \(f(x)=x^2\) on \([-1,2]\). Then
\[ f(-1)=1,\qquad f(2)=4,\qquad f'(x)=2x,\qquad f'(0)=0. \]

The endpoint heights differ, but 0 is inside the interval and has a horizontal tangent.

Unequal endpoint heights, but an interior horizontal tangent.

What the theorem says: If all three conditions hold, an interior zero derivative is guaranteed.

What it does not say: If a condition is missing, an interior zero derivative is impossible.

Without all the conditions, the result may happen or may fail. The guarantee is gone.

Final Report: Close the Case

ConditionIts job in the proof
Continuous on \([a,b]\)EVT guarantees that a highest and a lowest value are reached.
\(f(a)=f(b)\)Unless the graph is flat, a highest or lowest value lies strictly inside.
Differentiable on \((a,b)\)Fermat’s theorem makes the derivative at that interior point zero.
Detective’s Note: The point is strictly between the ends. A flat graph gives many possible points, so the theorem cannot promise exactly one.
The complete proof in short

If \(f\) is constant, every interior derivative is zero.

If it is not constant, some value is above or below the common endpoint height. By EVT, a highest or lowest value is reached. It cannot be at an endpoint, so it occurs at an interior point \(c\).

Since \(f\) is differentiable there, Fermat’s theorem gives \(f'(c)=0\).

\[\boxed{\text{There exists }c\in(a,b)\text{ such that }f'(c)=0.}\]

A horizontal tangent means a zero slope at that point. It does not mean that the whole graph is flat.
