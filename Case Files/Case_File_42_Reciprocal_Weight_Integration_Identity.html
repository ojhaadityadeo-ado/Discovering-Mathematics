MATH DETECTIVE: CASE FILE #42

Subject: The Reciprocal-Weight / Integration Identity

Mission: Interpret a non-integer binomial sum as shared voting credit. Compute Alice's credit directly, compute the total credit globally, use symmetry to compare them, and then verify the identity by both factorial algebra and integration.

The case: There are n+1 students, one of whom is Alice. Every nonempty committee receives one unit of voting credit and divides it equally among its members. Across all nonempty committees, how much total credit does Alice receive?

Follow 7 clues and the small-step questions. Try each clue before opening its reasoning. The weighted-credit lab works offline; equation formatting and display fonts use an internet connection.

Act 1: This sum is a weight, not an ordinary count

Our target is

\[\boxed{\sum_{r=0}^{n}\frac{\binom nr}{r+1}=\frac{2^{n+1}-1}{n+1}}.\]

The left side is often not an integer. For example, when n=1 it equals

\[\binom10+\frac{\binom11}{2}=1+\frac12=\frac32.\]

So what kind of interpretation should we look for?

Clue 1 · What is the safest interpretation of the left side?

Follow this clue: A single committee is allowed to contribute a fractional amount to one student.
Open the reasoning

A fractional total cannot be the number of ordinary discrete objects. But it can naturally be a sum of fractional credits. Here each committee contributes one unit altogether, divided among its members.

Small step 1. If a committee has 5 members, how much of its one unit does each member receive?

Open the answer

Each member receives \(1/5\). The five equal shares add back to the committee's full one unit.

Act 2: Compute Alice's credit directly

Only committees containing Alice contribute to Alice's personal total. Suppose such a committee contains Alice and exactly r other students.

Clue 2 · If Alice has exactly r companions, why is the contribution \(\binom nr/(r+1)\)?

Follow this clue: Separate “how many committees?” from “how much credit from each committee?”

Summing over every possible number of companions gives Alice's total credit:

\[\boxed{\text{Alice's credit}=\sum_{r=0}^{n}\frac{\binom nr}{r+1}}.\]

Small step 2. What does r=0 represent?

Open the answer

The committee contains Alice and nobody else. There is \(\binom n0=1\) such committee, its size is 1, and Alice receives its full one unit.

Act 3: Count all the credit before assigning it to people

Every nonempty committee distributes exactly one unit in total, no matter how many people share it.

Clue 3 · How much credit do all nonempty committees distribute altogether?

Follow this clue: Start with all subsets of n+1 students, then remove the empty one.

There are \(2^{n+1}\) subsets of the student set. One is empty, so the number of nonempty committees is

\[2^{n+1}-1.\]

Since every nonempty committee contributes one full unit across its members,

\[\text{total credit distributed}=2^{n+1}-1.\]

Small step 3. Why does a 2-person committee and a 7-person committee each contribute exactly one unit to the global total?

Open the answer

The 2-person committee gives two shares of \(1/2\); the 7-person committee gives seven shares of \(1/7\). In either case the shares sum to 1.

Act 4: Use symmetry to isolate Alice's share

No student has a special role in the collection of all nonempty committees. Swapping Alice's name with Bob's gives a size-preserving bijection between committees containing Alice and committees containing Bob. Corresponding committees therefore give equal-sized credit shares.

Clue 4 · What does this symmetry prove?

Follow this clue: Relabeling students changes names, not committee sizes or share sizes.

Therefore the total credit \(2^{n+1}-1\) is split equally among n+1 students:

\[\text{Alice's credit}=\frac{2^{n+1}-1}{n+1}.\]

Comparing this with the expression from Act 2 gives the identity.

\[\boxed{\sum_{r=0}^{n}\frac{\binom nr}{r+1}=\frac{2^{n+1}-1}{n+1}}.\]

Small step 4. Why is “everyone gets the same total” stronger than merely saying everyone appears in the same number of committees?

Open the answer

The contribution from a committee depends on its size. The relabeling argument preserves committee size as well as membership structure, so it preserves the actual weight \(1/|S|\), not just the number of committees.

Act 5: The factor n+1 and the boundary case

Clue 5 · Where does the factor n+1 come from?

Follow this clue: Multiply Alice's total by the number of students receiving equal totals.
Important qualification: the original sum is not the number of all nonempty subsets. Rather,
\[(n+1)\sum_{r=0}^{n}\frac{\binom nr}{r+1}=2^{n+1}-1.\]
Multiplying one student's weighted share by the n+1 symmetric students recovers the ordinary count of nonempty committees.

Stress-test: n = 0

Only Alice exists. The only nonempty committee is \(\{\text{Alice}\}\), so Alice receives one unit.

\[\sum_{r=0}^{0}\frac{\binom0r}{r+1}=\frac{\binom00}{1}=1,\qquad \frac{2^{1}-1}{1}=1.\]

Small step 5. Do we need n≥1 for this identity?

Open the answer

No. Unlike the toggle-Alice parity identity or the president-first story, this identity also works at n=0. Alice herself supplies the distinguished student, and the one nonempty committee gives her one unit.

Act 6: Factorial and reindexing proof, one step at a time

The weighted argument already proves the identity. Now transform each summand into a shifted binomial coefficient.

Step 1 · Expand the binomial coefficient.
\[\frac{\binom nr}{r+1}=\frac1{r+1}\cdot\frac{n!}{r!(n-r)!}.\]
Why? Use \(\binom nr=n!/[r!(n-r)!]\), then keep the extra factor \(1/(r+1)\) visible.

Small step 6. Which factorial can absorb the factor r+1?

Open the answer

Since \((r+1)!=(r+1)r!\), the product \((r+1)r!\) in the denominator becomes \((r+1)!\).

Step 2 · Combine r+1 with r!.
\[ \frac1{r+1}\cdot\frac{n!}{r!(n-r)!} =\frac{n!}{(r+1)!(n-r)!}. \]
Why? The denominator \((r+1)r!(n-r)!\) is exactly \((r+1)!(n-r)!\).

Small step 7. What factor should we introduce if we want the numerator to become \((n+1)!\)?

Open the answer

We need a factor n+1, because \((n+1)!=(n+1)n!\). To keep the value unchanged, multiply by \((n+1)/(n+1)\).

Step 3 · Create the factorial of n+1.
\[ \frac{n!}{(r+1)!(n-r)!} =\frac1{n+1}\cdot\frac{(n+1)n!}{(r+1)!(n-r)!} =\frac1{n+1}\cdot\frac{(n+1)!}{(r+1)!(n-r)!}. \]
Why is the outside factor \(1/(n+1)\) necessary? We inserted n+1 into the numerator, so dividing by n+1 compensates for it.

Small step 8. For a binomial coefficient \(\binom{n+1}{r+1}\), what should the second factorial in the denominator be?

Open the answer

It should be \([(n+1)-(r+1)]!=(n-r)!\), exactly the factorial already present.

Step 4 · Recognize the shifted binomial coefficient.
\[ \frac{\binom nr}{r+1} =\frac1{n+1}\frac{(n+1)!}{(r+1)!\,[(n+1)-(r+1)]!} =\frac1{n+1}\binom{n+1}{r+1}. \]

So term by term,

\[\boxed{\frac{\binom nr}{r+1}=\frac1{n+1}\binom{n+1}{r+1}}.\]
Step 5 · Substitute this into the whole sum.
\[ \sum_{r=0}^{n}\frac{\binom nr}{r+1} =\frac1{n+1}\sum_{r=0}^{n}\binom{n+1}{r+1}. \]
Why can \(1/(n+1)\) move outside? It is constant with respect to the summation index r.

Small step 9. If we set j=r+1, what are the new lower and upper limits?

Open the answer

When r=0, j=1. When r=n, j=n+1. Therefore j runs from 1 through n+1.

Step 6 · Reindex with j=r+1.
\[ \frac1{n+1}\sum_{r=0}^{n}\binom{n+1}{r+1} =\frac1{n+1}\sum_{j=1}^{n+1}\binom{n+1}{j}. \]
What changed? Only the label of the running index and its limits. The actual list of terms is the same.

Small step 10. What binomial-theorem sum do we know if the missing j=0 term is restored?

Open the answer

\(\sum_{j=0}^{n+1}\binom{n+1}{j}=2^{n+1}\). The omitted j=0 term is \(\binom{n+1}{0}=1\).

Step 7 · Remove the missing empty-subset term.
\[ \sum_{j=1}^{n+1}\binom{n+1}{j} =\sum_{j=0}^{n+1}\binom{n+1}{j}-\binom{n+1}{0} =2^{n+1}-1. \]

Hence

\[ \boxed{\sum_{r=0}^{n}\frac{\binom nr}{r+1} =\frac{2^{n+1}-1}{n+1}}. \]

Full factorial proof in one continuous chain

\[ \begin{aligned} \sum_{r=0}^{n}\frac{\binom nr}{r+1} &=\sum_{r=0}^{n}\frac{n!}{(r+1)!(n-r)!}\\[4pt] &=\frac1{n+1}\sum_{r=0}^{n}\frac{(n+1)!}{(r+1)!(n-r)!}\\[4pt] &=\frac1{n+1}\sum_{r=0}^{n}\binom{n+1}{r+1}\\[4pt] &=\frac1{n+1}\sum_{j=1}^{n+1}\binom{n+1}{j}\\[4pt] &=\frac1{n+1}\left(2^{n+1}-1\right). \end{aligned} \]

Act 7: Integration proof, one step at a time

The denominator r+1 also appears naturally when we integrate a power \(t^r\). Start again from the binomial theorem.

Step 1 · Write the binomial theorem with a variable t.
\[(1+t)^n=\sum_{r=0}^{n}\binom nr t^r.\]
Why use t? Integrating \(t^r\) creates the factor \(1/(r+1)\) that appears in the target sum.

Small step 11. What is \(\int t^r\,dt\) for r≥0?

Open the answer

\(\int t^r\,dt=t^{r+1}/(r+1)+C\). The denominator r+1 is exactly the reciprocal weight in our identity.

Step 2 · Integrate both sides from 0 to 1.
\[ \int_0^1(1+t)^n\,dt =\int_0^1\sum_{r=0}^{n}\binom nr t^r\,dt. \]

Because the sum is finite, integrate term by term:

\[ \int_0^1(1+t)^n\,dt =\sum_{r=0}^{n}\binom nr\int_0^1t^r\,dt. \]
Why is term-by-term integration valid here? There are only n+1 terms, so ordinary linearity of integration is enough; no infinite-series issue arises.

Small step 12. What does \(\int_0^1 t^r\,dt\) equal?

Open the answer

\(\left[t^{r+1}/(r+1)\right]_0^1=(1-0)/(r+1)=1/(r+1)\).

Step 3 · Evaluate the right side.
\[ \sum_{r=0}^{n}\binom nr\int_0^1t^r\,dt =\sum_{r=0}^{n}\binom nr\frac1{r+1} =\sum_{r=0}^{n}\frac{\binom nr}{r+1}. \]
What did integration do? It converted each factor \(t^r\) into the weight \(1/(r+1)\) after evaluation from 0 to 1.

Small step 13. What antiderivative should we use for \((1+t)^n\)?

Open the answer

\(\dfrac{(1+t)^{n+1}}{n+1}\), because differentiating it gives \((1+t)^n\).

Step 4 · Evaluate the left side.
\[ \begin{aligned} \int_0^1(1+t)^n\,dt &=\left[\frac{(1+t)^{n+1}}{n+1}\right]_{0}^{1}\\[4pt] &=\frac{(1+1)^{n+1}-(1+0)^{n+1}}{n+1}\\[4pt] &=\frac{2^{n+1}-1}{n+1}. \end{aligned} \]
Where does the “−1” come from? It is the lower endpoint: \((1+0)^{n+1}=1\).
Clue 6 · In the integration proof, which operation creates the reciprocal factor \(1/(r+1)\)?

Follow this clue: The power rule for integration increases the exponent and divides by the new exponent.

Full integration proof in one continuous chain

\[ \begin{aligned} \sum_{r=0}^{n}\frac{\binom nr}{r+1} &=\sum_{r=0}^{n}\binom nr\int_0^1t^r\,dt\\[4pt] &=\int_0^1\sum_{r=0}^{n}\binom nr t^r\,dt\\[4pt] &=\int_0^1(1+t)^n\,dt\\[4pt] &=\left[\frac{(1+t)^{n+1}}{n+1}\right]_0^1\\[4pt] &=\frac{2^{n+1}-1}{n+1}. \end{aligned} \]

Final case report

For every nonnegative integer n,

\[\boxed{\sum_{r=0}^{n}\frac{\binom nr}{r+1}=\frac{2^{n+1}-1}{n+1}}.\]

Weighted meaning: the left side is Alice's total voting credit over all nonempty committees.

Global count: the committees distribute \(2^{n+1}-1\) total units.

Symmetry: all n+1 students receive equal totals, so Alice receives \((2^{n+1}-1)/(n+1)\).

Factorial mechanism: \(\binom nr/(r+1)=\frac1{n+1}\binom{n+1}{r+1}\), and summing the shifted coefficients counts all nonempty subsets.

Integration mechanism: \(\int_0^1t^r\,dt=1/(r+1)\), so integrating the binomial theorem produces the weighted sum automatically.
