MATH DETECTIVE: CASE FILE #25

Subject: Why Second-Degree Equations Give Only a Few Types of Loci

Mission: Strip away position and direction, follow every possible case, and explain what the discriminant can—and cannot—tell us.

Detective Briefing: Six coefficients look like six ways to invent a new shape. But some only move or turn a familiar shape. Can we prove that our list leaves nothing out?

Choose an answer before opening its explanation. “Real locus” means all real points \((x,y)\) that satisfy the equation. The diagrams and questions work offline; equation formatting and the display fonts need internet access.

Act 1: What are we allowed to assume?

\[ Ax^2+2Hxy+By^2+2Gx+2Fy+C=0, \qquad (A,H,B)\ne(0,0,0). \]

All six coefficients are real. The coefficients of the mixed and linear terms are \(2H,2G,2F\), not \(H,G,F\).

Clue 1 · What immediate observation do you make about the condition on A, H and B?

Hint: Degree means the highest total power with a nonzero coefficient. The term xy has degree 2.

Open the reasoning

At least one of \(x^2,xy,y^2\) must have a nonzero coefficient. In particular, \(xy=0\) is a second-degree equation even though the two square terms are absent.

If \(A=H=B=0\), we get \(2Gx+2Fy+C=0\). It describes a line if \((G,F)\ne(0,0)\); no points if \(G=F=0,\ C\ne0\); and the whole plane if \(G=F=C=0\).

Clue 2 · So we have to check the kind of shape, not just its position. What can we do first?

Hint: Moving the axes is allowed. Simply deleting terms changes the equation.

Open the reasoning

A rotation turns the axes; a translation moves their origin. Neither stretches the plane. Every point has exactly one old and one new coordinate pair, so no solutions are lost or invented.

Size can still vary: two ellipses may have different axis lengths. We seek a few types, not a claim that every ellipse is the same size or shape.

Act 2: Can we remove the mixed term?

Start with \(Ax^2+2Hxy+By^2\). It tells us which squared directions are present. A useful way to store its coefficients is

\[ Q=\begin{pmatrix}A&H\\H&B\end{pmatrix},\qquad \begin{pmatrix}x&y\end{pmatrix}Q\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\end{pmatrix} =Ax^2+2Hxy+By^2. \]

The two off-diagonal entries each contribute \(Hxy\). That explains the factor 2. No matrix method is needed for the next step.

Turn the axes through an angle \(\theta\):

\[ x=u\cos\theta-v\sin\theta,\qquad y=u\sin\theta+v\cos\theta. \]

On substituting, the coefficient of \(uv\) is

\[ 2H\cos2\theta+(B-A)\sin2\theta. \]
Clue 3 · We want that coefficient to be zero. What can we choose?

Hint: For p cos φ + q sin φ, choose a unit direction perpendicular to (p,q). Handle p = q = 0 separately.

Open the reasoning and the special case

Put \(p=2H,\ q=B-A\). If \(p=q=0\), the expression is already zero for every angle.

Otherwise let \(r=\sqrt{p^2+q^2}>0\). Choose an angle with

\[ \cos2\theta=\frac{q}{r},\qquad \sin2\theta=-\frac{p}{r}. \]

This is possible because the two right-hand sides have squares adding to 1. Their substitution gives \(pq/r-qp/r=0\).

After rotation the whole equation becomes

\[ \lambda u^2+\mu v^2+2gu+2fv+C=0. \]

Here \(\lambda,\mu\) are the new quadratic coefficients; \(g,f\) are the new linear coefficients. Since the rotation can be undone, it cannot turn a nonzero quadratic part into zero. Thus \(\lambda,\mu\) cannot both be zero.

The first big split: either both squared terms remain, or exactly one remains. There is no third case.

Act 3: Two squared terms remain

Assume \(\lambda\ne0\) and \(\mu\ne0\).

Clue 4 · The mixed term is gone, but the linear terms remain. What can we do next?

Hint: A square such as λ(u + g/λ)² contains both λu² and 2gu. We know λ ≠ 0, not g ≠ 0.

Open the calculation
\[ \lambda u^2+2gu=\lambda\left(u+\frac g\lambda\right)^2-\frac{g^2}{\lambda}, \qquad \mu v^2+2fv=\mu\left(v+\frac f\mu\right)^2-\frac{f^2}{\mu}. \]

Both divisions are allowed in this case. Set

\[ U=u+\frac g\lambda,\quad V=v+\frac f\mu,\quad K=\frac{g^2}{\lambda}+\frac{f^2}{\mu}-C. \]
\[ \boxed{\lambda U^2+\mu V^2=K.} \]

Now only the signs and the remaining constant need attention.

Same signs

If both coefficients are negative, multiply the whole equation by \(-1\), including K. Below, \(\lambda,\mu>0\) and K means the right-hand side after this sign change.

Clue 5 · What immediate observation decides whether λU² + μV² = K has points when λ, μ > 0?

Hint: For the sum to be zero, both nonnegative terms must be zero—not just one.

Open the three cases

If \(K>0\), division by K gives

\[ \frac{U^2}{K/\lambda}+\frac{V^2}{K/\mu}=1. \]

Both denominators are positive, so this is an ellipse. It is a circle when \(\lambda=\mu\). If \(K=0\), only \(U=V=0\) works. If \(K<0\), no real point can work.

Opposite signs

Reorder the coordinates if needed so \(\lambda>0,\ \mu<0\). If \(K>0\), divide by K to get a hyperbola. If \(K<0\), multiply the equation by \(-1\), then exchange the roles of U and V. Again the form is

\[ \frac{U^2}{a^2}-\frac{V^2}{b^2}=1,\qquad a,b>0. \]
Clue 6 · Now K = 0, so division by K is not allowed. What can we do instead?

Hint: A product is zero when at least one factor is zero.

Open the factorisation
\[ \lambda U^2-|\mu|V^2 =(\sqrt\lambda\,U-\sqrt{|\mu|}\,V) (\sqrt\lambda\,U+\sqrt{|\mu|}\,V)=0. \]

Each factor describes a line through \((0,0)\). The lines are different because both square coefficients are nonzero.

Act 4: Only one squared term remains

Suppose \(\lambda\ne0,\ \mu=0\). Exchange the coordinates first if necessary. Complete the one available square:

\[ \lambda U^2+2fv+C'=0,\qquad U=u+\frac g\lambda,\quad C'=C-\frac{g^2}{\lambda}. \]
Clue 7 · We cannot complete a v-square because none is present. What should we check next?

Hint: If f ≠ 0, solve for v. If f = 0, v is completely free.

Open both branches

If \(f\ne0\), set \(V=v+C'/(2f)\). Then

\[ \lambda U^2+2fV=0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad U^2=-\frac{2f}{\lambda}V. \]

The multiplier of V is nonzero. This is a parabola; its sign selects the opening direction.

If \(f=0\), set \(k=-C'/\lambda\). We get \(U^2=k\). V may be any real number.

kAllowed UReal locus
\(k>0\)\(U=\sqrt{k}\) or \(U=-\sqrt{k}\)Two parallel lines
\(k=0\)\(U=0\)One double line
\(k<0\)No real UNo real points

“Double line” means the same linear factor occurs twice: \(U^2=U\cdot U\). As a set of points, it is just one line.

Act 5: Watch the cases, then move the axes

Try this: keep “two positive squares” selected and move K through zero. Then try opposite signs. Finally choose “one square” and switch the remaining linear term on and off.

Clue 8 · Have we proved a classification, or only collected examples? What closes the argument?

Hint: To prove a complete list, explain why no algebraic case has been left out.

Open the completeness argument

Rotation always removes the mixed term. A nonzero quadratic part then has either two nonzero square coefficients or one. With two, the signs are the same or opposite, and K is zero or nonzero (with its sign checked where needed). With one, the remaining linear coefficient is zero or nonzero. Every resulting case has been covered.

Reversible changes pair old points with new points. So every original real locus comes from one of these patterns—not necessarily the exact unit-sized equations in the opening table.

Act 6: What does D actually tell us?

We have learned to look at the signs of \(\lambda,\mu\). Can we read that information before rotating?

\[ D=H^2-AB,\qquad \lambda\mu=AB-H^2=-D. \]

The product is unchanged by rotation. One explanation is \(Q_{\rm new}=S^TQS\), where S is a rotation matrix. Since \(\det S=1\), both matrices have the same determinant.

DProduct \(\lambda\mu\)What follows?
\(D<0\)PositiveBoth coefficients are nonzero and have the same sign.
\(D>0\)NegativeThey have opposite signs.
\(D=0\)ZeroExactly one is zero; they cannot both be zero.
Clue 9 · We find D < 0. Can we immediately write ‘real ellipse’?

Hint: Compare x² + y² = 1, x² + y² = 0 and x² + y² = −1.

Open the counterexample

All three equations in the hint have \(D=-1\), but their real loci are a circle, one point and no points. The quadratic part gives the sign pattern, not the whole answer. The completed-square constant matters.

Likewise, \(D=0\) says “one squared direction,” not automatically “parabola.” We must check the remaining linear term.

Act 7: What does the full determinant add?

The full coefficient matrix also records the linear terms and constant:

\[ M=\begin{pmatrix}A&H&G\\H&B&F\\G&F&C\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \Delta=\det M. \]

Indeed, \(\begin{pmatrix}x&y&1\end{pmatrix}M\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\\1\end{pmatrix}\) is the original polynomial.

We are about to compute \(\Delta\) from a simpler equation. Why are we allowed to do that?

Why rotation and translation preserve this determinant

Write the coordinate change as

\[ \begin{pmatrix}x\\y\\1\end{pmatrix} =T\begin{pmatrix}U\\V\\1\end{pmatrix}, \qquad T=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta&h\\ \sin\theta&\cos\theta&k_0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}. \]

The new matrix is \(T^TMT\). Since \(\det T=1\),

\[ \det(T^TMT)=(\det T)^2\det M=\Delta. \]

So rotation and translation preserve \(\Delta\). Multiplying the whole equation by a nonzero number s is different: it multiplies \(\Delta\) by \(s^3\), though it leaves the zero/nonzero test unchanged.

Two nonzero squared coefficients

For \(\lambda U^2+\mu V^2=K\), the constant on the left is \(-K\), so

\[ M_{\rm new}=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda&0&0\\0&\mu&0\\0&0&-K\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \Delta=-\lambda\mu K. \]

Since \(\lambda\mu\ne0\), \(\Delta=0\iff K=0\). The earlier branches then give a point (same signs) or two intersecting real lines (opposite signs).

One nonzero squared coefficient

For \(\lambda U^2+2fV+C'=0\),

\[ M_{\rm new}=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda&0&0\\0&0&f\\0&f&C'\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \Delta=\lambda(0\cdot C'-f^2)=-\lambda f^2. \]

Since \(\lambda\ne0\), \(\Delta\ne0\iff f\ne0\): this is the parabola branch. If \(\Delta=0\), we return to \(U^2=k\), giving parallel lines, a double line, or no real points.

Meaning of “algebraically degenerate” here: the full matrix has determinant zero. This is an algebraic test. It is not a test for whether the real graph is empty.

Clue 10 · Both x² + y² + 1 = 0 and x² + 1 = 0 have no real points. What should we check before calling them degenerate?

Hint: The two full matrices are diagonal: diag(1,1,1) and diag(1,0,1).

Open the calculation
\[ \Delta_1=\det\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\0&1&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}=1,\qquad \Delta_2=\det\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\0&0&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}=0. \]

Both equations are impossible over the reals. Yet their determinant tests differ. Keep “real existence” and “algebraic degeneracy” as two separate questions.

Use the same original coefficient convention throughout: \(Ax^2+2Hxy+By^2+2Gx+2Fy+C=0\).

Why does \((A+B)\Delta\) settle real existence in the first row?

Derive the sign test

Rotation preserves the sum: \(A+B=\lambda+\mu\). Keep the signed coefficients and K from the same equation, before any sign normalisation. For \(D<0\), \(\lambda,\mu\) have the same sign and \(\lambda\mu>0\).

\[ (A+B)\Delta=-(\lambda+\mu)\lambda\mu K. \]

This is negative precisely when K has the same sign as \(\lambda,\mu\). That is exactly when the two squared terms can equal nonzero K and form a real ellipse. The opposite sign gives no real points.

In this row neither \(A+B\) nor \(\Delta\) is zero. Also, scaling the original equation by \(s\ne0\) multiplies \((A+B)\Delta\) by \(s^4>0\). Thus its sign does not depend on which scaled equation we use.

Within the real-ellipse case, the circle condition in the original coordinates is \(A=B\) and \(H=0\). Then both squared directions have the same coefficient.

One full check: a moved and turned ellipse

Consider \(5x^2+6xy+5y^2-16x-16y+8=0\). First read the coefficients carefully: \(A=B=5,\ H=3,\ G=F=-8,\ C=8\).

We find \(D=3^2-5\cdot5=-16\) and \(\Delta=-128\). Also \((A+B)\Delta=10(-128)<0\). The table predicts a real ellipse. Can the coordinate changes confirm it?

\[ u=\frac{x+y}{\sqrt2},\qquad v=\frac{-x+y}{\sqrt2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 8u^2+2v^2-16\sqrt2\,u+8=0. \]

Complete the square, then translate:

\[ 8(u-\sqrt2)^2+2v^2=8,\qquad U=u-\sqrt2,\quad V=v. \]
\[ \boxed{U^2+\frac{V^2}{4}=1.} \]

The ellipse is centered at \((1,1)\) in the original plane and has turned axes. Both the sign tests and the direct calculation give the same answer.

Act 9: When is the hyperbola rectangular?

“Rectangular hyperbola” means its two asymptotes meet at a right angle. First make sure the equation is a nondegenerate hyperbola: \(D>0,\ \Delta\ne0\).

Clue 11 · Now we observe A + B = 0. What does this tell us about its two squared coefficients?

Hint: Rotation keeps λ + μ = A + B. For this hyperbola λ and μ are already nonzero with opposite signs.

Follow the conclusion in both directions
\[ A+B=0\iff \lambda+\mu=0\iff |\lambda|=|\mu|. \]

In the standard hyperbola equation \(U^2/a^2-V^2/b^2=1\), equal coefficient magnitudes give \(a=b\). The asymptotes are \(V=\pm(b/a)U\).

Their direction vectors \((a,b)\) and \((a,-b)\) have dot product \(a^2-b^2\). This is zero exactly when \(a=b\), since a and b are positive. So the asymptotes are perpendicular exactly in this case.

\[ \boxed{A+B=0\iff\text{rectangular hyperbola}} \]

Only after checking \(D>0,\Delta\ne0\). For example, \(x^2-y^2=0\) also has \(A+B=0\), but is a pair of intersecting lines, not a nondegenerate hyperbola.

Final Report: Why the list ends here

Rotate → count the squared terms → complete squares → check signs and the remaining linear term.

Every nonzero quadratic part enters this chain. Every branch has been covered, and every coordinate change can be undone. That is why the list is complete.

D tells us the signs of the squared terms. \(\Delta\) tests algebraic degeneracy and separates the parabola from the other one-square cases. Real existence can still require a constant-sign check.
