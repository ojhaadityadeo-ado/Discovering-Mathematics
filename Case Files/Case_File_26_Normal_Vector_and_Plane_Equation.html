MATH DETECTIVE: CASE FILE #26

Subject: Why a Normal Vector Determines a Plane Equation

Mission: Find an equation that allows every movement along a plane and rules out every movement away from it.

Detective Briefing: A tabletop has many directions along its surface. Yet one perpendicular direction, together with one point, is enough to pin down its plane. Why?

Choose an answer before opening its reasoning. The questions and visual lab work offline; equation formatting and display fonts need internet access. We name the fixed point \(A_0\), so it is not confused with the coefficient A.

Act 1: What information fixes a plane?

A line has one direction, with two opposite senses. A plane has infinitely many directions. On a tabletop, you can move north, east, diagonally, or by combining two independent directions.

Clue 1 · Suppose we know one point and one direction along a plane. What immediate observation do you make?

Hint: Picture a door turning around its hinge. The hinge stays fixed while the plane of the door changes.

Open the reasoning

One direction and one point fix a line. Infinitely many planes contain that line. We need a second independent direction along the plane, or a direction perpendicular to the whole plane.

The setting

Let a plane \(\Pi\) pass through \(A_0\). Let \(\mathbf n\ne\mathbf0\) be a normal vector: a vector perpendicular to every direction along the plane.

\[ \mathbf a=\overrightarrow{OA_0},\qquad \mathbf r=\overrightarrow{OP}. \]

Here O is the coordinate origin; P is the point we want to test.

Clue 2 · Why must the normal be nonzero—and why do we also need the point A₀?

Hint: A vertical pole tells you that a floor is horizontal. It does not tell you the floor's height.

Open the reasoning

The zero vector has no direction. Its dot product with every vector is zero, so it cannot select a plane. A nonzero normal fixes orientation, but planes at different positions can share it. Requiring the plane to pass through \(A_0\) fixes the position too.

Act 2: Turn movement into an equation

To test whether P is in the plane through \(A_0\), first use the displacement from \(A_0\) to P:

\[ \overrightarrow{A_0P}=\mathbf r-\mathbf a. \]

What does a dot product tell us?

\[ \mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v =u_1v_1+u_2v_2+u_3v_3 =|\mathbf u|\,|\mathbf v|\cos\theta. \]

The first form uses coordinates. The second uses the lengths and the angle between two nonzero vectors. A zero dot product means they are perpendicular. If one vector is zero, the dot product is still zero, although there is no angle to assign to that vector.

Clue 3 · So we want an equation for movement along the plane. Which dot product should be zero?

Hint: The position vector r starts at O. The displacement we need starts at A₀.

Open the first direction of the proof

If \(P\in\Pi\) and \(P\ne A_0\), the vector \(\mathbf r-\mathbf a\) points along the plane. Therefore it is perpendicular to \(\mathbf n\). If \(P=A_0\), the displacement is zero and the same equation holds.

\[ P\in\Pi\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{\mathbf n\cdot(\mathbf r-\mathbf a)=0.} \]

Using \(\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf r=0\) instead would generally select the parallel plane through O. It agrees with our plane only when O is on it.

Clue 4 · Every point in the plane satisfies our equation. Have we finished? What must we check next?

Hint: A correct equation must include all the right points and no others.

Why the converse matters

So far, all plane points pass our test. We must still rule out an off-plane point that might pass it too. The next step isolates the part of a displacement that moves away from the plane.

Act 3: Which part of the movement does the dot product see?

Choose two perpendicular unit vectors \(\mathbf e_1,\mathbf e_2\) along the plane. “Unit” means length 1. Choose the third unit direction perpendicular to the plane:

\[ \widehat{\mathbf n}=\frac{\mathbf n}{|\mathbf n|}. \]

Why are we allowed to divide by \(|\mathbf n|\)?

Because \(\mathbf n\ne\mathbf0\), its length is positive. Division changes its length to 1 without changing its direction.

These three mutually perpendicular unit directions act like a new set of three coordinate axes. Every displacement in space has a unique expression

\[ \mathbf r-\mathbf a=u\mathbf e_1+v\mathbf e_2+w\widehat{\mathbf n}. \]

The numbers u and v describe movement along the plane. The number w describes signed movement perpendicular to it. Negative w means movement in the opposite direction to the chosen normal.

The anchor is \(A_0=(1,-1,\tfrac12)\). Reversing the normal keeps P fixed and reverses the sign of w. Scaling the normal changes the dot product's size, but not whether it is zero. Values are rounded for display.

Now take the dot product with \(\mathbf n\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf n\cdot(\mathbf r-\mathbf a) &=u(\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf e_1)+v(\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf e_2) +w(\mathbf n\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n})\\ &=u\cdot0+v\cdot0+w|\mathbf n|\\ &=w|\mathbf n|. \end{aligned} \]

The first two terms vanish because \(\mathbf e_1,\mathbf e_2\) are perpendicular to the normal. The last dot product is \(|\mathbf n|\cdot1\cdot\cos0=|\mathbf n|\).

Clue 5 · The equation says w|n| = 0. What immediate observation finishes the converse?

Hint: The along-plane contributions already have dot product zero; they cannot cancel a nonzero normal contribution.

Finish both directions

From \(w|\mathbf n|=0\) and \(|\mathbf n|>0\), we get \(w=0\). Hence \(\mathbf r-\mathbf a=u\mathbf e_1+v\mathbf e_2\), entirely along the plane through \(A_0\). Therefore P is in that plane.

\[ \boxed{P\in\Pi\iff\mathbf n\cdot(\mathbf r-\mathbf a)=0.} \]

The equation allows any u and v, while requiring zero perpendicular movement. That is why it describes a whole plane.

Act 4: Read the Cartesian equation

Let \(\mathbf n=(A,B,C)\), \(A_0=(x_0,y_0,z_0)\), and \(P=(x,y,z)\). Expanding the dot product gives

\[ A(x-x_0)+B(y-y_0)+C(z-z_0)=0. \]

Rearrange it as

\[ \boxed{Ax+By+Cz+D=0},\qquad D=-(Ax_0+By_0+Cz_0). \]
Clue 6 · What can we read immediately from the coefficients A, B and C?

Hint: These coefficients came from the normal vector in the dot product.

Open the meaning of the coefficients

The coefficients form a normal vector, not a general direction along the plane. A direction \(\mathbf d=(d_1,d_2,d_3)\) lies along the plane exactly when \(Ad_1+Bd_2+Cd_3=0\).

For fixed A, B, C, changing D changes the plane's position. The formula for D ensures the plane contains \(A_0\).

What if \(A=B=C=0\)?

The equation becomes \(D=0\). If \(D\ne0\), it has no solutions. If \(D=0\), every point in space is a solution. Neither set is a plane. Thus every Cartesian plane equation requires

\[ A^2+B^2+C^2>0. \]
A short worked check

Take \(A_0=(1,2,-1)\) and \(\mathbf n=(2,-1,3)\). The equation is

\[ 2(x-1)-(y-2)+3(z+1)=0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 2x-y+3z+3=0. \]

Substituting \(A_0\) gives \(2-2-3+3=0\), as required. The point \(P=(2,4,-1)\) also satisfies it. Its displacement from \(A_0\) is \((1,2,0)\), and \((2,-1,3)\cdot(1,2,0)=2-2=0\).

Act 5: Does changing the normal change the plane?

Clue 7 · We replace n by kn, where k ≠ 0, and keep A₀ fixed. What should happen to the set of solutions?

Hint: The equations q = 0 and kq = 0 have the same solutions when k is nonzero.

Open the algebra and geometry together
\[ (k\mathbf n)\cdot(\mathbf r-\mathbf a)=0 \iff k\bigl[\mathbf n\cdot(\mathbf r-\mathbf a)\bigr]=0 \iff \mathbf n\cdot(\mathbf r-\mathbf a)=0. \]

A positive multiplier changes the normal's length. A negative one also reverses its direction. Both preserve the plane through \(A_0\).

In Cartesian form, scale all four coefficients, including D: \(kAx+kBy+kCz+kD=0\). Scaling only A, B, C while leaving D unchanged can move the plane.

In the lab, choose \(2\mathbf n\), then \(-\mathbf n\). Notice that the dot product changes, but the zero test and the plane stay the same.

Act 6: Can three points supply the missing normal?

Suppose we know three points \(A_0,B_0,C_0\) that are not on one straight line. Form two displacements from the same point:

\[\mathbf u=\overrightarrow{A_0B_0},\qquad \mathbf v=\overrightarrow{A_0C_0}.\]

These are independent directions: neither is a multiple of the other. They give us two different directions along the plane.

Clue 8 · So we have two directions along the plane. What can we do to find a direction perpendicular to both?

Hint: A dot product is a number. We need a vector pointing perpendicular to both given directions.

Open the reasoning: what does a cross product do?

For \(\mathbf u=(u_1,u_2,u_3)\) and \(\mathbf v=(v_1,v_2,v_3)\),

\[ \mathbf u\times\mathbf v =(u_2v_3-u_3v_2,\ u_3v_1-u_1v_3,\ u_1v_2-u_2v_1). \]

Its dot product with each original vector is zero. For example,

\[ \mathbf u\cdot(\mathbf u\times\mathbf v) =u_1u_2v_3-u_1u_3v_2+u_2u_3v_1-u_1u_2v_3 +u_1u_3v_2-u_2u_3v_1=0. \]

The same cancellation works with \(\mathbf v\). Its length has a useful geometric meaning:

\[ |\mathbf u\times\mathbf v|=|\mathbf u|\,|\mathbf v|\sin\theta. \]

Here \(\theta\) is the angle between the vectors, from \(0\) to \(\pi\). This length is the area of the parallelogram they form. Independent directions give a positive area, so the cross product is nonzero.

Every movement \(s\mathbf u+t\mathbf v\) has dot product zero with \(\mathbf u\times\mathbf v\). Thus the cross product is a normal to their plane.

\[ \boxed{(\overrightarrow{A_0B_0}\times\overrightarrow{A_0C_0}) \cdot(\mathbf r-\mathbf a)=0.} \]

Two independent directions give the normal; the fixed point gives the position.

Why is this plane unique?

All points reached by

\[\mathbf r=\mathbf a+s\mathbf u+t\mathbf v,\qquad s,t\in\mathbb R,\]

form a plane. Any plane through all three points must contain the two directions \(\mathbf u,\mathbf v\), and therefore all these movements from \(A_0\). Two independent directions already fill a plane, so there is no room to choose a different plane through the same three points.

Clue 9 · Someone replaces “three noncollinear points” with “three distinct points.” What immediate problem do you see?

Hint: Try the points (0,0,0), (1,0,0) and (2,0,0).

Open the check: a whole family of planes

For \(A_0=(0,0,0),\ B_0=(1,0,0),\ C_0=(2,0,0)\),

\[ \mathbf u=(1,0,0),\quad \mathbf v=(2,0,0),\quad \mathbf u\times\mathbf v=(0,0,0). \]

Both \(y=0\) and \(z=0\) contain all three points, as does every plane

\[y\cos\phi+z\sin\phi=0.\]

These planes can turn around the common x-axis, like a door about a hinge. The zero cross product gives the equation \(0=0\), which describes all of space. It does not select one of these planes.

Follow one full example

Take \(A_0=(1,0,0),\ B_0=(0,1,0),\ C_0=(0,0,1)\). Then

\[ \mathbf u=(-1,1,0),\qquad \mathbf v=(-1,0,1), \] \[ \mathbf u\times\mathbf v =(1\cdot1-0\cdot0,\ 0\cdot(-1)-(-1)\cdot1,\ (-1)\cdot0-1\cdot(-1)) =(1,1,1). \] \[ (1,1,1)\cdot(x-1,y,z)=0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{x+y+z=1.} \]

Each of the three given points has coordinate sum 1, so all three pass the check.

What if we reverse the order of the cross product?

Then \(\mathbf v\times\mathbf u=-(\mathbf u\times\mathbf v)\). The equation becomes \(-x-y-z=-1\). We have reversed the normal, but the set of points is unchanged.

Act 7: Same direction—same plane?

Two planes have the same orientation when their normals are proportional:

\[\mathbf n_2=k\mathbf n_1,\qquad k\ne0.\]

They may be distinct parallel planes, or they may be the very same plane.

Clue 10 · The normals are proportional. What can we check next to tell whether the planes are the same?

Hint: The normals fix orientation. We still have to check position.

Open the reasoning and the point check

Write the equations as \(\mathbf n_1\cdot\mathbf r+D_1=0\) and \(\mathbf n_2\cdot\mathbf r+D_2=0\). Divide the second by \(k\):

\[\mathbf n_1\cdot\mathbf r+\frac{D_2}{k}=0.\]

If one point satisfies both equations, subtracting them gives \(D_2/k=D_1\). The equations then have exactly the same solutions. If the constants differ, no point can satisfy both: the planes are distinct and parallel.

This point check works after we know the normals are proportional. Two planes with different orientations can share a point without being the same plane.

Case closed: what did the equation really say?

\[ \boxed{\mathbf n\cdot(\mathbf r-\mathbf a)=0,\qquad\mathbf n\ne\mathbf0.} \]

Start at a fixed point of the plane. Move any amount along the plane. Allow no movement in the normal direction. That is the meaning of the equation.
