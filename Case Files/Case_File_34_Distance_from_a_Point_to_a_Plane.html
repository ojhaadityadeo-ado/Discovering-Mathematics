MATH DETECTIVE: CASE FILE #34

Subject: Distance from a Point to a Plane

Mission: Find the closest point on a plane, turn its normal direction into a distance, and explain every part of the formula.

Detective Briefing: A plane has infinitely many points. Which one is closest to a point P in space? We will first find the geometry, then let it guide the calculation.

Choose answers to 8 main clues. Pause at the small-step questions before opening their answers. The visual lab and answer checking work offline; equation formatting and display fonts need internet access.

Act 1: What does the starting condition mean?

Let

\[\Pi:Ax+By+Cz+D=0,\qquad P=(x_0,y_0,z_0).\]

The vector \(\mathbf n=(A,B,C)\) is normal to the plane: it points perpendicular to every direction along the plane. We require \(\mathbf n\ne\mathbf0\).

Clue 1 · What does \(\mathbf n\ne\mathbf0\) mean, and why do we need it?

Hint: A vector is zero only when all its components are zero.

\[|\mathbf n|=\sqrt{A^2+B^2+C^2}>0.\]

Small step 1 · If \(A=B=C=0\), what would the equation describe?

Check this step

It becomes \(D=0\). If \(D\ne0\), no point satisfies it. If \(D=0\), every point in space satisfies it. Neither case is a plane.

Small step 2 · Can a genuine plane have two zero coefficients?

Check this step

Yes. The plane \(z=4\) has \(A=B=0,\ C=1,\ D=-4\). Its normal is \((0,0,1)\), which is nonzero.

Our target is to explain, not just use,

\[\boxed{d(P,\Pi)=\frac{|Ax_0+By_0+Cz_0+D|}{\sqrt{A^2+B^2+C^2}}.}\]

Act 2: Find the closest point first

Draw the perpendicular from P to the plane, meeting it at H. Choose any other point Q on the plane. The segment HQ lies along the plane, so PH is perpendicular to HQ.

Clue 2 · We want to compare PQ with PH. What can we use immediately?

Hint: The extra movement from H to Q is at right angles to PH.

\[PQ^2=PH^2+HQ^2\ge PH^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad PQ\ge PH.\]

Small step 3 · When is the distance to Q exactly the same as the distance to H?

Check this step
\[PQ=PH\iff HQ^2=0\iff Q=H.\]

Thus H is the unique closest point. Every other point adds a positive squared distance.

If P already lies on the plane, take H = P. The same squared-distance identity holds, and every other Q has positive distance. No nonzero perpendicular segment is needed in this case.

Now our task is clear: find H, then calculate PH. The algebra below will also construct H directly.

Act 3: Use the normal to locate H

How can we move from P only in a direction perpendicular to the plane? Use a multiple of \(\mathbf n\):

\[\mathbf h=\mathbf p-t\mathbf n,\qquad H=(x_0-tA,\ y_0-tB,\ z_0-tC).\]

Here \(\mathbf p,\mathbf h\) are the position vectors of P and H. The real number t may be positive, negative, or zero. Choosing a minus sign does not restrict the direction of travel.

Small step 4 · What is the dot product of two vectors, and how does it describe perpendicularity?

Check this step
\[\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v=u_1v_1+u_2v_2+u_3v_3.\]
\[\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v=|\mathbf u|\,|\mathbf v|\cos\theta\quad \text{for nonzero }\mathbf u,\mathbf v.\]

For nonzero vectors, a zero dot product means \(\cos\theta=0\), so the angle is \(90^\circ\). If a displacement is zero, its dot product is also zero, though it has no direction.

Consider the condition

\[A(x_0-tA)+B(y_0-tB)+C(z_0-tC)+D=0.\]
Clue 3 · Why must this equation hold for the value of t we want?

Hint: The normal gives a line of possible points. The plane equation selects the point where that line meets the plane.

Small step 5 · Expand the substitution. Which terms contain t?

Check this step
\[Ax_0-tA^2+By_0-tB^2+Cz_0-tC^2+D=0.\]
\[Ax_0+By_0+Cz_0+D-t(A^2+B^2+C^2)=0.\]

Write \(E=Ax_0+By_0+Cz_0+D\) and \(N=A^2+B^2+C^2=|\mathbf n|^2\). The equation is \(E-tN=0\). Since \(N>0\),

\[\boxed{t=\frac{E}{N} =\frac{Ax_0+By_0+Cz_0+D}{A^2+B^2+C^2}.}\]

Small step 6 · Have we merely guessed H, or shown that exactly one such point exists?

Check this step

The equation \(E-tN=0\) has exactly one solution because \(N>0\). This gives one point on the normal line through P that also lies on the plane. Its displacement from P is parallel to the normal (or zero when P is on the plane), so it is exactly the perpendicular foot.

Act 4: Turn the parameter into a distance

Clue 4 · We have found t. Is t itself the perpendicular distance?

Hint: A multiple of a vector changes its length by the absolute value of the multiplier.

\[\overrightarrow{PH}=-t\mathbf n,\qquad PH=|-t\mathbf n|=|t|\,|\mathbf n|.\]

Small step 7 · Substitute \(t=E/N\) and \(|\mathbf n|=\sqrt N\). Why does only one square root remain below?

Check this step
\[PH=\left|\frac E N\right|\sqrt N =\frac{|E|}{N}\sqrt N =\frac{|E|}{\sqrt N},\qquad N>0.\]
\[\boxed{d(P,\Pi)=\frac{|Ax_0+By_0+Cz_0+D|}{\sqrt{A^2+B^2+C^2}}.}\]

The numerator comes from substituting P in the plane equation. The denominator accounts for the length of the normal. The absolute value makes the result a nonnegative distance.

The same calculation also gives the closest point itself:

\[\boxed{\mathbf h=\mathbf p- \frac{\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf p+D}{|\mathbf n|^2}\mathbf n.}\]

Notice the square in the foot formula’s denominator, but only the length in the distance formula’s denominator. They calculate different things.

Act 5: Check both the foot and the distance

Take the plane \(2x-y+2z=9\) and \(P=(0,0,0)\). First write the equation with zero on the right:

\[2x-y+2z-9=0,\qquad \mathbf n=(2,-1,2),\quad D=-9.\]

Small step 8 · Calculate E, N and t. What does the negative sign of t mean here?

Check this step
\[E=-9,\qquad N=4+1+4=9,\qquad t=-1.\]

Because \(\mathbf h=\mathbf p-t\mathbf n\), a negative t makes the displacement point in the positive normal direction.

\[H=(0,0,0)-(-1)(2,-1,2)=(2,-1,2).\]

Small step 9 · Which two checks confirm that this point is the perpendicular foot?

Check this step
\[2(2)-(-1)+2(2)=9,\qquad \overrightarrow{PH}=(2,-1,2)=\mathbf n.\]

H satisfies the plane equation, and PH is parallel to its normal. Both checks matter: merely finding a point on the plane would not show it is the foot.

\[d=\frac{|-9|}{3}=3,\qquad PH=\sqrt{2^2+(-1)^2+2^2}=3.\]

Here t = −1, while the distance is 3. This makes the difference between parameter and distance clear.

What do the controls move?

Let \(H_0=(2,-1,2)\), \(\widehat{\mathbf n}_0=(2,-1,2)/3\), and \(\mathbf e=(1,2,0)/\sqrt5\). The vectors \(\widehat{\mathbf n}_0\) and \(\mathbf e\) have length 1 and are perpendicular.

\[ H=H_0+u\mathbf e,\qquad P=H+s\widehat{\mathbf n}_0,\qquad Q=H+q\mathbf e. \]

Thus PH = |s| and HQ = |q|. Q moves along one direction in the plane to illustrate the comparison. The proof in Act 2 applies to every point Q in the plane. The control s is measured using the original normal; choosing a negative equation multiplier reverses the displayed signed distance.

Numbers are rounded for display. Try the origin example, slide parallel to the plane, change the equation’s scale, and finally put Q at H.

Act 6: What does substitution measure?

Write \(E(\mathbf p)=\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf p+D\). From \(E=t|\mathbf n|^2\) and \(\mathbf p-\mathbf h=t\mathbf n\),

\[\frac{E(\mathbf p)}{|\mathbf n|} =(\mathbf p-\mathbf h)\cdot\frac{\mathbf n}{|\mathbf n|}.\]

This is the signed movement from the foot H to P in the chosen unit normal direction. Thus E is that signed distance multiplied by the normal’s length.

Clue 5 · What happens to E if we move P by a vector v parallel to the plane?

Hint: Expand the dot product of n with p + v.

Small step 10 · Show that the parallel movement contributes nothing to E.

Check this step
\[\begin{aligned} E(\mathbf p+\mathbf v) &=\mathbf n\cdot(\mathbf p+\mathbf v)+D\\ &=\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf p+D+\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf v\\ &=E(\mathbf p)+0. \end{aligned}\]

A parallel movement can change the location of the foot without changing the distance. All points in a plane parallel to Π have the same signed distance from Π.

Relative to the chosen normal, \(E>0\) labels one side, \(E<0\) the other, and \(E=0\) the plane. Ordinary distance keeps only the magnitude.

Act 7: Does changing the equation change the distance?

Multiply every coefficient by a nonzero number k. The equation describes exactly the same plane, because we can divide it back by k.

Clue 6 · How does the distance formula respond to this change?

Hint: A geometric distance must not depend on which proportional equation we choose.

\[\frac{|kE|}{|k\mathbf n|} =\frac{|k|\,|E|}{|k|\,|\mathbf n|} =\frac{|E|}{|\mathbf n|}.\]

Small step 11 · What changes if k is negative? What happens to t and the foot?

Check this step
\[\frac{kE}{|k|\,|\mathbf n|} =\frac{k}{|k|}\frac{E}{|\mathbf n|}.\]

The signed distance reverses, because the normal direction reverses. Also, \(t_{\rm new}=t/k\), so

\[\mathbf p-t_{\rm new}(k\mathbf n) =\mathbf p-\frac tk(k\mathbf n) =\mathbf p-t\mathbf n=\mathbf h.\]

The parameter may change, but the foot and ordinary distance do not.

Clue 7 · We know a point on the plane has distance zero. Does the reverse hold?

Hint: A quotient with a positive denominator is zero exactly when its numerator is zero.

\[\boxed{d(P,\Pi)=0 \iff |Ax_0+By_0+Cz_0+D|=0 \iff P\in\Pi.}\]

Act 8: Find the distance between parallel planes

First write the two equations with exactly the same normal:

\[\Pi_1:Ax+By+Cz+D_1=0,\qquad \Pi_2:Ax+By+Cz+D_2=0.\]

Choose any point P on the first plane. What immediate observation can we make?

\[Ax_0+By_0+Cz_0=-D_1.\]

Substitute this in its distance from the second plane:

\[d(P,\Pi_2)= \frac{|-D_1+D_2|}{\sqrt{A^2+B^2+C^2}}.\]

The result does not depend on which point P we chose. It is the common perpendicular separation of the planes:

\[\boxed{d(\Pi_1,\Pi_2)= \frac{|D_2-D_1|}{\sqrt{A^2+B^2+C^2}}.}\]
Clue 8 · For \(2x-y+2z-9=0\) and \(4x-2y+4z-30=0\), what should we do before subtracting constants?

Hint: The constants can be compared in this formula only after the x, y and z coefficients match.

Small step 12 · Normalize the second equation and calculate the separation.

Check this step
\[4x-2y+4z-30=0 \iff 2x-y+2z-15=0.\]
\[D_1=-9,\quad D_2=-15,\quad d=\frac{|-15-(-9)|}{3}=\frac6{3}=2.\]

We divided every coefficient by 2. Dividing only the constant would change the plane.

Check the distance using two actual points

The point \(H_1=(2,-1,2)\) lies on the first plane. Move 2 units in its unit normal direction:

\[H_2=H_1+2\frac{(2,-1,2)}3 =\left(\frac{10}3,-\frac53,\frac{10}3\right).\]
\[2\left(\frac{10}3\right)-\left(-\frac53\right)+ 2\left(\frac{10}3\right)=15,\qquad |H_2-H_1|=2.\]

The new point lies on the second plane, and the connecting segment is normal to both. This checks the answer geometrically.

If the normalized constants agree, the planes coincide and the distance is zero. If the normals are not parallel, the planes intersect, so their minimum separation is zero; the parallel-plane formula above does not apply.
