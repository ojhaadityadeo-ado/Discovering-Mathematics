MATH DETECTIVE: CASE FILE #35

Subject: The Shortest Distance Between Two Lines in Space

Mission: Find the part of a connector that moving its endpoints cannot remove, then prove that this remaining part is the shortest distance.

Detective Briefing: Two lines in space need not meet, even if their directions are not parallel. Such lines are called skew lines. We will explain the scalar triple product formula and why parallel lines need a different formula.

Choose answers to 8 main clues. Try the short small-step questions before opening their answers. The two labs and answer checking work offline; equation formatting and display fonts need internet access.

Act 1: What do the assumptions tell us?

Let

\[L_1:\mathbf r=\mathbf a+t\mathbf u,\qquad L_2:\mathbf r=\mathbf b+s\mathbf v,\qquad t,s\in\mathbb R.\]

The vectors \(\mathbf a,\mathbf b\) locate chosen points A and B. The direction vectors \(\mathbf u,\mathbf v\) are nonzero. For now, assume

\[\mathbf u\times\mathbf v\ne\mathbf0.\]
Clue 1 · What does this extra condition mean?

Hint: Intersecting lines can also have nonparallel directions.

Small step 1 · What would fail if \(\mathbf u=\mathbf0\)?

Check this step

Then \(\mathbf r=\mathbf a+t\mathbf0=\mathbf a\) gives only one point, not a line. The same applies to \(\mathbf v=\mathbf0\).

Small step 2 · What is the cross product, and why can we use it here?

Check this step
\[\mathbf u\times\mathbf v= (u_2v_3-u_3v_2,\ u_3v_1-u_1v_3,\ u_1v_2-u_2v_1).\]
\[|\mathbf u\times\mathbf v|=|\mathbf u|\,|\mathbf v|\sin\theta.\]

For nonparallel nonzero vectors, \(0<\theta<\pi\), so this length is positive. The cross product is perpendicular to both vectors. Its length is also the area of their parallelogram.

We will prove, for all nonparallel lines,

\[\boxed{d(L_1,L_2)= \frac{|(\mathbf b-\mathbf a)\cdot(\mathbf u\times\mathbf v)|} {|\mathbf u\times\mathbf v|}.}\]

This includes skew lines and intersecting lines. A formula for parallel lines will come later.

Act 2: Why is AB not necessarily shortest?

A and B are only our chosen starting points. We can slide either endpoint along its line. So we seek points P on the first line and Q on the second that make PQ as short as possible.

Clue 2 · Suppose a nonzero connector PQ is shortest. What can we say about its direction?

Hint: Hold Q fixed first. Which point of the first line is closest to Q? Then reverse the roles.

Why this is only the beginning of a proof

If PQ is not perpendicular to the first line, replacing P by the perpendicular foot from Q makes the segment shorter. The same argument applies to the second line.

So a nonzero shortest connector must be parallel to \(\mathbf n=\mathbf u\times\mathbf v\). But this does not yet prove that such endpoints exist. We must find them and show that no other connector is shorter.

If the lines intersect, the shortest connector is the zero vector. It has no direction; we will include that case directly.

Write any pair of endpoints as

\[P=\mathbf a+t\mathbf u,\qquad Q=\mathbf b+s\mathbf v.\]

Small step 3 · Subtract the starting point from the ending point. What is the connector vector?

Check this step
\[\overrightarrow{PQ}=\mathbf q-\mathbf p =(\mathbf b+s\mathbf v)-(\mathbf a+t\mathbf u) =\mathbf w+s\mathbf v-t\mathbf u,\qquad \mathbf w=\mathbf b-\mathbf a.\]

The minus sign in front of t comes from subtracting P.

Act 3: Find the part that cannot change

Make the common normal a unit vector:

\[\widehat{\mathbf n} =\frac{\mathbf u\times\mathbf v}{|\mathbf u\times\mathbf v|}.\]

Small step 4 · What does a dot product with a unit vector measure?

Check this step
\[\mathbf c\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n} =|\mathbf c|\cos\phi.\]

It is the signed component of \(\mathbf c\) along the unit normal. In coordinates, \(\mathbf c\cdot\mathbf d=c_1d_1+c_2d_2+c_3d_3\). For nonzero vectors, the other description is \(\mathbf c\cdot\mathbf d=|\mathbf c||\mathbf d|\cos\phi\).

The unit length matters: without it, the dot product would also include the length of the chosen normal.

Take the dot product of the connector with this unit normal:

\[\begin{aligned} (\mathbf w+s\mathbf v-t\mathbf u)\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n} &=\mathbf w\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n} +s(\mathbf v\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}) -t(\mathbf u\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}). \end{aligned}\]
Clue 3 · What immediate observation removes the two terms involving s and t?

Hint: The normal was chosen to be perpendicular to both line directions.

\[\boxed{\overrightarrow{PQ}\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n} =\mathbf w\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}.}\]

This signed component is fixed for every pair P, Q. Sliding the endpoints changes the connector, but cannot change this component.

Small step 5 · Why must every connector have length at least the magnitude of this component?

Check this step
\[|\mathbf c\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}| =|\mathbf c|\,|\cos\phi|\le|\mathbf c|.\]

This is also true for \(\mathbf c=\mathbf0\). Apply it to \(\mathbf c=\overrightarrow{PQ}\):

\[PQ\ge|\mathbf w\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}| =\frac{|\mathbf w\cdot(\mathbf u\times\mathbf v)|}{|\mathbf u\times\mathbf v|}.\]

We have proved a lower bound: no connector can be shorter than this number. We have not yet proved that any connector reaches it.

Act 4: Can we reach the lower bound?

The vectors \(\mathbf u,\mathbf v\) are independent: neither is a multiple of the other. They span a plane of directions. The nonzero normal points outside that plane, so the three vectors \(\mathbf u,\mathbf v,\widehat{\mathbf n}\) form a basis of space.

This means every vector has one unique expression using them:

\[\mathbf w=\alpha\mathbf u+\beta\mathbf v+\gamma\widehat{\mathbf n}.\]

Small step 6 · Why is \(\gamma=\mathbf w\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}\)?

Check this step
\[\mathbf w\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n} =\alpha\,0+\beta\,0+\gamma\,1=\gamma.\]

We used perpendicularity and \(\widehat{\mathbf n}\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}=1\). The vectors \(\mathbf u,\mathbf v\) need not be perpendicular to each other, so α and β are not generally their simple dot products with w.

Clue 4 · We want to remove the αu and βv terms from w + sv − tu. Which parameters do that?

Hint: Check the minus sign in −tu and the plus sign in +sv separately.

Small step 7 · Substitute these parameters and show every cancellation.

Check this step
\[\begin{aligned} \overrightarrow{PQ} &=\alpha\mathbf u+\beta\mathbf v+\gamma\widehat{\mathbf n} +(-\beta)\mathbf v-\alpha\mathbf u\\ &=(\alpha-\alpha)\mathbf u+(\beta-\beta)\mathbf v +\gamma\widehat{\mathbf n}\\ &=\gamma\widehat{\mathbf n}. \end{aligned}\]

This is a real connector between points on the two lines. Its length is

\[PQ=|\gamma\widehat{\mathbf n}|=|\gamma| =|\mathbf w\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}|.\]

Every connector is at least this long, and our chosen endpoints achieve that length. Therefore

\[\boxed{d(L_1,L_2)= \frac{|(\mathbf b-\mathbf a)\cdot(\mathbf u\times\mathbf v)|} {|\mathbf u\times\mathbf v|}.}\]

The formula gives an actual minimum, not just a bound that the lines might only approach.

Small step 8 · Why is this closest pair unique when the directions are not parallel?

Check this step

Any connector has the form

\[(\alpha-t)\mathbf u+(\beta+s)\mathbf v+\gamma\widehat{\mathbf n}.\]

The first two terms together are perpendicular to the last. Thus

\[PQ^2= |(\alpha-t)\mathbf u+(\beta+s)\mathbf v|^2+\gamma^2.\]

To reach the minimum \(\gamma^2\), the first vector must be zero. Independence of \(\mathbf u,\mathbf v\) then forces \(t=\alpha,\ s=-\beta\), one pair only. This includes the single intersection point when \(\gamma=0\).

Act 5: Move the endpoints and keep one component fixed

Start with the simple skew lines

\[L_1:(x,y,z)=(t,0,0),\qquad L_2:(x,y,z)=(0,s,3).\]
\[\mathbf u=(1,0,0),\quad \mathbf v=(0,1,0),\quad \mathbf w=(0,0,3),\quad \mathbf u\times\mathbf v=(0,0,1).\]

The formula gives d = 3. The closest endpoints are \((0,0,0)\) and \((0,0,3)\). But an arbitrary connector is \((-t,s,3)\), so

\[PQ^2=t^2+s^2+9\ge9,\]

with equality only at \(t=s=0\). The direction vectors are perpendicular, yet the lines do not intersect.

Act 6: Why does volume divided by area appear?

The cross product gives the area of the parallelogram spanned by \(\mathbf u,\mathbf v\). The absolute scalar triple product gives the volume of the parallelepiped made from \(\mathbf u,\mathbf v,\mathbf w\). This is a box that may be slanted.

Clue 5 · What does volume divided by base area measure?

Hint: For a slanted box, volume is still base area times perpendicular height.

\[\text{volume} =|\mathbf w\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}|\, |\mathbf u\times\mathbf v|, \qquad \frac{\text{volume}}{\text{base area}} =|\mathbf w\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}|.\]

Small step 9 · Why is that height not generally the length AB?

Check this step

The vector \(\mathbf w=\overrightarrow{AB}\) may include movement along the base plane as well as movement normal to it. Only its normal component determines the height. In our lab, the chosen A and B happen to be the closest pair, so AB equals the height; that is a special choice of representatives.

Clue 6 · Would choosing different starting points A and B on the same lines change the answer?

Hint: Write the new separation vector before taking its triple product.

Small step 10 · If \(\mathbf a'=\mathbf a+p\mathbf u\) and \(\mathbf b'=\mathbf b+q\mathbf v\), what happens to the numerator?

Check this step
\[\mathbf w'=\mathbf w+q\mathbf v-p\mathbf u.\]
\[\mathbf w'\cdot(\mathbf u\times\mathbf v) =\mathbf w\cdot(\mathbf u\times\mathbf v)+q\,0-p\,0.\]

The numerator is unchanged. The formula depends on the lines, not on which points we first chose.

What if we rescale or reverse a direction vector?

Replacing \(\mathbf u,\mathbf v\) by \(k\mathbf u,\ell\mathbf v\), with \(k,\ell\ne0\), gives the same lines after changing their parameters. The cross product becomes \(k\ell(\mathbf u\times\mathbf v)\). Both absolute numerator and denominator are multiplied by \(|k\ell|\), so the distance is unchanged.

Act 7: What does a zero triple product mean?

Clue 7 · For nonparallel lines, what follows if the scalar triple product is zero?

Hint: Use the endpoints constructed in Act 4, not just the lower bound.

Small step 11 · Why is the existence step essential to this conclusion?

Check this step

A zero lower bound alone says only \(PQ\ge0\), which is true for every pair of points. But we proved there are endpoints with connector \(\gamma\widehat{\mathbf n}\). If the triple product is zero, then \(\gamma=0\), so

\[\mathbf q-\mathbf p=\mathbf0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad P=Q.\]

They are the same point on both lines. Therefore the lines really intersect.

\[\boxed{\text{For nonparallel lines: }\quad L_1\cap L_2\ne\varnothing \iff (\mathbf b-\mathbf a)\cdot(\mathbf u\times\mathbf v)=0.}\]

A nonzero triple product means the nonparallel lines are skew. In the lab, h = 0 makes the lines intersect; h > 0 separates them. Perpendicular direction vectors alone do not force an intersection.

Act 8: What changes for parallel lines?

When the directions are parallel, \(\mathbf u\times\mathbf v=\mathbf0\). The previous numerator and denominator are both zero. The fraction is undefined; it does not tell us the distance is zero.

Clue 8 · We still want to remove all possible movement along the lines. What should we do now?

Hint: There is now only one independent direction of motion along the lines.

\[\mathbf w_\parallel= \frac{\mathbf w\cdot\mathbf u}{|\mathbf u|^2}\mathbf u,\qquad \mathbf w_\perp=\mathbf w-\mathbf w_\parallel.\]

Small step 12 · Check that the remaining vector is perpendicular to u.

Check this step
\[\mathbf w_\perp\cdot\mathbf u =\mathbf w\cdot\mathbf u- \frac{\mathbf w\cdot\mathbf u}{|\mathbf u|^2}|\mathbf u|^2=0.\]

Endpoint movement can remove \(\mathbf w_\parallel\). For example, keep \(Q=B\) by taking \(s=0\), and choose \(t=(\mathbf w\cdot\mathbf u)/|\mathbf u|^2\). Then \(\overrightarrow{PQ}=\mathbf w-t\mathbf u=\mathbf w_\perp\). Movement along either parallel line cannot change \(\mathbf w_\perp\). Every connector has squared length \(|\mathbf w_\perp|^2\) plus a nonnegative squared component along u, and we can choose the endpoints to make that last component zero.

\[\boxed{d=|\mathbf w_\perp| =\sqrt{|\mathbf w|^2- \frac{(\mathbf w\cdot\mathbf u)^2}{|\mathbf u|^2}}.}\]

Small step 13 · Why can we write the same result with a cross product?

Check this step
\[|\mathbf w\times\mathbf u|^2 =|\mathbf w|^2|\mathbf u|^2-(\mathbf w\cdot\mathbf u)^2.\]
\[\boxed{d=\frac{|\mathbf w\times\mathbf u|}{|\mathbf u|}.}\]

For nonzero w this also says \(|\mathbf w_\perp|=|\mathbf w|\sin\phi\). If w is zero, both forms give zero directly.

For a concrete example, take

\[L_1=(t,0,0),\qquad L_2=(3+s,2,0). \quad \mathbf w=(3,2,0),\quad\mathbf u=(1,0,0).\]

Small step 14 · Find the distance and the condition on t and s for a closest pair.

Check this step
\[\mathbf w_\parallel=(3,0,0),\quad \mathbf w_\perp=(0,2,0),\quad d=2.\]
\[\overrightarrow{PQ}=(3+s-t,2,0),\qquad PQ^2=(3+s-t)^2+4.\]

The minimum occurs whenever \(t=3+s\). For every real s there is such a t. That gives infinitely many closest pairs.

Visual lab 2 · slide a closest pair togetherThe parallel lines stay 2 units apart. A shared slide changes both endpoints but not their separation.

With two independent line directions, there is one way to cancel the two removable terms. With parallel directions, the endpoints can slide together without changing their connector.

For distinct parallel lines the distance is positive and there are infinitely many closest pairs. If the lines coincide, the distance is zero and each point gives a pair with P = Q.
