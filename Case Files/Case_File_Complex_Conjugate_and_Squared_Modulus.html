MATH DETECTIVE: CASE FILE

Subject: Why a Complex Number Times Its Conjugate Gives Its Squared Length

Mission: Starting from a point \(P=(x,y)\) represented by \(z=x+iy\), discover why multiplying by \(\bar z=x-iy\) produces the real number \(x^2+y^2\), and then reinterpret the same result geometrically as two opposite rotations whose scale factors multiply.

The case: The squared distance of \(P=(x,y)\) from the origin is \(x^2+y^2\). Can complex multiplication manufacture exactly this real, nonnegative quantity—and explain why it must appear?

Follow the clues in order. Predict the next move before opening an explanation. The dynamic geometry reference is for intuition; the algebra and the scale–rotation interpretation provide the proof.

Act 1: Identify the geometric quantity

Let

\[z=x+iy.\]

The complex number corresponds to the point \(P=(x,y)\) in the plane. Its modulus is the distance from the origin:

\[|z|=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}.\]

Therefore its squared length is

\[|z|^2=x^2+y^2.\]
Clue 1 · If our target is \(x^2+y^2\), what must our complex multiplication somehow accomplish?

Follow this clue: Compare the desired \(x^2+y^2\) with what ordinary squaring produces.

Small step 1. Why work with the squared length rather than \(|z|\) itself?

Open the answer

The square removes the square root: \(|z|^2=x^2+y^2\). Polynomial expressions such as \(x^2+y^2\) are much easier to obtain from multiplication.

Act 2: Try the obvious multiplication—and see why it fails

What happens if we simply square \(z\)?

\[ \begin{aligned} z^2&=(x+iy)^2\\ &=x^2+2ixy+i^2y^2\\ &=x^2-y^2+2ixy. \end{aligned} \]

This is generally not real, and even its real part contains \(x^2-y^2\), not \(x^2+y^2\).

Clue 2 · What is the smallest change that can reverse the troublesome signs?

Follow this clue: The pattern \((A+B)(A-B)\) is designed to cancel cross terms.
Open the reasoning

The natural partner of \(x+iy\) is \(x-iy\). It changes only the sign of the imaginary part and is called the complex conjugate.

\[\bar z=x-iy.\]

Small step 2. Which familiar algebraic pattern are we trying to create?

Open the answer

The difference-of-squares pattern \((A+B)(A-B)=A^2-B^2\). Here \(A=x\) and \(B=iy\).

Act 3: Multiply by the conjugate

Now compute:

\[ \begin{aligned} z\bar z &=(x+iy)(x-iy)\\ &=x^2-ixy+ixy-i^2y^2\\ &=x^2+y^2. \end{aligned} \]
Clue 3 · Which two cancellations are doing the real work?

Follow this clue: Track the cross terms first, then use \(i^2=-1\).

But \(x^2+y^2=|z|^2\). Therefore

\[\boxed{z\bar z=|z|^2}.\]

Small step 3. Why is \(z\bar z\) automatically real and nonnegative?

Open the answer

Because it equals \(x^2+y^2\), a sum of two real squares. Hence \(z\bar z\ge0\).

Small step 4. When can \(z\bar z=0\)?

Open the answer

Only when \(x^2+y^2=0\), which forces \(x=0\) and \(y=0\). Thus \(\boxed{z\bar z=0\iff z=0}\).

Act 4: What conjugation does geometrically

Conjugation sends

\[(x,y)\longmapsto(x,-y).\]

So it reflects the point across the real axis.

Clue 4 · Which geometric features survive this reflection?

Follow this clue: Compare \(x^2+y^2\) with \(x^2+(-y)^2\).
Open the reasoning

Reflection across the real axis does not change the distance from the origin. Algebraically,

\[|\bar z|=\sqrt{x^2+(-y)^2}=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}=|z|.\]

Act 5: Read \(z\bar z\) through complex multiplication

For \(z\ne0\), write its polar form as

\[z=\rho(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta),\qquad \rho=|z|.\]

Its conjugate is

\[\bar z=\rho(\cos\theta-i\sin\theta)=\rho(\cos(-\theta)+i\sin(-\theta)).\]
Clue 5 · What should multiplication do to the two moduli and the two angles?

Follow this clue: Complex multiplication acts as scale × scale and rotation + rotation.

Therefore

\[ \begin{aligned} z\bar z &=\rho^2\bigl(\cos(\theta-\theta)+i\sin(\theta-\theta)\bigr)\\ &=\rho^2(\cos0+i\sin0)\\ &=\rho^2. \end{aligned} \]

But

\[\rho^2=|z|^2=x^2+y^2.\]

Geometric reading: multiplying by \(z\) scales by \(|z|\) and rotates by \(\theta\); multiplying by \(\bar z\) scales by the same \(|z|\) and rotates back by \(-\theta\). The rotations cancel, while the scale factors multiply:

\[|z|\cdot|z|=|z|^2=x^2+y^2.\]

The result lies on the positive real axis, exactly as the algebra predicted.

Small step 5. Why is the product positive real rather than merely real?

Open the answer

The net rotation is \(0\), not \(\pi\), and the net scale factor is \(\rho^2\ge0\). Thus the product lies on the nonnegative real axis.

Small step 6. What happens when \(z=0\), where the argument is undefined?

Open the answer

The polar-angle interpretation is unnecessary there: directly, \(0\cdot\overline0=0=|0|^2\). The identity still holds.

Act 6: Consequence — moduli multiply

Now let \(z,w\) be any complex numbers. The identity we proved lets us control the length of a product.

Clue 6 · If we want \(|zw|\), why is it natural to begin with \(|zw|^2\)?

Follow this clue: Reuse the identity with the single complex number \(u=zw\).
Step 1 · Apply the squared-modulus identity.
\[|zw|^2=(zw)\overline{zw}.\]
Step 2 · Conjugate a product factor by factor.
\[\overline{zw}=\bar z\,\bar w.\]

Hence

\[|zw|^2=zw\bar z\bar w.\]

Small step 7. What rearrangement becomes useful now?

Open the answer

Complex multiplication is commutative, so group \(z\bar z\) and \(w\bar w\): \(zw\bar z\bar w=(z\bar z)(w\bar w)\).

\[ \begin{aligned} |zw|^2 &=(z\bar z)(w\bar w)\\ &=|z|^2|w|^2. \end{aligned} \]

Both sides are nonnegative, so taking square roots gives

\[\boxed{|zw|=|z||w|}.\]

This matches the scale–rotation picture perfectly: multiplication by \(z\) scales every length by \(|z|\), and then multiplication by \(w\) scales by another factor \(|w|\).

Act 7: Quotients and reciprocals

If \(w\ne0\), then from \(|zw|=|z||w|\) we obtain

\[\boxed{\left|\frac zw\right|=\frac{|z|}{|w|}}.\]

The conjugate identity also gives a concrete formula for a reciprocal.

Clue 7 · Starting from \(z\bar z=|z|^2\), what should we divide by to isolate \(1/z\)?

Follow this clue: We want one side to become \(1/z\) and the other to involve \(\bar z\).

For \(z\ne0\), \(|z|^2>0\). Rearranging

\[z\bar z=|z|^2\]
gives
\[\boxed{\frac1z=\frac{\bar z}{|z|^2}}.\]

Small step 8. Why is \(z\ne0\) essential here but not in \(z\bar z=|z|^2\)?

Open the answer

The original identity is valid at \(z=0\). The reciprocal formula divides by \(z\) and by \(|z|^2\), so both must be nonzero.

Clue 8 · For a unit complex number, why does conjugation become the same as inversion?

Follow this clue: Substitute \(|z|=1\) into the main identity.

Final case report

Algebra:

\[(x+iy)(x-iy)=x^2+y^2.\]

Geometry of conjugation: \(\bar z\) is the reflection of \(z\) across the real axis and has the same modulus.

Geometry of multiplication: for nonzero \(z\), multiplying by \(z\) scales by \(|z|\) and rotates by an argument of \(z\); multiplying by \(\bar z\) scales by \(|z|\) and rotates back by the opposite angle modulo \(2\pi\).

\[\boxed{z\bar z=|z|^2=x^2+y^2}.\]

Consequences:

\[\boxed{|zw|=|z||w|},\qquad \boxed{\left|\frac zw\right|=\frac{|z|}{|w|}},\qquad \boxed{\frac1z=\frac{\bar z}{|z|^2}\ (z\ne0)}.\]
