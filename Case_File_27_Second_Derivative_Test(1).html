MATH DETECTIVE: CASE FILE #27

Subject: Why the Second Derivative Classifies a Stationary Point

Mission: Read the sign hidden in the definition of \(f''(c)\), follow the first derivative on both sides of c, and find exactly when the test can—and cannot—decide.

Detective Briefing: A stationary point has a horizontal tangent. That alone does not tell us whether the graph turns up, turns down, or passes straight through. The second derivative gives one more clue: how the slope changes near that point.

Act 1: What must be checked before the test?

A point \(c\) is a stationary point when

\[f'(c)=0.\]

The tangent is horizontal there. But a horizontal tangent can appear at a minimum, a maximum, or neither.

Strict local minimum For all sufficiently close \(x\ne c\),
\[f(c)
Strict local maximum For all sufficiently close \(x\ne c\),
\[f(c)>f(x).\]
Neither Nearby values occur on both sides of \(f(c)\), or the graph does not turn there.
The second derivative test

Suppose f is differentiable on an open interval around c, \(f'(c)=0\), and \(f''(c)\) exists.

\[ \begin{array}{c|c} f''(c)>0 & c\text{ is a strict local minimum}\\ f''(c)<0 & c\text{ is a strict local maximum}\\ f''(c)=0 & \text{the test gives no decision} \end{array} \]
Clue 1 · Before we use the sign of the second derivative, what must we check?

Hint: An extremum may occur at a height other than zero. The key question is whether the tangent at c is horizontal.

Open the reasoning

The test is about a stationary point. A positive second derivative says that slopes are increasing near c. It does not say that the current slope is zero. We need \(f'(c)=0\) so that the nearby slopes can be compared with zero.

We also need f to be differentiable near c. Otherwise the nearby values \(f'(c+h)\), used in the definition of \(f''(c)\), may not exist.

Act 2: What does a positive second derivative really say?

Start from the definition:

\[ f''(c)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f'(c+h)-f'(c)}{h}. \]

At a stationary point, \(f'(c)=0\). Therefore

\[ \boxed{f''(c)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f'(c+h)}{h}.} \]

This quotient compares the nearby slope with the zero slope at c.

Clue 2 · Let (L=f''(c)>0). What does the positive limit allow us to say?

Hint: A positive limit keeps nearby quotient values positive on both sides of h = 0.

Make “sufficiently close” precise

Take \(\varepsilon=L/2\). Since the quotient tends to L, there is a number \(\delta>0\) such that

\[ 0<|h|<\delta \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \left|\frac{f'(c+h)}h-L\right|<\frac L2. \]

Hence

\[ \frac L2<\frac{f'(c+h)}h<\frac{3L}{2}. \]

In particular, the quotient is positive for every sufficiently small nonzero h. We do not need its exact value.

Clue 3 · For a small negative h, the quotient (f'(c+h)/h) is positive. What sign must (f'(c+h)) have?

Hint: A positive number divided by a negative number would be negative.

Act 5: Stress-test the assumptions

Clue 6 · Did the proof need (f'') to be continuous near c?

Hint: Look back at the quotient: it contains nearby values of f′, not nearby values of f′′.

Where did each assumption enter?
  • f is differentiable near c, so \(f'(c+h)\) exists for small h.
  • \(f'(c)=0\), so the numerator becomes \(f'(c+h)\).
  • The nonzero limit \(f''(c)\) forces the quotient to keep its sign near h = 0.

We never used \(f''(c+h)\). It need not be continuous—or even exist—at every nearby point. Continuity of \(f''\) near c is a stronger condition often used in shorter textbook proofs, but it is not needed here.

Why does the stationary condition matter?

Consider

\[ f(x)=x+x^2,\qquad f'(x)=1+2x,\qquad f''(x)=2. \]

Hint: A positive change in slope does not make the present slope horizontal.

The test is a guarantee, not a list of every possible extremum. For example, \(f(x)=|x|\) has a strict local minimum at 0, but \(f'(0)\) does not exist. The second derivative test simply does not apply there.

Act 6: Why does a zero second derivative settle nothing?

Clue 8 · Suppose (f'(0)=0) and (f''(0)=0). What conclusion is valid?

Hint: Three functions can have the same two derivative values at 0 and still behave in three different ways.

FunctionFirst derivativeSecond derivativeBehavior at 0
\(x^4\)\(4x^3\)\(12x^2\)Strict local minimum
\(-x^4\)\(-4x^3\)\(-12x^2\)Strict local maximum
\(x^3\)\(3x^2\)\(6x\)Neither

For all three functions,

\[ f'(0)=0,\qquad f''(0)=0. \]
Check each behavior without guessing from its picture
  • For \(x\ne0\), \(x^4>0=x^4\big|_{x=0}\). Therefore \(x^4\) has a strict local minimum at 0.
  • For \(x\ne0\), \(-x^4<0=(-x^4)\big|_{x=0}\). Therefore \(-x^4\) has a strict local maximum at 0.
  • For \(x<0\), \(x^3<0\); for \(x>0\), \(x^3>0\). Values lie below and above \(f(0)=0\), so \(x^3\) has neither.

A zero second derivative means the quotient tends to zero. Its small values may be positive on both sides, negative on both sides, or have different signs. The limit zero does not record enough information to decide.
