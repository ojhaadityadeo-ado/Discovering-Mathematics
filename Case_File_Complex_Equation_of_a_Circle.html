Subject: A Circle Hidden in a Complex Equation

Mission: Turn an equation involving z and its conjugate into a distance statement. Find its center, decide whether a circle really exists, and show that every circle can be written this way.

The case: A locus is given by \(z\overline z+\overline a z+a\overline z+b=0\), where a is a fixed complex number and b is a fixed real number. The variable z represents the points we are looking for. How can we read the shape from this equation?

Try 8 main clues and the small-step questions before opening the explanations. The visual lab and answer checking work offline; equation formatting and display fonts use the internet. Print your notes before closing if you want to keep them.

Act 1: What would make this a circle?

A locus is the set of all points that satisfy a condition. A circle consists of points at one fixed positive distance R from one fixed point c:

\[|z-c|=R,\qquad R>0.\]

Here \(|z-c|\) is a distance, not just an algebraic symbol. If \(z=x+iy\) and \(c=p+iq\), then

\[|z-c|=\sqrt{(x-p)^2+(y-q)^2}.\]
Clue 1 · So we have to recognize a fixed distance. What should we try to make from the given equation?

Follow this clue: What expression already means the square of the distance from one complex point to another?
Open the reasoning

If we can rewrite the equation as \(|z-c|^2=K\), then c is the reference point and K is the proposed squared radius. We must still check the sign of K before calling the locus a circle.

Small step 1. What does \(w\overline w\) mean geometrically?

Open the answer
For \(w=s+it\), \(w\overline w=(s+it)(s-it)=s^2+t^2=|w|^2\). It is a squared distance from the origin, so it is real and nonnegative.
Why this matters: Our goal is not just to simplify the expression. It is to reveal a distance that stays fixed as z moves.

Act 2: Find the missing part of the square

The terms \(\overline a z+a\overline z\) are our clue. We want both of them to appear when we expand a squared modulus.

Clue 2 · Which product produces both mixed terms with the required signs?

Follow this clue: Expand just the two mixed terms in each choice. Which signs match the original equation?
Open the reasoning

Conjugation distributes over addition, so \(\overline{z+a}=\overline z+\overline a\). Therefore

\[\begin{aligned} |z+a|^2 &=(z+a)\overline{(z+a)}\\ &=(z+a)(\overline z+\overline a)\\ &=z\overline z+z\overline a+a\overline z+a\overline a\\ &=z\overline z+\overline a z+a\overline z+|a|^2. \end{aligned}\]

The last line only changes the order of a product and uses \(a\overline a=|a|^2\).

Small step 2. Which term is missing from the first three terms of the original equation?

Open the answer
The term \(|a|^2\). Adding it to \(z\overline z+\overline a z+a\overline z\) completes the squared modulus \(|z+a|^2\).

Now make the change without changing the solutions. First move b to the other side, then add \(|a|^2\) to both sides:

\[\begin{aligned} z\overline z+\overline a z+a\overline z+b&=0\\ z\overline z+\overline a z+a\overline z&=-b\\ z\overline z+\overline a z+a\overline z+|a|^2&=|a|^2-b\\ |z+a|^2&=|a|^2-b. \end{aligned}\]

Small step 3. Why is adding the same quantity to both sides important?

Open the answer
It keeps exactly the same solutions. Every step can be reversed. We have not merely found a condition that some solutions satisfy; we have found an equivalent equation.
\[\boxed{z\overline z+\overline a z+a\overline z+b=0 \iff |z+a|^2=|a|^2-b.}\]

This is completing the square in complex-number form.

Why this matters: A mixed expression has become one squared distance. The center and the sign check are now visible.

Act 3: Read the center, then check the sign

Clue 3 · The expression is \(|z+a|\). Which point is it measuring distance from?

Follow this clue: Write the expression in the form z minus a fixed point.
Open the reasoning

The displacement from the point \(-a\) to the point z is \(z-(-a)=z+a\). Its length is therefore their distance. So the reference point is \(-a\), not a.

Small step 4. As a quick sign check, which z makes \(|z+a|\) equal to zero?

Open the answer
Exactly \(z=-a\). This confirms the reference point. If \(a=1-2i\), then \(-a=-1+2i\): both coordinate signs reverse.

Let \(K=|a|^2-b\). Since a and b are fixed, K is fixed too. But a squared distance cannot be negative.

Clue 4 · If K is negative, what is the locus in the complex plane?

Follow this clue: A complex coordinate still represents a point with a real, nonnegative distance.
Open the reasoning

Although z can be nonreal, \(|z+a|^2\) is always a nonnegative real number. It cannot equal a negative K. There are no solutions in the complex plane in this case.

Sign of \(K=|a|^2-b\)What the equation saysLocus
K > 0\(|z-(-a)|=\sqrt K\)A circle, center \(-a\), radius \(\sqrt K\)
K = 0\(|z+a|=0\), so \(z=-a\)One point

Small step 5. When K is positive, why is the radius \(\sqrt K\), not \(\pm\sqrt K\)?

Open the answer
A distance is nonnegative. The real-number equation \(t^2=K\) has two signed solutions, but \(t=|z+a|\) cannot be negative. A radius has no minus choice.

Act 4: Which assumption made the equation real?

We began by assuming b is real. Why is that needed? Look at the first three terms before thinking about b.

The first is \(z\overline z=|z|^2\), which is real. The other two are conjugates of each other:

\[\overline{\,\overline a z\,}=a\overline z.\]

Small step 6. What happens when you add a complex number to its conjugate?

Open the answer
If \(w=s+it\), then \(w+\overline w=2s=2\operatorname{Re}(w)\). The imaginary parts cancel.

Consequently,

\[\overline a z+a\overline z=2\operatorname{Re}(\overline a z).\]
Clue 5 · Suppose b had a nonzero imaginary part. Could any z satisfy the original equation?

Follow this clue: The imaginary parts in the two mixed terms already cancel each other. What is left to cancel the imaginary part of b?
Open the reasoning

If \(b=p+iq\), the imaginary part of the entire left side is q, regardless of z. For the left side to be zero, q must be zero. So a nonempty locus requires b to be real.

Being real is necessary, but not sufficient: even a real b can give K < 0 and an empty locus.

Can we check the geometry in ordinary coordinates?

Write \(a=u+iv\) and \(z=x+iy\), with u, v, x and y real. Then

\[\begin{aligned} \overline a z&=(u-iv)(x+iy)=ux+vy+i(uy-vx),\\ a\overline z&=(u+iv)(x-iy)=ux+vy+i(vx-uy). \end{aligned}\]

Small step 7. Which parts cancel when we add these two lines?

Open the answer
The imaginary parts add to \(i(uy-vx+vx-uy)=0\). The real parts add to \(2ux+2vy\).

The original equation is therefore

\[x^2+y^2+2ux+2vy+b=0.\]

Completing the two real squares gives

\[(x+u)^2+(y+v)^2=u^2+v^2-b.\]

This has the same reference point \((-u,-v)\), which is the complex number \(-a\). Also \(u^2+v^2=|a|^2\), so the same sign test appears.

Why this matters: The complex notation has not created a new kind of distance. It packages the familiar two-coordinate circle equation into one squared modulus.

Act 5: Can we work backward from any circle?

So far, we have shown what the given equation describes. To show that the form covers every circle, start with an arbitrary center c and positive radius R.

\[|z-c|^2=R^2.\]

Expand step by step:

\[\begin{aligned} (z-c)(\overline z-\overline c)&=R^2,\\ z\overline z-\overline c z-c\overline z+|c|^2&=R^2,\\ z\overline z-\overline c z-c\overline z+|c|^2-R^2&=0. \end{aligned}\]
Clue 6 · Which choices of a and b turn this into our original form?

Follow this clue: Match the coefficient of z̄ first. Then compare the constant terms.
Open the reasoning

The coefficient of \(\overline z\) is \(-c\), so set \(a=-c\). Then \(\overline a=-\overline c\), which also matches the coefficient of z. The remaining constant is \(b=|c|^2-R^2\), a real number.

Small step 8. Do these choices automatically pass the positive-radius test?

Open the answer
Yes: \(|a|^2-b=|-c|^2-(|c|^2-R^2)=R^2>0\). The center is \(-a=c\), just as required.
Why this matters: We have proved both directions. Every equation of this form with K > 0 gives a circle, and every circle has such an equation.

Act 6: Read the coefficients carefully

Consider the equation

\[z\overline z+(1+2i)z+(1-2i)\overline z-4=0.\]
Clue 7 · Which value is a, and what center does it suggest?

Follow this clue: In the standard form, the coefficient of z is ā, while the coefficient of z̄ is a.
Open the reasoning

Compare like terms, not just the first coefficient you see:

\[\overline a=1+2i,\qquad a=1-2i,\qquad b=-4.\]

The center is \(-a=-1+2i\). We still need to compute the radius and check that it is positive.

Small step 9. What is \(|1-2i|^2\)? Is it the same calculation as \((1-2i)^2\)?

Open the answer
The squared modulus is \(1^2+(-2)^2=5\). It is not the ordinary square, which is \((1-2i)^2=-3-4i\). A squared modulus multiplies a number by its conjugate.
\[\begin{aligned} |a|^2-b&=5-(-4)=9,\\ |z+a|^2&=9,\\ |z+1-2i|^2&=9. \end{aligned}\]

Small step 10. Why is the radius 3, rather than 9?

Open the answer
The equation gives the squared distance. Taking the nonnegative square root gives \(R=\sqrt9=3\).
\[\boxed{\text{Center }=-1+2i,\qquad \text{radius }=3.}\]

With \(z=x+iy\), the same equation is

\[\boxed{(x+1)^2+(y-2)^2=9.}\]

Small step 11. Can we check a point on the circle directly in the original equation?

Open the answer
Take \(z=2+2i\), three units to the right of the center. Then \(z\overline z=8\), \((1+2i)z=-2+6i\), and \((1-2i)\overline z=-2-6i\). The left side is \(8-2-2-4+6i-6i=0\).

As a different check, the center itself is not on this circle: substituting \(z=-a\) gives \(b-|a|^2=-9\), not zero. The equation describes the circumference, not the filled disk.

Act 7: What else can the equation tell us?

We want to know whether the origin lies on the circle. What is the quickest next step?

Substitute z = 0. All terms except b disappear, so the original equation holds exactly when b = 0.

\[\boxed{\text{For a genuine circle, }0\text{ lies on it}\iff b=0.}\]

Small step 12. If b = 0, is the locus always a genuine circle?

Open the answer
No. We also need \(|a|^2>0\), so \(a\ne0\). If a = 0 and b = 0, the equation is \(|z|^2=0\), which gives only the origin.

For a genuine circle, the center’s distance from the origin is \(|-a|=|a|\). When b = 0, \(R^2=|a|^2\), hence \(R=|a|\). This is exactly the condition that the circumference reaches the origin.

More generally, for a genuine circle,

\[b=|\text{center}|^2-R^2.\]
Value of bPosition of the origin
b < 0Inside the circle, because \(|\text{center}|<R\)
b = 0On the circle
b > 0Outside the circle, because \(|\text{center}|>R\)

The equation contains \(\overline z\). Does that force symmetry about the real axis?

Clue 8 · What must be true for a genuine circle to be unchanged by reflection across the real axis?

Follow this clue: Reflection moves the center to its conjugate. When is that still the same point?
Open the reasoning

Reflection takes the circle with center c and radius R to the circle with center \(\overline c\) and the same radius. A genuine circle has a unique center, so the reflected circle is the same circle exactly when \(c=\overline c\).

Here \(c=-a\). This condition means \(-a=-\overline a\), or \(a=\overline a\): a is real.

The example has center \(-1+2i\), whose reflection is \(-1-2i\). They are different centers, so the reflected circle is different. Use the reflection checkbox in the lab to compare them.

Small step 13. Why is having conjugate terms not enough to guarantee real-axis symmetry?

Open the answer
Conjugate terms make the equation real-valued. Symmetry asks a different question: if z is a solution, must its reflected point \(\overline z\) also be a solution? For a genuine circle here, that requires a real center.

Final case report

For fixed complex a and real b,

\[z\overline z+\overline a z+a\overline z+b=0 \iff |z-(-a)|^2=|a|^2-b.\]

If \(|a|^2-b>0\): a circle with center \(-a\) and radius \(\sqrt{|a|^2-b}\).

If \(|a|^2-b=0\): just the point \(-a\).

If \(|a|^2-b<0\): no points.

Conversely: every circle with center c and radius R > 0 arises by setting \(a=-c\) and \(b=|c|^2-R^2\).

The useful habit is to ask: “What distance is this equation hiding, and can that distance actually exist?”
