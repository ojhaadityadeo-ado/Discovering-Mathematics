MATH DETECTIVE: CASE FILE #43

Subject: The Sum of Squares Identity

Mission: Count an n-person joint team from two schools in two different ways. Organize the teams by how many students come from the first school, explain why a square appears, then verify the identity by comparing the coefficient of \(t^n\).

The case: School A sends n students and School B sends n students. From all 2n students, exactly n must be chosen for one joint team. How many teams are possible?

Follow 7 clues and the small-step questions. Try each clue before opening its reasoning. The two-school team lab works offline; equation formatting and display fonts use an internet connection.

Act 1: Count the final team directly

There are n students from School A and n students from School B, so there are

\[n+n=2n\]

students altogether. We want one team containing exactly n students.

Clue 1 · What is the immediate count?

Follow this clue: Ignore the school labels for a moment. How many ways can we choose n people from one pool of 2n?
Open the reasoning

Once the 2n students are viewed as one combined pool, a valid outcome is simply an n-person subset. Therefore the direct count is

\[\boxed{\binom{2n}{n}}.\]

Act 2: Sort teams by their composition

Now count the same teams in a more structured way. Let r be the number of selected students from School A.

Clue 2 · If r students come from School A, how many must come from School B?

Follow this clue: The final team must contain n students in total.

If r places are filled by School A, the remaining number of places is

\[n-r.\]

For this fixed value of r:

\[ \underbrace{\binom nr}_{\text{choose r from School A}} \qquad\text{and}\qquad \underbrace{\binom n{n-r}}_{\text{choose n-r from School B}}. \]

Small step 1. Why can r range all the way from 0 to n?

Open the answer

A valid team may contain no students from School A, all n students from School A, or anything in between. So every integer r from 0 through n is possible.

Act 3: Why multiply—and why does a square appear?

Clue 3 · Why is the count for one fixed r a product?

Follow this clue: First choose the School A part, then independently choose the School B part.

The schools are disjoint groups. Once we choose an r-person subset from School A and an \((n-r)\)-person subset from School B, their union is one unique final team. Conversely, the final team uniquely tells us both school-specific selections.

\[\text{teams with r from A}=\binom nr\binom n{n-r}.\]
Clue 4 · Where does the square come from?

Follow this clue: Recall the symmetry identity for binomial coefficients.

By symmetry of binomial coefficients,

\[\binom n{n-r}=\binom nr.\]

Therefore

\[ \binom nr\binom n{n-r}=\binom nr\binom nr=\binom nr^2. \]
Keep the meaning clear: the square does not mean one subset is selected twice. One choice comes from School A and one from School B. Their numbers are equal because of binomial symmetry.

Act 4: Add all possible compositions

Every final team has one definite value of r: the number of its members who came from School A. Different r-values cannot describe the same team.

Clue 5 · Why can we sum from r=0 to r=n?

Follow this clue: Count how many School A students appear in one fixed final team.

Adding the counts of all composition classes gives

\[ \sum_{r=0}^{n}\binom nr\binom n{n-r}. \]

Using symmetry inside every term,

\[ \sum_{r=0}^{n}\binom nr\binom n{n-r} =\sum_{r=0}^{n}\binom nr^2. \]

But Act 1 counted the same collection directly as \(\binom{2n}{n}\). Therefore

\[\boxed{\sum_{r=0}^{n}\binom nr^2=\binom{2n}{n}}.\]

Small step 2. In the lab, why does changing r rearrange the teams into a different row without changing the grand total?

Open the answer

The value of r only tells us which composition class we are looking at. All classes together partition the same collection of n-person teams chosen from the same 2n students.

Act 5: Stress-test the ends—and the empty case

Endpoint r=0

No one is chosen from School A, so all n team members come from School B.

\[\binom n0^2=1.\]

Endpoint r=n

All n team members come from School A and none from School B.

\[\binom nn^2=1.\]
Clue 6 · What do the two end terms represent?

Follow this clue: Ask what r=0 and r=n say about the School A portion of the team.

Extra check: n=0

There are no students at either school, and we must choose a team of size 0. The empty team is the one valid outcome:

\[\sum_{r=0}^{0}\binom0r^2=\binom00^2=1=\binom00.\]

Small step 3. Does the identity require n≥1?

Open the answer

No. The identity also holds for n=0. Both sides equal 1, corresponding to the unique empty team.

Act 6: Coefficient proof, one step at a time

The double-counting proof already establishes the identity. Now verify it algebraically by comparing the coefficient of \(t^n\).

Step 1 · Write each factor using the binomial theorem.
\[ (1+t)^n=\sum_{r=0}^{n}\binom nr t^r. \]

For the second copy, use a different index:

\[ (1+t)^n=\sum_{s=0}^{n}\binom ns t^s. \]
Why use r and s? The two factors make two independent choices of exponents. Using different letters lets us track which exponent came from which factor.

Small step 4. If we multiply one term \(\binom nr t^r\) by one term \(\binom ns t^s\), what power of t appears?

Open the answer

The powers multiply as \(t^r t^s=t^{r+s}\). So the resulting exponent is r+s.

Step 2 · Multiply the two binomial expansions.
\[ (1+t)^n(1+t)^n =\left(\sum_{r=0}^{n}\binom nr t^r\right) \left(\sum_{s=0}^{n}\binom ns t^s\right). \]

A typical product term is

\[\binom nr\binom ns t^{r+s}.\]

Small step 5. Which pairs of exponents contribute specifically to the coefficient of \(t^n\)?

Open the answer

Exactly those pairs satisfying \(r+s=n\). Equivalently, once r is chosen, s must equal n-r.

Step 3 · Extract the coefficient of t^n on the left.

To obtain \(t^n\), we must choose exponent r from the first factor and exponent \(n-r\) from the second. Therefore

\[ [t^n]\big((1+t)^n(1+t)^n\big) =\sum_{r=0}^{n}\binom nr\binom n{n-r}. \]
Why do we add these products? Every pair \((r,n-r)\) produces another contribution to the same power \(t^n\). Coefficients of like powers add.

Small step 6. Why do no other r-values need to be considered?

Open the answer

Because r already ranges from 0 to n. For each such r, the required partner n-r also lies from 0 to n. These are exactly all pairs of allowed exponents whose sum is n.

Step 4 · Turn the left coefficient into the square sum.

Use binomial symmetry:

\[\binom n{n-r}=\binom nr.\]

Hence

\[ [t^n]\big((1+t)^n(1+t)^n\big) =\sum_{r=0}^{n}\binom nr^2. \]

Small step 7. Why is this square still a product of two different choices in the coefficient calculation?

Open the answer

The first factor contributes \(\binom nr\); the second contributes \(\binom n{n-r}\). They come from different factors. Symmetry tells us their numerical values are equal, which lets the product be written as a square.

Step 5 · Simplify the product before reading the right-hand coefficient.
\[ (1+t)^n(1+t)^n=(1+t)^{2n}. \]

Now apply the binomial theorem once more:

\[ (1+t)^{2n}=\sum_{k=0}^{2n}\binom{2n}{k}t^k. \]
What is the coefficient of \(t^n\)? Set k=n. It is \(\binom{2n}{n}\).

Small step 8. Why are we allowed to equate the two coefficients of \(t^n\)?

Open the answer

The two expressions are the same polynomial. Equal polynomials have equal coefficients for every power of t, so their \(t^n\) coefficients must match.

Step 6 · Equate the two t^n coefficients.
\[ \sum_{r=0}^{n}\binom nr\binom n{n-r}=\binom{2n}{n}. \]

Finally use \(\binom n{n-r}=\binom nr\):

\[ \boxed{\sum_{r=0}^{n}\binom nr^2=\binom{2n}{n}}. \]

Full coefficient proof in one continuous chain

\[ \begin{aligned} (1+t)^n(1+t)^n &=\left(\sum_{r=0}^{n}\binom nr t^r\right) \left(\sum_{s=0}^{n}\binom ns t^s\right)\\[4pt] &=(1+t)^{2n}. \end{aligned} \]

Comparing coefficients of \(t^n\),

\[ \begin{aligned} \sum_{r=0}^{n}\binom nr\binom n{n-r} &=\binom{2n}{n},\\[4pt] \sum_{r=0}^{n}\binom nr^2 &=\binom{2n}{n}. \end{aligned} \]

Small step 9. What is the algebraic proof doing that mirrors the two-school counting proof?

Open the answer

The first polynomial factor records how many students are chosen from School A, and the second records how many are chosen from School B. Extracting \(t^n\) keeps exactly those pairs of choices whose sizes add to n.

Act 7: What should we remember?

Clue 7 · What is the central reason the identity works?

Follow this clue: Ask what collection is being counted in Act 1 and again in Acts 2–4.

The main idea: count one collection in two ways.

Directly: choose n students from the combined 2n students, giving \(\binom{2n}{n}\).

By composition: for each r, choose r from School A and n-r from School B, giving \(\binom nr\binom n{n-r}\).

Why the square appears: \(\binom n{n-r}=\binom nr\), although the two selections come from different schools.

Final case report

For every nonnegative integer n,

\[\boxed{\sum_{r=0}^{n}\binom nr^2=\binom{2n}{n}}.\]

Combinatorial meaning: both sides count n-person teams chosen from two disjoint schools of n students each.

Why we multiply: a School A selection and a School B selection can be combined independently, and their union uniquely determines the pair.

Why we sum: every final team has exactly one value of r, the number of School A students it contains.

Why a square appears: the two factors count selections from different schools but have equal numerical values by binomial symmetry.

Why the algebra agrees: the coefficient of \(t^n\) in the product of two binomial expansions collects exactly the terms whose exponents add to n.
