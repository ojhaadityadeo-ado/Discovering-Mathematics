MATH DETECTIVE: CASE FILE #21

Subject: Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, Part II

Mission: Explain why any antiderivative gives the same definite integral, then use this idea when one or both limits move.

Detective Briefing: Part I gave us one antiderivative by adding up signed area. Part II explains why we may use any antiderivative: its starting height may change, but its total change does not.

Act 1: Why Does Any Antiderivative Work?

The statement we will prove

Let \(f\) be continuous on \([a,b]\), with \(a<b\). Let \(F\) be continuous on \([a,b]\), differentiable on \((a,b)\), and satisfy \(F'(x)=f(x)\) there. Then

\[\boxed{\int_a^b f(x)\,dx=F(b)-F(a).}\]

An antiderivative of f is a function whose derivative is f. We will use the same F at both endpoints.

Part I constructed a particular antiderivative:

\[A(x)=\int_a^x f(t)\,dt,\qquad A'(x)=f(x).\]

If F and A have the same derivative, must they be the same function?

Detective hint: Differentiate F − A. Then use the MVT result: a function with zero derivative throughout an interval is constant, including the endpoints when it is continuous there.
Step 1 · Compare the two functions.
\[(F-A)'=F'-A'=f-f=0\qquad(a<x<b).\]

By our earlier MVT result, \(F-A\) is constant in the interior. Both functions are continuous at the endpoints, so this same constant reaches \(a\) and \(b\). We justify the endpoint continuity of A in Act 3.

\[F(b)-A(b)=F(a)-A(a).\]
Step 2 · Use the two special values of A.
\[A(a)=\int_a^a f(t)\,dt=0,\qquad A(b)=\int_a^b f(t)\,dt.\]

Substitute these into the equality above:

\[ F(b)-\int_a^b f(t)\,dt=F(a)-0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{\int_a^b f(t)\,dt=F(b)-F(a).} \]

We do not need F to start at zero. We need its change from a to b. Any fixed difference between F and A cancels in that change.

Where does MVT enter, exactly?

Set \(H=F-A\). Once endpoint continuity is known, H is continuous on \([a,b]\) and differentiable on \((a,b)\). MVT gives some \(c\in(a,b)\) with

\[H(b)-H(a)=H'(c)(b-a)=0.\]

This is the endpoint equality used in the proof. The same reasoning works between any two points of the interval.

Adding a constant changes neither the derivative nor the endpoint difference:

\[ (F+C)'=F',\qquad (F(b)+C)-(F(a)+C)=F(b)-F(a). \]

F and G are both antiderivatives of f. May we always use F(b) − G(a)?

Detective hint: If G = F + 5, then F(b) − G(a) = F(b) − F(a) − 5. The two constants no longer cancel.

Use the same choice of antiderivative for both values. For example, in the lab take \(F=A\) and \(G=A+5\). Then

\[ F(2)-G(-1)=3-5=-2, \qquad F(2)-F(-1)=3-0=3. \]

The mixed expression is not the integral. The rule is “end minus start of one function,” not “choose any two heights.”

Act 3: Do the Endpoints Matter?

Part I gave \(A'=f\) in the interior. Our formula also uses values at \(a\) and \(b\), so we must connect the interior to those endpoint values.

Why A is continuous at the endpoints

A continuous f on a closed interval is bounded. Choose \(M\ge0\) such that \(|f(t)|\le M\). For any \(x,y\in[a,b]\), integral bounds give

\[ |A(y)-A(x)| =\left|\int_x^y f(t)\,dt\right| \le M|y-x|. \]

As y approaches x, the right side tends to zero. Thus A is continuous throughout \([a,b]\), including at the endpoints. This uses only integral bounds, not the formula we are proving.

Suppose F has the correct derivative inside the interval, but we change its value at the final endpoint. Must the formula still work?

Detective hint: Changing just F(b) does not change any interior derivative, but it does change F(b) − F(a).

Take \(f(x)=1\) on \([0,1]\), and define

\[ F(x)=\begin{cases}x,&0\le x<1,\\4,&x=1.\end{cases} \]

Then \(F'(x)=1=f(x)\) for \(0<x<1\). Yet

\[ \int_0^1 1\,dx=1 \qquad\text{but}\qquad F(1)-F(0)=4-0=4. \]
Evidence · a changed endpoint breaks the ruleThe missing condition is continuity of F at 1.

We require continuity of F at the endpoints, not endpoint derivatives. The derivatives used in the proof are at interior points.

Act 4: Two Parts, Two Jobs

Are the two parts of FTC saying exactly the same thing?

They connect the same two ideas, but they start from different information.

Part I gives an antiderivative for every continuous f. Part II lets us find a definite integral from an antiderivative we know.

The endpoint difference is a net change. It is not the area under F. In our lab, the signed area is under \(f(t)=2t\); the matching height difference is on the graph of its antiderivative F.

The integration letter is only a name: \(\int_a^b f(x)\,dx=\int_a^b f(t)\,dt\). In the next acts, t will be the integration input and x will control the moving limits.

Act 5: The Upper Limit Has Its Own Speed

Conditions for the moving-limit formulas

From here on, f is continuous on an open interval containing all fixed and moving limits. The moving limits are differentiable functions of x. We work where these conditions hold.

Keep a fixed, and let

\[ B(x)=\int_a^{g(x)} f(t)\,dt,\qquad A(s)=\int_a^s f(t)\,dt. \]

Now \(B(x)=A(g(x))\): first find the endpoint \(g(x)\), then find the accumulation up to it.

If the endpoint moves twice as fast, should its height alone give the rate of change of B?

Detective hint: B is the composition A(g(x)). The chain rule multiplies the derivative of A by the derivative of g.

Part I gives \(A'(s)=f(s)\). The chain rule then gives

\[ B'(x)=A'(g(x))g'(x) =\boxed{f(g(x))\,g'(x)}. \]

Think “height at the moving edge × signed speed of that edge.” The speed can be positive, negative, or zero.

Act 6: Both Limits Can Move

Choose a fixed reference point r in the interval where f is continuous. Define \(A(s)=\int_r^s f(t)\,dt\). Additivity gives

\[ B(x)=\int_{u(x)}^{v(x)}f(t)\,dt =A(v(x))-A(u(x)). \]

Why does the lower edge get a minus sign?

Detective hint: When f is positive and u < v, moving u right while keeping v fixed removes a strip. Algebraically, B = A(v) − A(u).

Use the chain rule on each term:

\[ \boxed{B'(x)=f(v(x))v'(x)-f(u(x))u'(x).} \]

There is a contribution from each edge. The minus sign comes from subtracting the accumulation up to the lower limit.

See the same two contributions before taking a derivative

For a small nonzero step h, additivity gives the exact identity

\[ B(x+h)-B(x) =\int_{v(x)}^{v(x+h)}f(t)\,dt -\int_{u(x)}^{u(x+h)}f(t)\,dt. \]

Each short integral has its own direction. Divide by h and take the limit to get the two edge rates above.

Equal speeds do not always mean zero change. If \(u=x\) and \(v=x+1\), the interval width stays 1, but

\[B'(x)=f(x+1)-f(x)=(x+2)-(x+1)=1.\]

The right edge adds at a greater height than the left edge removes. Width alone does not determine an integral.

If u(0) = v(0), the integral is zero at x = 0. Must its derivative be zero there too?

Detective hint: Try u(x) = −x and v(x) = x. At zero both limits are zero, but their speeds are −1 and +1.

For \(f(t)=1+t\), \(u(x)=-x\), and \(v(x)=x\), Part II gives

\[ \begin{aligned} B(x)&=\int_{-x}^{x}(1+t)\,dt\\ &=\left(x+\frac{x^2}{2}\right) -\left(-x+\frac{x^2}{2}\right)=2x. \end{aligned} \]

So \(B(0)=0\), but \(B'(0)=2\). The moving-limit rule gives the same answer:

\[ B'(0)=f(0)\cdot1-f(0)\cdot(-1)=1+1=2. \]

If instead \(u(x)=v(x)\) for every x in an interval, then \(B(x)=0\) throughout that interval and \(B'=0\). Equal at one input is not the same as equal throughout an interval.

The identities remain valid if \(u(x)>v(x)\): reversing limits supplies the sign. Also, “moving the lower limit right removes area” is only a simple picture when \(u<v\) and f is positive. The signed formula is the general rule.

Final Report: Same Change, Any Starting Height

Part I gives an accumulation function A with \(A'=f\). Every other antiderivative F differs from A by a constant on the interval. Endpoint continuity carries this relation to a and b, and the constant cancels.

\[ F-A=C,\qquad A(a)=0,\qquad A(b)=\int_a^b f \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{\int_a^b f=F(b)-F(a).} \]
