MATH DETECTIVE: CASE FILE #18

Subject: Lagrange’s Mean Value Theorem

Mission: Use Rolle’s theorem to explain why an instantaneous rate must equal the average rate somewhere, even when the endpoint heights are different.

Detective Briefing: Rolle’s theorem works with equal endpoint heights. Our main idea is to remove the rise or fall of the secant line, apply Rolle to the new function, and then return to the original graph.

Act 1: Remove the Overall Rise or Fall

Let \(f\) be a real-valued function on \([a,b]\), where \(a<b\). This time we do not require \(f(a)=f(b)\).

Continuous on \([a,b]\)Continuity is required on the whole interval, including the endpoints.
Differentiable on \((a,b)\)The derivative exists at every point strictly between the endpoints.
The statement to explain
There is at least one \(c\in(a,b)\) such that
\[ \boxed{f'(c)=\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}.} \]

The fraction is the secant slope: total change in height divided by total change in input. It is the average rate over the interval. The derivative \(f'(c)\) is the instantaneous rate at \(c\).

Geometrically, the tangent at that point is parallel to the secant line.

Write the average slope as

\[ m=\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}. \]

Rolle needs equal endpoint heights. What can we subtract from \(f\) to make both endpoint values zero?

Detective’s Note: Subtracting f(a) makes the left endpoint zero, but the right one is f(b) − f(a). We need something that has the correct value at both ends.
Build the secant line
\[ \ell(x)=f(a)+m(x-a). \]

Check its values at the endpoints:

\[ \begin{aligned} \ell(a)&=f(a)+m(a-a)=f(a),\\ \ell(b)&=f(a)+m(b-a)\\ &=f(a)+\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}(b-a)\\ &=f(a)+f(b)-f(a)=f(b). \end{aligned} \]

Now define \(F(x)=f(x)-\ell(x)\). Then

\[ F(a)=f(a)-\ell(a)=0,\qquad F(b)=f(b)-\ell(b)=0. \]

We have made a new function with equal endpoint values.

Subtracting a constant only shifts a graph up or down. Subtracting this line also removes its overall secant slope.

Act 2: What Does the New Height Mean?

At the same input \(x\), what does \(F(x)=f(x)-\ell(x)\) measure?

Detective’s Note: Compare f(x) and ℓ(x) at the same x. Above the line gives a positive difference; below gives a negative difference.

Above the line: \(f(x)>\ell(x)\), so \(F(x)>0\).

Below the line: \(f(x)<\ell(x)\), so \(F(x)<0\).

On the line: \(f(x)=\ell(x)\), so \(F(x)=0\). This happens at both endpoints.

We subtract a height that depends on \(x\), not the same fixed amount everywhere.

Visual proof: remove the secant lineTwo examples on [0, 2]. In both, the secant is ℓ(x) = 2x and its slope is m = 2.

Try 0%, then 50%, then 100%. Only full subtraction gives the function F used in the proof. Both plots use the same height scale and zero level. Move x to compare the tangent slopes.

Act 3: Apply Rolle, Then Return to f

Our new function is \(F=f-\ell\). Check Rolle’s conditions for this new function:

What Rolle needsWhy F has it
Continuous on \([a,b]\)f is continuous, and a line is continuous. Their difference is continuous.
Differentiable on \((a,b)\)f is differentiable inside, and a line is differentiable. Their difference is differentiable.
Equal endpoint valuesWe checked that \(F(a)=F(b)=0\).

So Rolle gives some \(c\in(a,b)\) with \(F'(c)=0\).

The secant line has constant derivative \(\ell'(x)=m\). Therefore

\[ F'(x)=f'(x)-\ell'(x)=f'(x)-m. \]

If the new graph has slope zero at c, what is the slope of the original graph there?

Detective’s Note: Add m to both sides of 0 = f′(c) − m. The derivative of F is zero; the derivative of f need not be zero.
\[ F'(c)=0 \ \Longrightarrow\ f'(c)-m=0 \ \Longrightarrow\ \boxed{f'(c)=m=\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}.} \]

The tangent of F is horizontal. The tangent of f is parallel to the secant line.

Equal slopes do not mean equal heights. For \(f(x)=x^2\) on \([0,2]\), take \(c=1\):

\[ f(1)=1,\quad \ell(1)=2,\quad F(1)=-1,\qquad f'(1)=2,\quad F'(1)=0. \]

The graph of f does not need to meet the secant at c. The theorem matches their slopes, not their heights.

Act 4: See the Special Cases

In what sense is Rolle’s theorem a special case of MVT?

Detective’s Note: Equal endpoint values make the numerator f(b) − f(a) zero. They do not make the whole graph flat.
\[ f(a)=f(b) \ \Longrightarrow\ m=0 \ \Longrightarrow\ f'(c)=0. \]

Rolle is the zero-average-slope case of MVT. In this case, the secant line is horizontal, and \(\ell(x)=f(a)\).

A similar idea for the Intermediate Value Theorem (IVT)

The general IVT says: if f is continuous on \([a,b]\), every value between \(f(a)\) and \(f(b)\) is reached.

Why is the familiar opposite-sign condition a special case?

Detective’s Note: One endpoint value is positive and the other is negative. Zero is a particular value between them. IVT is about function values, not derivatives.

With opposite endpoint signs, choose the target value 0 in the general IVT. Continuity gives some \(c\in(a,b)\) such that \(f(c)=0\).

The point is inside because neither endpoint value is zero.

Opposite signs are not needed for every zero. For \(f(x)=x^2\) on \([-1,1]\),

\[ f(-1)=f(1)=1,\qquad f(-1)f(1)=1>0,\qquad f(0)=0. \]

Both endpoints are positive, but a zero still exists. The sign condition gives a guarantee; it is not a requirement for every zero.

Do not mix them: \(f(c)=0\) is a statement about height. \(f'(c)=0\) is a statement about slope.

Act 5: What Does MVT Say About c?

Does the theorem name the point c or say there is exactly one?

Detective’s Note: “There exists” does not mean “exactly one”. A line has the required derivative at every interior point.
A non-zero average slope, with two matching points
Take \(f(x)=x+\sin x\) on \([0,2\pi]\). Then
\[ m=\frac{2\pi-0}{2\pi-0}=1,\qquad f'(x)=1+\cos x. \]

Both \(c_1=\pi/2\) and \(c_2=3\pi/2\) lie inside and satisfy \(f'(c_i)=1\).

Two green tangents, both parallel to the orange secant.
Why the midpoint need not work

For \(f(x)=x^3\) on \([0,1]\), the average slope is 1. The derivative is \(3x^2\), so the matching point satisfies

\[ 3c^2=1,\qquad c=\frac1{\sqrt3}\in(0,1). \]

The midpoint \(1/2\) has derivative \(3/4\), not 1. So “somewhere inside” does not mean “at the midpoint”.

Act 6: Does the Derivative Need to Be Continuous?

Do we need to add continuity of \(f'\) to the theorem?

Detective’s Note: We needed continuity of f to get continuity of F, and differentiability of f to use Rolle. We never used continuity of f′.

The proof needs f continuous on \([a,b]\), f differentiable on \((a,b)\), and Rolle’s theorem for F.

It does not assume that \(f'\) is continuous. It also does not require derivatives at the endpoints.

Keep these apart: a derivative existing and that derivative being continuous are different statements.

Optional example: a derivative exists but is not continuous

On \([-1,1]\), define

\[ f(x)=\begin{cases} x^2\sin(1/x),&x\ne0,\\ 0,&x=0. \end{cases} \]

At 0, the difference quotient is \(h\sin(1/h)\). Its absolute value is at most \(|h|\), so it tends to 0. Hence \(f'(0)=0\).

For \(x\ne0\),

\[ f'(x)=2x\sin(1/x)-\cos(1/x). \]

At \(x_n=1/(2\pi n)\), this derivative is −1 for every positive integer n. These points approach 0, but their derivative values do not approach \(f'(0)=0\). So \(f'\) is not continuous at 0.

Still, f is continuous on the closed interval and differentiable inside. MVT applies.

Final Report: Close the Case

Detective’s Note: MVT matches an instantaneous rate to total change divided by the input interval. It is not an average of endpoint heights or endpoint derivatives.
The complete proof in short

Let \(m=(f(b)-f(a))/(b-a)\), let \(\ell(x)=f(a)+m(x-a)\), and define \(F=f-\ell\).

F is continuous on \([a,b]\), differentiable on \((a,b)\), and satisfies \(F(a)=F(b)=0\).

Rolle gives an interior c with \(F'(c)=0\). Since \(F'=f'-m\), we get

\[ \boxed{f'(c)=m=\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}.} \]

One picture to remember: after removing the secant line, a horizontal tangent of F becomes a tangent of f parallel to the original secant.

MVT guarantees at least one interior matching point. It does not say the point is unique, at the midpoint, or on the secant line.
