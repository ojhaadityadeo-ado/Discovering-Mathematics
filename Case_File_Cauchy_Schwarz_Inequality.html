MATH DETECTIVE: CASE FILE

Subject: Cauchy–Schwarz Inequality

Mission: Build an expression that can never be negative, uncover a hidden quadratic, use its discriminant to force the desired inequality, then interpret the result through vectors and the physics of work.

The case: For real numbers \(a_1,\ldots,a_n\) and \(b_1,\ldots,b_n\), prove that \(\left(\sum a_ib_i\right)^2\le\left(\sum a_i^2\right)\left(\sum b_i^2\right)\), and determine exactly when equality occurs.

Follow the clues in order. Try to predict each move before opening the explanation. The physics section is intuition only—the algebraic proof is completed first.

Act 1: Find something that cannot be negative

If the final inequality compares squares and sums of squares, what kind of expression might be safe to start from?

Clue 1 · Which expression is automatically nonnegative for every real x?

Follow this clue: A sum of squares can never be negative.

Define

\[ f(x)=\sum_{i=1}^n(a_ix-b_i)^2. \]

Then, for every real \(x\),

\[f(x)\ge0.\]

Small step 1. Why is the phrase “for every real x” stronger than simply saying “for x=1”?

Open the answer

At one value of x we would know only one numerical inequality. Knowing nonnegativity for every real x tells us the entire quadratic graph never dips below the x-axis, which places a strong restriction on its discriminant.

Small step 2. Could one negative square be cancelled by a positive square?

Open the answer

No. A real square is never negative. Every summand is already at least zero, so their sum is at least zero without any cancellation argument.

Act 2: Expand and expose the quadratic

For one index \(i\),

\[(a_ix-b_i)^2=a_i^2x^2-2a_ib_ix+b_i^2.\]
Clue 2 · After summing over i, what should be grouped together?

Follow this clue: We want to recognize one ordinary quadratic in x.
\[ \begin{aligned} f(x) &=\sum_{i=1}^n\left(a_i^2x^2-2a_ib_ix+b_i^2\right)\\ &=\left(\sum_{i=1}^n a_i^2\right)x^2 -2\left(\sum_{i=1}^n a_ib_i\right)x +\left(\sum_{i=1}^n b_i^2\right). \end{aligned} \]

Introduce temporary names

\[ A=\sum a_i^2,\qquad B=\sum a_ib_i,\qquad C=\sum b_i^2. \]

Then

\[f(x)=Ax^2-2Bx+C.\]

Small step 3. Why introduce A, B and C?

Open the answer

They let us temporarily forget the long sums and reason about the familiar quadratic \(Ax^2-2Bx+C\). Once the quadratic argument is finished, we restore the original sums.

Small step 4. Which of A, B, C is not automatically nonnegative?

Open the answer

B need not be nonnegative because products \(a_ib_i\) can have either sign. But \(A=\sum a_i^2\ge0\) and \(C=\sum b_i^2\ge0\).

Act 3: Read the graph of the quadratic

We know two facts:

\[f(x)=Ax^2-2Bx+C\qquad\text{and}\qquad f(x)\ge0\text{ for every real }x.\]

First suppose \(A>0\). Then the graph opens upward.

Clue 3 · What would a positive discriminant force?

Follow this clue: Picture an upward-opening parabola crossing the x-axis twice.

But our quadratic is never negative. Therefore a positive discriminant is impossible:

\[\boxed{\Delta\le0}.\]

Small step 5. Is \(\Delta=0\) allowed?

Open the answer

Yes. Then the parabola touches the x-axis at exactly one point and remains nonnegative. That case will become the equality case of Cauchy–Schwarz.

Small step 6. Why did we separate the case \(A>0\)?

Open the answer

Because the usual discriminant argument assumes a genuine quadratic. If \(A=0\), the coefficient of \(x^2\) vanishes, so we handle that edge case separately.

Act 4: Turn the discriminant condition into the inequality

For

\[Ax^2-2Bx+C,\]

the discriminant is

\[\Delta=(-2B)^2-4AC.\]
Clue 4 · What should we do with \(\Delta\le0\)?

Follow this clue: The discriminant already contains exactly the combination \(B^2\) and \(AC\) we want.
Step 1 · Insert the discriminant.
\[4B^2-4AC\le0.\]

Small step 7. What is the immediate simplification?

Open the answer

Add \(4AC\) to both sides and divide by the positive number 4. This gives \(B^2\le AC\).

Step 2 · Simplify.
\[B^2\le AC.\]
Step 3 · Restore the original meanings.
\[ \boxed{ \left(\sum_{i=1}^n a_ib_i\right)^2 \le \left(\sum_{i=1}^n a_i^2\right) \left(\sum_{i=1}^n b_i^2\right) }. \]

Small step 8. Why is it helpful that the middle coefficient was written as \(-2B\) rather than giving it some unrelated name?

Open the answer

Because the discriminant becomes \((-2B)^2-4AC=4(B^2-AC)\), so the desired comparison \(B^2\le AC\) appears immediately.

Act 5: Close the missing edge case

What if

\[A=\sum_{i=1}^n a_i^2=0?\]
Clue 5 · What must be true of every \(a_i\)?

Follow this clue: A sum of nonnegative squares can be zero only if every square is zero.

Thus every \(a_i=0\), so

\[\sum a_ib_i=0.\]

The inequality becomes

\[0\le0.\]

Small step 9. Why is this edge case worth stating even though it is easy?

Open the answer

Because the discriminant argument was made under the assumption \(A>0\). A complete proof must also account for \(A=0\).

Act 6: Equality — when does the bound become exact?

Equality in Cauchy–Schwarz means

\[B^2=AC.\]

For \(A>0\), this is the same as

\[\Delta=0.\]

Then the quadratic touches zero for one real value \(x=\lambda\):

\[ 0=f(\lambda)=\sum_{i=1}^n(a_i\lambda-b_i)^2. \]
Clue 6 · What does a zero sum of squares force?

Follow this clue: No summand can be negative.

Hence, for every \(i\),

\[a_i\lambda-b_i=0,\]

so

\[b_i=\lambda a_i.\]

Therefore equality occurs when the two vectors are proportional:

\[\boxed{\mathbf b=\lambda\mathbf a}.\]

Small step 10. Does \(\lambda\) have to be positive?

Open the answer

No. The vectors may point in the same direction or in opposite directions. Any real scalar multiple gives equality after the dot product is squared.

Small step 11. What happens if one vector is the zero vector?

Open the answer

Then both sides of Cauchy–Schwarz are zero, so equality holds. The zero vector is compatible with the usual statement that the vectors are linearly dependent.

Act 7: Physics intuition — why should such a bound exist?

Physics lens: In three dimensions, \(\sum a_ib_i\) is a dot product. If \(\mathbf F\) is force and \(\mathbf s\) is displacement, then work is \(W=\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf s\). Cauchy–Schwarz says the magnitude of this interaction cannot exceed the product of the two vector magnitudes.

\[ |\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b|\le\|\mathbf a\|\,\|\mathbf b\|. \]
Clue 7 · For fixed \(\|\mathbf F\|\) and \(\|\mathbf s\|\), when is \(|W|=|\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf s|\) largest?

Follow this clue: Think about the magnitude of the component along the displacement.

Small step 12. What happens to work if force is perpendicular to displacement?

Open the answer

The component of force along the displacement is zero, so \(\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf s=0\). This fits the dot-product picture perfectly.

Small step 13. Why does equality in Cauchy–Schwarz match the physics picture?

Open the answer

Equality requires proportional vectors. Geometrically that means parallel or anti-parallel directions, exactly the configurations where the magnitude of the projection is the full vector magnitude.

A compact physical reading

If \(\mathbf F\) has fixed magnitude, no orientation can produce more work over a fixed displacement than complete alignment. Cauchy–Schwarz is the general algebraic statement behind that limitation:

\[ |W|=|\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf s|\le\|\mathbf F\|\,\|\mathbf s\|. \]
The proof did not assume the geometric dot-product formula \(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b=\|\mathbf a\|\|\mathbf b\|\cos\theta\). The physics interpretation comes afterward, so there is no circular reasoning.

Act 8: Stress-tests and interpretation

Stress-test A · Identical vectors

If \(\mathbf b=\mathbf a\), then

\[ \left(\sum a_i^2\right)^2 = \left(\sum a_i^2\right)\left(\sum a_i^2\right), \]

so equality holds.

Stress-test B · Perpendicular vectors

Take \(\mathbf a=(1,0)\) and \(\mathbf b=(0,1)\). Then

\[ \sum a_ib_i=0, \qquad \sum a_i^2=1, \qquad \sum b_i^2=1. \]

Thus

\[0\le1.\]

Stress-test C · Opposite directions

Take \(\mathbf b=-3\mathbf a\). Then the dot product is negative, but after squaring the left side, equality still holds. This is why proportionality, not merely “same direction,” is the equality condition.

Small step 14. Why is the left side squared in the standard algebraic form?

Open the answer

Because the raw dot-product-like sum can be negative. Squaring removes the sign and compares its magnitude with the product of the squared lengths.

Final case report

Start with a quantity that is nonnegative for every real \(x\):

\[ f(x)=\sum_{i=1}^n(a_ix-b_i)^2\ge0. \]

Expanding gives

\[ f(x)=Ax^2-2Bx+C, \quad A=\sum a_i^2, \quad B=\sum a_ib_i, \quad C=\sum b_i^2. \]

If \(A>0\), this upward-opening quadratic is never negative, so

\[\Delta=(-2B)^2-4AC\le0.\]

Hence

\[B^2\le AC,\]

which is

\[ \boxed{ \left(\sum_{i=1}^n a_ib_i\right)^2 \le \left(\sum_{i=1}^n a_i^2\right) \left(\sum_{i=1}^n b_i^2\right) }. \]

If \(A=0\), every \(a_i=0\), so the inequality is immediate. Equality occurs exactly when the two vectors are linearly dependent.
