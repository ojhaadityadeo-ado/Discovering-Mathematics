MATH DETECTIVE: CASE FILE #24

Subject: Why a Circle’s Tangent Is Perpendicular to Its Radius

Mission: Use distance, a right triangle, and one carefully chosen point to explain both directions of the tangent–radius theorem.

Detective Briefing: A line may cut a circle twice, touch it once, or miss it. Our first clue is not an intersection coordinate. It is the shortest distance from the center to the line.

Act 1: Check the Setting

The claim

Let a circle have center O and radius \(R>0\). Let P be on the circle.

\[ \boxed{ \ell\text{ is tangent at }P \iff \ell\text{ passes through }P\text{ and }\ell\perp OP.} \]

What do the conditions \(R>0\) and “P is on the circle” tell us?

Detective hint: Every circle point is R units from O. What does \(R>0\) then say about OP?
\[ P\text{ on the circle}\Longrightarrow OP=R, \qquad R>0\Longrightarrow OP\ne0. \]

If P were not on the circle, a perpendicular through P need not touch the circle. If \(R=0\), the “circle” is only one point and there is no nonzero radius direction. These are not small details; the proof will use both facts.

What does tangent mean here?

For a circle, we may call a line tangent when it has exactly one common point with the circle. We will prove why this one-point case is tied to a perpendicular radius.

Act 2: Find One Measurement for Three Situations

Picture one line cutting the circle, one touching it, and one missing it.

What single measurement could tell these three cases apart?

Detective hint: A line is endless, and its slope changes when the picture is rotated. Look for its nearest point to O.

Let H be the perpendicular foot from O to a line \(\ell\), and write

\[ OH=d. \]

Why is H the point of \(\ell\) closest to O?

Detective hint: Triangle OHX is right-angled at H. What nonnegative extra term appears?
\[ OX^2=OH^2+HX^2=d^2+HX^2. \]

Moving away from H in either direction makes \(HX^2\) positive, so the distance from O increases. The whole problem is now controlled by the smallest distance d.

Act 3: Let d Compete with R

Extra clue 1 · We know OX² = d² + HX². To find where the line meets the circle, what condition should we add?

Hint: Which distance defines the circle: OX, HX or d?

Open the reasoning

The line condition is already built into the choice of X. To put X on the circle too, require \(OX=R\). Then \(HX^2=R^2-d^2\), so we can count the possible points without finding their full coordinates.

A point X is on the circle exactly when \(OX=R\). Put this into the right-triangle equation:

\[ R^2=d^2+HX^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad HX^2=R^2-d^2. \]

Can the sign of \(R^2-d^2\) decide the number of intersections?

Act 4: Why Must a Tangent at P Be Perpendicular?

Suppose \(\ell\) is tangent at P. It has exactly one circle point, P.

Our classification says a one-point line has \(d=R\). It also says its only circle point is the perpendicular foot H.

What must be true about H and P?

Detective hint: The tangent has only one circle point, while the \(d=R\) case identifies that point as H.
\[ H=P \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{OP\perp\ell.} \]

Act 5: Check the Converse—and Uniqueness

Extra clue 2 · P already lies on the circle. To prove the perpendicular through P is tangent, what must we check next?

Hint: A tangent has exactly one circle point. Showing that one chosen Q is outside would not rule out all other intersections.

Open the reasoning

Take an arbitrary \(Q\ne P\) on the perpendicular line. Proving \(OQ>R\) for every such Q leaves P as the only circle point. Right triangle OPQ gives the comparison we need.

Now suppose \(\ell\) passes through P and \(\ell\perp OP\). Choose any other point \(Q\in\ell\).

Why can Q not be another point of the circle?

Detective hint: Because \(Q\ne P\), what can you say about \(PQ^2\)?
\[ OQ^2=OP^2+PQ^2=R^2+PQ^2>R^2. \]

Thus every other point of \(\ell\) is outside the circle. Only P lies on both the line and circle, so \(\ell\) is tangent at P.

Have we also shown that the tangent at P is unique?

Detective hint: Recall what \(R>0\) told us about OP.
\[ \boxed{ \ell\text{ is tangent at }P \iff P\in\ell\text{ and }\ell\perp OP.} \]

Act 6: Discover the Tangent Equation

Choose coordinates with \(O=(0,0)\). The circle is

\[ x^2+y^2=R^2. \]

Let \(P=(x_0,y_0)\) be the point of contact. We may write

\[ x_0^2+y_0^2=R^2. \]

Why is this equation true?

Because P lies on the circle, its coordinates must satisfy the circle equation. This is exactly the meaning of “P is on the circle.”

Extra clue 3 · We know the tangent passes through P and is perpendicular to OP. What can turn these two facts into an equation?

Hint: The vector must start at P to describe the line through P. A vector from O would describe a different perpendicular line.

Open the reasoning

Use \(\overrightarrow{PX}=(x-x_0,y-y_0)\) and \(\overrightarrow{OP}=(x_0,y_0)\). For \(X\ne P\), a zero dot product expresses perpendicularity. The point \(X=P\) also satisfies the resulting equation, since PX is then zero.

For a point \(X=(x,y)\) on the tangent, the vector from P to X is \((x-x_0,y-y_0)\). The radius vector is \((x_0,y_0)\).

Before using a dot product, what does it tell us?

\[ \mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v =u_1v_1+u_2v_2 =|\mathbf u|\,|\mathbf v|\cos\theta. \]

The coordinate form lets us calculate. The angle form tells us that two nonzero vectors are perpendicular exactly when their dot product is zero.

Since the tangent direction is perpendicular to the radius,

\[ (x-x_0,y-y_0)\cdot(x_0,y_0)=0. \]

What equation appears when we expand this dot product?

Detective hint: Expand first, then move \(x_0^2+y_0^2\) to the other side.
\[ \begin{aligned} (x-x_0)x_0+(y-y_0)y_0&=0,\\ xx_0+yy_0&=x_0^2+y_0^2,\\ \boxed{xx_0+yy_0=R^2.} \end{aligned} \]

The equation is not a fact to memorize without meaning. Its normal vector is \((x_0,y_0)\), the radius direction.

If the center is \((h,k)\)

The same vector idea gives

\[ \boxed{(x_0-h)(x-h)+(y_0-k)(y-k)=R^2.} \]

Here the radius direction is \((x_0-h,y_0-k)\).

Act 7: Stress-Test the Idea

QuestionWhat happens?
Why must P lie on the circle?Otherwise \(OP\ne R\) in general. The perpendicular through P may cut the circle twice or miss it.
Why require \(R>0\)?When \(R=0\), OP is the zero vector. It gives no direction and does not select one perpendicular line.
Does one intersection always mean tangent for every curve?No. For \(p>0\), the line \(y=0\) meets \(y^2=4px\) only at its vertex, but the tangent there is \(x=0\). Our one-point test works for circles because we proved the distance classification.
Does an ellipse’s normal usually point to its center?No. At \((x_0,y_0)\) on \(\frac{x^2}{a^2}+\frac{y^2}{b^2}=1\), a normal direction is \((\frac{x_0}{a^2},\frac{y_0}{b^2})\), usually different from \((x_0,y_0)\).

What is special about a circle? Every boundary point is the same distance R from one center O. That single fixed distance lets the nearest-point argument control every tangent.
